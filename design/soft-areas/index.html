Soft areas

A weighted range says how often values come up. For a thing, such as a scatter or a stone, that is one roll, and it is easy. A dirt surface and a rock's outline are curves and surfaces, and the roll has to be turned into one. There are several ways to do that, with different looks and different costs. This page lays them out, draws them, and ends with the decision to make.

The question and the choices

A weighting turns a number between 0 and 1 into a position along a range. To draw a whole surface or a whole outline from it, something has to supply a number at every point, and the number at neighbouring points must be related or the result is noise. How a weighting becomes a surface or a curve is the choice. The options, in one line each:

  • (a) Rank a smooth field. Today's top. Sort the field's values and use each value's rank as the number. Exact frequencies, neighbours alike.
  • (b) Quantile-map the field. Use each value's own cumulative probability instead of its rank. Local and tile-size independent, exact only on average.
  • (c) Independent points, joined. Roll each control point on its own and join them, straight or smooth. Explicit feature size. Exact at the points only.
  • (d) Sum of seeded fields. One field per range in a formula, each ranked by its own weighting, then the arithmetic. Each part exact, the whole not.
  • (g) The same sum, ranked together. Add the fields into one roll and rank it through the combined odds. The whole exact, the parts not.
  • (e) Threshold against coverage. Areas only. A smooth field compared with a coverage curve, so the edge is wherever they cross. Can make islands and holes.
  • (f) Edge displacement. Areas only. Push the inner outline outward by a smooth 1D roll along the edge. Always one curve.
  • (h) Own wobble, no grading. Rock outlines. A smooth wobble per stone, each point's value through its own cumulative probability and then the weighting, with no sorting. See the section on rock outlines.

The foundation: today's cloud in six pictures

Band::rolled, band.rs:424

Every option below is a variation of this one procedure, so it comes first. A cloud is a top (a surface) written with a weighting, such as top="$groundHeight + low(0dmm .. 3dmm)". The solver gives the body a band, a low and a high at every node. The body supplies a smooth field of rolls. Band::rolled then decides how high each node stands inside the band, in six steps. The example is a side view of a dirt top: 14 nodes along a strip, a band, and a weighting you can change.

  1. The body's smooth field of rolls. Dirt rolls hills, water rolls waves. How neighbouring nodes relate is the body's business. The only thing rolled needs is one number per node.
  2. Which nodes take part. Nodes the body's take refuses, that have no odds, or whose roll is not a finite number are left out. For dirt that means nodes with no room between low and high, such as the ones at a pin. They keep the body's own value.
  3. Sort by roll. Least roll first. Two nodes with the same roll (9 and 10 here) go in node order, so no thread can change the result.
  4. Rank. Each node gets (position + 0.5) / count. The ranks are evenly spaced from 0 to 1, whatever the rolls were. This is why the frequencies come out exact.
  5. The weighting turns rank into t. Its quantile curve is read at each rank (the node's own weighting: nodes under different pins can carry different ones, are ranked in one pool, and are exact only for the pooled mix). Evenly spaced ranks come out of low bunched near 0, so most nodes get a small t.
  6. Height. lo + (hi - lo) * t. Putting the field (dashed) beside the surface (solid) shows what the cloud does: the hills are in the same places and in the same order, and the heights are redistributed, mostly low with a few rises.

The same idea in 2D

Two properties that come from ranking

Exact frequencies. A smooth field and a lumpy one give the same histogram, because the ranks are the same whatever the field was. The bars are what was drawn and the line is the weighting.

Neighbours stay alike. Ranking is monotone: a node with a greater roll gets a greater height. If the heights were drawn independently the surface would be noise.

Rock outlines: three ways

outlines only, no surfaces

A rock's outline is a closed curve between the rock's core and its cell. Pick points round the stone, give each a distance from the core to the cell edge with the weighting's odds, and join them with straight lines. Three ways to give each point its distance, in plain terms:

  • Graded: grading on a curve (ranking). Make one bumpy curve round the stone, sort its values, and hand out a fixed set of distances in that order. Every stone gets exactly the weighting's mix, and only the order differs. A dealt deck.
  • Each point rolls (option c). Each point throws its own dice through the weighting. Every point's odds are right, but one stone's mix is only close, and neighbouring points are unrelated, so the outline can be a star.
  • Own wobble, no grading (a new option, h). Make a smooth wobble per stone, with 2 or 3 bumps round it. Each point's wobble value goes through its own cumulative probability, then the weighting. No sorting. Neighbours stay alike, each stone is different, and the mix is exact only across many stones.

The outline unrolled: angle round the stone across, distance from the core to the cell edge up. Ten points, low(12). The graded method hands out the same ten values every time, so the wobble only decides where each one goes. The wobble method has no such deck: each point's value is its own.

Eight stones under each method

Each row is the same eight stones: the same seeds, the same wobble for graded and own wobble, and the same random numbers for the dice. Twelve points per stone, a wobble of 2 or 3 bumps. The grey disc is the core, the dashed ring is the cell edge.

What to see. Graded and own-wobble stones look like siblings, because they share one wobble and differ only in how it becomes a distance. Their outlines are smooth and one-lobed. The difference is in the reach: every graded stone spans the same range of distances, from near the core out to near the cell edge under flat (the same deck every time), while own-wobble stones differ in how far they reach, and some are rounder. Rolled stones are the odd ones out: jagged, with sharp spikes beside short points, under flat and high as much as low. Under low all three are mostly near the core with a lobe or two, and under high mostly out to the cell with dents. With 12 points on 2 to 3 bumps there is no aliasing, so the stones differ only in character, not in whether they are stars.

Reading the numbers. The pooled gap of rolls and own wobble is at the level of chance (0.014 to 0.026), and the graded method is worse (0.03 to 0.083), because a fixed deck of few values cannot match a curve. One stone's mix is exact only for graded (0) and is 0.14 to 0.25 off for the others. Graded has no variety at all, and the other two have some. Neighbours are alike under graded and own wobble at 16 points (the step falls to 0.19 to 0.23) and not under rolls (0.28 to 0.34, whatever the points). Stars: rolled stones become star-ish as points are added (14%, 34%, 54% at 6, 10, 16 under low, and 17%, 26%, 51% under flat), and own-wobble stones stop being star-ish by 16 points (1% and 0%). Own wobble is star-ish at 6 points (36% under low, 42% under flat), because 6 points on 2 to 3 bumps leaves only 2 or 3 points on each bump, and the points alias. The spike count is a crude measure, and the pictures above are the better check.

Recommendation for rock outlines, kept apart from the comparison

Own wobble, no grading (h) wins, with one condition. It has the pooled odds of fair dice (at the level of chance), every stone is different, neighbours are alike so there are no stars, and nothing about the stone depends on any other stone or on how many points it has beyond its own wobble. Graded loses on two counts: its pooled gap is worse (0.03 to 0.083) and every stone has the same mix, so the stones are rearrangements of one stone. Each point rolls loses on stars (half of the stones at 16 points) and on neighbours. The condition: about five or more points per bump of the wobble. With fewer the points alias and the stone is a star anyway (36% to 42% of stones at 6 points on 2 to 3 bumps). So the number of points should follow the bumps, which is a rule the rock owns. The costs of (h): a single stone's mix is 0.14 to 0.16 off the weighting, and the mix is exact only across many stones; and it is a third source for u, the wobble's own cumulative probability, beside rank and a roll.

This changes the earlier recommendation for rock outlines, from (c) to (h). It does not change tops. The rest of the decision, below, takes it into account.

Discrete draws: scatter and sites

the easy case

A scatter, a cliff's stones, a size or a count each need one roll per thing, and nothing joins the things up. The roll is ranked through the weighting for each candidate (so the share of candidates at each depth is exact) and the weighting says how deep a thing may stand. Inside the inner area things are certain. In the gap the chance of standing at depth u is one minus the weighting's running total. (a) Rank a smooth field

This is today's top, picture for picture the six above. One smooth field, sorted, ranked, mapped through the weighting. Strength: frequencies are exact over the nodes that take part, whatever the field is, and neighbours stay alike. Weakness: the weighting reshapes the field. With low most nodes take a small t, so most of the surface lies in a low flat floor, and the few nodes with the greatest rolls stand up as spikes. Whether that reads as dirt is a question of look, and the pictures below show it. The result depends on every node of the tile: add area elsewhere and every rank changes.

(b) Quantile-map the field per point

Skip steps 3 and 4. A Gaussian field's value at a point already says where it sits among all the values that field could take: its own cumulative probability, erf of the value. Use that as the number. Strength: it is local, so a point's height depends only on the field there, not on the rest of the tile, and it does not depend on the tile's size. Weakness: frequencies are exact only on average. A tile with few features can have far more high ground than the weighting says, because the field happens to be high over most of the tile. The page measures this below.

(c) Independent rolls at control points, then join

Drop the field. Put control points where the feature size says, roll each on its own, put each through the weighting, and join them with straight lines or a spline. This is what was proposed for a rock's outline: pick points around the stone, join them. Strength: the feature size is explicit (the spacing), nothing depends on the rest of the tile, and the outline is always one curve with no islands. A pin is one more control point. Weakness: frequencies hold at the points and not along the curve. A straight join averages neighbours, which pulls the curve toward the middle of the range, and a spline can overshoot, so it is clamped. Neighbours are alike only as far as the join makes them.

(d) Sum of seeded fields, one per range

The seed idea. A formula with two ranges, such as [0 .. 1] + low(0 .. 1), gets a field for each range, forked from the node's seed and keyed by the range's place. Each is ranked through its own weighting and the arithmetic runs at each node. It is how controls already work, and it is the same mechanism as (a) with one field per range, as octaves. Strength: one rule for controls and tops, and most of the arithmetic a cloud refuses today becomes possible. Weakness: each range's frequencies are exact and the sum's are not. The section on seeds measures how far off.

(g) The same sum, ranked together

A middle option. Add the forked fields into one roll with two patterns, rank that, and map it through the combined odds of the whole formula, as today's independent does with its quantile grid. Strength: the whole is exact, and the whole is what shows. Weakness: the parts are not exact, and it keeps the combining step that option (d) removes.

(e) Threshold against coverage

This is area model B. Compare a smooth field with a coverage curve at each point of the gap: a point is in where the field is at most the coverage at its depth. The edge is wherever they cross. Strength: the curve written is the coverage you see. Weakness: a threshold of a 2D field is a level set, and level sets have islands (bits of area cut off from the main body) and holes (bare patches inside it). That may be wanted for a scatter's density and is wrong for a stone's outline. The same is true of (a) and (b) when they are used for an outline: a point of the gap is in where its depth is at most its t, which is the same threshold with coverage model A, so they also make islands and holes. The explorer counts them.

(f) Edge displacement

Push the inner outline outward by a smooth 1D roll along the edge, ranked or quantile-mapped through the weighting. The edge is a function of arc length. Strength: always one curve, no islands or holes, and a 1D field is cheap. Weakness: it fixes the edge to one value of depth per position along it, so it cannot make a bay or an overhang. Where the gap is uneven the depth is a share of the local gap, so it never crosses the outer outline, and where the inner outline is concave the straight lines of the displacement can cross each other if the depth is measured in millimetres along a normal, which is why a share of the gap along the nearest-point line is used.

Other options considered and left out. Midpoint displacement (split a segment, offset the middle, repeat) is option (c) with the feature size halved at each round, and each round's offset needs a range it can take, so the weighting would have to be applied to the offsets and not to the result. A random walk has no fixed range, so it needs clamping at the ends and the clamp becomes the shape.

Does one option serve tops and areas?

Partly. A surface and an outline differ in dimension and in how many nodes they have, and the weighting means the same for both. The answer in three cases:

  • Many nodes of one smooth thing (a dirt surface, a long edge): rank a smooth field. For a surface the nodes are 2D and this is (a). For a long edge the nodes lie along the arc and this is (f), which is (a) restricted to one dimension. One rule with a dimension.
  • A short closed outline (a rock's, 6 to 16 points): a smooth wobble per stone through its own cumulative probability, (h), with no sorting. Ranking over so few points hands every stone the same set of distances in a different order, and independent rolls leave neighbours unrelated, so the outline can be a star. Measured over 200 stones in the section on rock outlines: (h) is at the level of chance on the pooled odds, every stone differs, and it has no stars by 16 points on 2 to 3 bumps.
  • A threshold of a 2D field ((a), (b) or (e) used for an outline): neither. It makes islands and holes, which a single body's edge must not have. It fits a density, which is what a scatter already is.

So the same option does not serve both, but one idea does. A weighting is a quantile map from a uniform number u to t. The options differ only in where u comes from: its rank among many nodes (a and f, stratified so the frequencies are exact), its own cumulative probability (b), or an independent roll (c). The criterion for choosing is where the thing gets its neighbour relation: from a smooth field, which ranks (a surface, a long edge), or from a join of separate points, which roll independently (a short closed outline). This is a second path for u, and the page says so. The code already has the precedent: a lone stone in a cloud draws its own roll (rock.rs), because a count of one ranks to a constant 0.5.

The band is a constraint, not a draw

A Band holds a lo and a hi Field, one value per lattice node. solve turns the body's own band and its pins into the band the body fills: later pins win where places overlap, a pin eases back to the body's band in the body's way (dirt by slope, water over a fixed distance, cliffs and floors not at all), and several pins pull by odds o = w / (1 - w) that keep every pin exact at its place. The module says it outright: nothing in it draws a random number.

band.rs:1-34 solve, band.rs:663 guide/heights

A value range is a draw

A Weighting is a quantile map: one uniform u in, a position t in [0, 1] out, and Dist::at is lo + (hi - lo) * t. It never takes a second random number, so a flat range is the identity. Each thing draws its own u from a keyed seed, so every draw is independent.

A cloud is both: a band, plus a field that is ranked through a weighting

The 9 Nov design said a weighting on a band is an error, and for a plain band that is still so. The clouds change (guide/clouds) gave it a meaning, so a top= or a pin may carry one today. A band with odds is a cloud, and the program makes its heights in six steps, in Band::rolled:

  1. The body makes its own smooth field of rolls over the nodes (hills for dirt, waves for water). How neighbours relate is the body's business.
  2. A node takes part if it has odds and the body's take accepts it (dirt: where there is room).
  3. The nodes are sorted by roll, least first. Ties go in node order, so no thread can change the result.
  4. A node's rank is (position + 0.5) / count, from 0 to 1.
  5. The weighting turns that rank into t.
  6. The height is lo + (hi - lo) * t.

Two properties come from ranking. The heights come up as often as the weighting says, whatever the field's own distribution, because ranks are evenly spread. And neighbours stay alike, because rank only reorders the roll and never scrambles it. The solver is untouched: the band it solves is the same one, and the cloud only decides where in the band the surface sits.

A roll is a seed

one rule for controls, tops and area bands

One rule, with a qualification. A control's sum is exact, because it draws numbers. A top's sum is approximate under (d), because it adds smooth fields that are correlated by chance, and exact under (g). The explorer never shows a convolution as if it were what a build makes. Shawn's question: a value with several ranges can take one draw for all of them or one draw per range, and each has consequences. The alternative is that one draw for the value is a seed, a random number generator that does the draws for every range inside it. Controls already work this way. Tops do not, and that is where the problem is.

Controls already do it

Draw::draw clones the stream into a base and rolls. The first range reads the stream, as a lone range always did. Each later range draws from base.fork(k), keyed by its place k in the formula. The arithmetic then runs on plain numbers. The stream moves by one draw however many ranges there are, so editing a value never moves what is drawn after it. This is the 11 Nov ruling, two ranges are two rolls everywhere, including top=.

roll, weighting.rs:1213 draw, :1244

Tops do not, because a cloud gets one roll per node

A body hands Band::rolled one smooth roll per node, and that one roll is ranked through one weighting. A formula with two ranges in a top therefore has to be collapsed first into one distribution. independent meets the ranges on a 32 by 32 grid of quantiles, counts the results into 32 bins, and makes a weighting out of the bins. Then one field is ranked through it. That costs three things:

  • Refusals. dt::cloud refuses a cloud times a range, division by a range, sin, cos and abs of a cloud, and a cloud at the end of a range (CLOUD_RULE, solve.rs:489, and the clouds guide).
  • One noise pattern, even for [0 .. 2] + [0 .. 2], where two would give octaves.
  • An approximation of its own: the 32 bins. I measured it at under 0.02 (below).
independent, solve.rs:603 arith, :496 CLOUD_RULE, :489 rolled, band.rs:424 guide/clouds

The proposal: the body hands each node a seed

Do existing hashes move? A top with one bare range can keep today's path, the body's own field ranked, and build as it does now. Anything with arithmetic is different: a number added or multiplied, and min, max, clamp and smooth, are held on the two surfaces today, and per node they would reorder float operations and pile mass at the limit. So the honest claim is "identical up to float, with the goldens re-recorded", unless the new path is made to give today's values on one range and the torture goldens prove it. Keeping today's path beside the new one would be the second path this section says it removes. I grepped library/, test tiles included, and found no top or pin with two ranges. I did not run a build, so no identity is claimed.

The same rule for all three. A roll is a seed, and everything inside it forks from it. For a control, the value's draw is the seed and each range forks. For a top, the node's roll is the seed and each range forks a field. For an area band, the element's roll is the seed: it forks one roll per candidate, and a gap stone's own seed forks the rolls for its outline points. One rule, stated once, and the area band does nothing the other two do not.

Gains

  • One rule for controls, tops and area bands.
  • Most dt::cloud refusals can go: a cloud times a range, a cloud at the end of a range, and division of a cloud by a range. They exist because of the per-node cost of the combined odds. sin, cos and abs of a cloud stay, because the surfaces can fold and the pins and the solver need correct hulls, unless the interval type gains true images (abs is easy, sin and cos need their turning points). Division by a range that spans zero already returns a plain error.
  • Ranges inside ranges become possible: [0 .. low(0 .. 3)], as the end is itself a draw at each node.
  • Two ranges give two noise patterns added, like octaves.

Costs, measured

Ranking makes a cloud's frequencies exact. With separate fields (option d) each range is exact, but the sum is only approximately the combined odds, because two independent smooth fields on a small tile are correlated by chance. Ranking the sum through the combined odds (option g) keeps the whole exact and gives up the parts. I measured it in Node: two ranked smooth fields on the 128 by 128 lattice of a 1 by 1 tile, summed, compared with the true convolution of the two weightings, 60 seeds for each feature size. The number is the largest gap between the sum's cumulative share and the true one (0.07 means that at some height 7% of nodes sit on the wrong side).

Why the two graphs run opposite

they answer two different questions

In the frame of the options: model A is how options (a), (b) and (f) read a weighting, and model B is option (e). The graph under the explorer on the left is the weighting. It says where the edge lands, exactly as a weighting on a top says where the surface lands. The one on the right says how likely a given spot is covered. A spot is covered when the edge lands beyond it, so that curve always runs from 1 at the inner outline to 0 at the outer outline, whatever the weighting. The weighting only bends it.

With high (the case in Shawn's screenshot) edges usually land far out, so most spots are covered and the right curve bows up. With low the edge usually lands near the inner outline, so coverage drops fast. A top has the same right-hand curve implicitly: how likely a height is to lie under the surface is one minus the running total of its weighting. Nobody draws it for tops.

The second reading

Shawn's intuition is a second, coherent model. If I want things 100% likely near the edge I should write the odds there as 100%, and then say how they fall off. In that model, B, the curve you write is the coverage across the gap, scaled so its peak is 100%. The model in the page is A: the curve you write is where the edge lands, as on a top.

Area bands: the form and its checks

the same whichever option is chosen

Which option draws the edge does not change how an area band is written or checked. The sections below are the form, the kind, who rolls, what each element does with a band, the checks and the language check. The stone outline assumes option (c), points joined straight, and the page says where another option would change it.

The proposal

every example here is a proposal; nothing in the repo runs it

An area band, plain or cloud

An area band is a pair of regions, an inner one (min) and an outer one (max) with min inside max, written as a range of places. A plain one, [face .. faceMax], has no odds, as a plain top has none: the element uses its own roll. A weighted one, low(face .. faceMax, 4), uses the same weightings a size or a top uses. Where a top says "the surface may sit anywhere from low to high, and how often each height comes up is the weighting", an area says the same of how far out the edge or the sites go. A zero-width band, [pool .. pool], is the plain region pool (the band fits where a region is wanted), so nothing already written changes.

The top and the area, side by side

A top: Band::rolled, band.rs:424

  1. The body makes its own roll at each node: smooth noise for dirt and water.
  2. Nodes that have odds and that take accepts are ranked by roll, ties in node order.
  3. Rank → (position + 0.5) / count.
  4. The weighting turns the rank into t.
  5. Height = lo + (hi - lo) * t.
  6. fit keeps it between low and high.

An area: the same six steps

  1. The element makes its own roll at each node or candidate: white noise for scatter things and cliff sites, smooth noise for a body's edge and a stone's outline.
  2. Nodes or candidates in the gap that the element accepts are ranked by roll, ties in order.
  3. Rank → (position + 0.5) / count.
  4. The weighting turns the rank into t, how deep it may go.
  5. A node is inside where its depth u is at most t: u = dm / (dm + dM), 0 at the inner outline and 1 at the outer. A site stands there, or does not.
  6. fit keeps the result between the outlines.

Ranking is what makes the frequencies exact. The depths the weighting hands out come up as often as it says, whatever roll the element used, so the chance of standing at depth u follows the weighting's curve (this is about depth, not about how much of the gap's area is kept). With a white-noise roll it is an independent thinning with exact counts. With a smooth roll the kept parts come in patches. How neighbouring rolls relate is the element's own business, as it is for dirt and flagstones.

The weighting words, at, let and the range exist; the one word added is the kind band. A range of places is not a range of an expression kind. Kind::Region in units.rs is the kind of $tile, $region and parent(…), and a declared region name reads as Kind::Any, which unifies with anything. So the decoder resolves both ends to places before the kind check and gives the range the kind Band, refusing a range whose ends are not places (dt::type, worded by units::misfit). A weighting wraps that range as it wraps any plain range, so low(low(a .. b)) and low(a) stay dt::weighting.

A let can hold a band because the area holds odds and never rolls: each element that reads the name draws its own roll, as each element reads a top's cloud. That is the 9 Nov rule unchanged, a name holds the distribution and each use rolls.

Who rolls

The area holds the two outlines and the odds, and never rolls. Each element draws its own roll from its own keyed seed, and the weighting places it, as Band::rolled ranks a top's rolls. No seed, no key and no random number belongs to the area itself. One caveat: the outlines are lines, and a line's wobble is drawn from the tile seed, so the two masks are fixed per seed, as a top's body is. The odds are the only part left unrolled. within "[a .. b]" is the one place an area draws today (drawn_depth, places.rs:152, in blocks and independent of the tile seed), so it goes in the ledger to retire.

How two elements stay consistent. A cliff and the grass beside it each roll their own. They agree on three things and are free on one:

  • Inside the inner area both are certain.
  • In the gap both follow the same curve of depth: the chance of standing at depth u is one minus the weighting's running total, for both. The counts differ, because the weighting acts on depth and the elements have different candidates.
  • Where the grass should follow the cliff exactly, the stack says so: grass listed after the cliff sees where it stands (at="foot(cliff)", near of=cliff), as it sees rocks listed before it.
  • What differs is the individual stone against the individual blade in the gap. That is what two cloud tops (dirt and water over one band) do today.

A shared roll key, so that every element in a band draws one field, was considered and not recommended. Tops do not do it, elements roll different kinds of noise, and it would add a key to the area, which breaks "the area never rolls". seed= already pins an entry's own randomness.

A site's outline is a weighted area, drawn like a top

A site in the gap stands because its roll was high enough. Its cell needs an outline, and Shawn's rule is that the outline is another weighted area with the same weighting, so a stone's outline leans toward the inner area more often than away. The outline is drawn the way a top's surface is drawn, as rolled points joined up.

  1. Points. Choose points around the stone, evenly in angle from its centre with a keyed start.
  2. Rolls. Each point gets a roll from the stone's own keyed seed. The rolls are ranked and the weighting turns each rank into t, as in Band::rolled.
  3. Place. Each point goes on its ray, between the core and the edge of the stone's cell, at the position whose depth u (inherited from the parent band: 0 at the inner outline, 1 at the outer) is nearest t, ties toward the core. u need not be monotone along a ray and may never equal t, so this is a nearest-depth clamp and always has an answer. With low, t is mostly small. A ray toward the inner area runs through falling depth and reaches depth t or the cell edge. A ray away from it only rises, so it stops at the core.
  4. Join. Straight lines between the points. The polygon is the rock's at=, its area.
  5. Fit. The polygon is cut to the stone's cell and the parent's outer outline. The stone's own inner area, core ∪ (cell ∩ inner), is added. That is the same clamp as everywhere, so the outline stays between the stone's two outlines whatever the points do. The result is no longer star-shaped, so it is re-decomposed into convex parts, which the rock already takes.

Settled by this design

  • No smoothing at this step. The rock's stages already take off corners (cuts, chunks, chips, wear). A smooth curve here would be a second smoothing mechanism, and the first one is the rock's own, so there is one.
  • How many points. A spacing in mm at the reference square along the stone's footprint, not a count, so a small stone has few points and a big one has many (about one every 2 mm; a 4 mm stone gets around 6, a 10 mm stone around 16), with at least 3, which is geometry and not a limit. It is a named constant in the code, documented on the rock page, as drawn_depth's 6 to 15 mm blocks are, so it is not a hidden word, and it gets a control only when a tile needs one. The explorer's points slider only shows the effect.
  • Convexity. A non-convex outline is fine. The raw polygon is star-shaped about the stone's centre, so it is a fan of convex triangles, and after fit the result is re-decomposed into convex parts. The rock already takes an area as a list of convex parts, so no special case arises.
  • Neighbours. Gap stones share cells, so independently rolled outlines could leave gaps or overlaps. A point that clamps at a cell edge lies on it, and where both stones reach a shared edge the joint is the cell's own edge, taken by both and never rolled. Where a neighbour does not reach, the edge is free. That is the rock's joint rule (a joint is a face shared with a standing neighbour, held and never moved), and it is the mechanism for the held faces the rock interface already says never move.
  • Few points. Ranking over 3 to 16 points gives every stone the same mix of depths in a different order. The frequency across the gap is carried by the sites, which are many, and not by one stone's points.
  • Between the outlines. By construction: every point is in the cell and the outer outline, the core and the certain part are added, and fit cuts the polygon to the cell and the outer outline (a chord between two points of a non-convex outer outline can leave it, and fit removes that part).

Is it closed, the same form all the way down?

Yes, with one piece added. Intersecting a band with a region gives a band: both ends are cut by the same region, and the weighting and the depth carry over. A cell wholly inside the inner area becomes [C .. C], the plain region C. A cell wholly in the gap with no inner part has an empty inner area, which a band allows. Anything placed in a stone takes the band of its own cell the same way. The added piece is the core, and it is part of the stone's own band, not a constant: the stone's inner area is core ∪ (cell ∩ inner), so its outline is a band of the same form. Without the core a point could roll the whole outline away from its own centre. The core is the cell's inscribed disc (question 4). The rolled points use the same ranking function as Band::rolled, applied to a list of points instead of a field of nodes: one function, not a second path.

The rock's interface. The Stone and Courses round made a cell an area and a top: area (convex polygons with roles surface, joint, seam), a bed, a top and cuts, with the box as the same region in another form. Here the polygon above is the cell's area and its top is a top band that may be a cloud, each rolled by the rock from its own seed. The interface does not change shape. Joint faces a neighbour holds stay joints and only free edges move, so held faces never move. I read the two round-2 briefs and not the rock code.

The containment check and the refusals

  • Containment. The inner area must lie inside the outer one, node by node, checked in check at the seed it is run at, with dt::band-outside. It is a new code because dt::region means "cannot be made". It stands unchanged for clouds, since the cloud adds odds and not bounds. Cells below the document's band need no check (above).
  • Check equals build. Wobble is drawn from the tile seed, so a check at one seed would pass a document that fails at another. The default is therefore seed-free and conservative: the inner lines grown by their wobble must lie inside the outer lines shrunk by theirs. It can refuse a document that would have passed at its own seed, and the error says by how much. Checking at one named seed is the fallback, for a tile where the margin is wanted and tight.
  • Weightings. dt::weighting stands for a weighting wrapping something other than a plain range of places, and for a weighting on a within distance, which builds one mask and so has nothing to roll. dt::range stands for a strength outside its limits. dt::cloud is about a top's arithmetic; an area has none, so a cloud area where a plain region is needed is dt::type.
  • Not clipped. Intersecting the inner area with the outer one to make a document pass would change output silently, which the errors rule forbids.

The decision

separate from the comparison above

Everything above is the comparison. The four decisions are in the order that lets you take 2 and 4 on their own and 1 and 3 together. This is what has to be decided, and my recommendation, kept apart from it. Nothing here is settled.

1. How a weighted range becomes a surface or a curve

PackageTopsLong edgesA rock's outlineWhat it costs
P1, recommended(a), as today(f), the same rule in one dimension(h), own wobble through its own cumulative probability, no gradingThree sources for u under one meaning for a weighting: a rank, a roll, and a field's own cumulative probability. The rule: a smooth field of many nodes ranks, a short closed outline uses its own wobble. Measured: tops exact (0); a rock outline at the level of chance, varied, no stars from 16 points.
P2(a)(f)(f) on the angle, gradedOne path. Measured over 200 stones: pooled gap 0.03 to 0.083, and every stone has the same depths in a different order (variety 0).
P3(b)(b) in 1D(h)Local and tile-size independent, which suits maps. Frequencies are right only on average: measured gap 0.15 at one feature across a tile, 0.063 at two, 0.041 at three, 0.017 at eight.

Recommendation: P1. A top has many nodes of one smooth thing and a promise that heights come up as often as the weighting says, and (a) keeps that promise exactly and is already built, so nothing existing changes. A long edge is the same thing in one dimension, so (f) is not a new idea, and it is one curve with no islands. A rock's outline is a short closed curve, and for it own wobble, (h), wins in the measurements of the section on rock outlines: its pooled odds are at the level of chance, every stone differs, neighbours are alike so there are no stars, and it does not depend on any other stone. Ranking the points (graded) has a worse pooled gap (0.03 to 0.083) and gives every stone the same mix. Independent rolls (c) make half the stones star-ish at 16 points. The condition is about five points per bump of the wobble. It is also what the rock's interface, an area as convex parts, can take. The trigger to revisit is maps: if tiles in a map must agree on a point whatever else is on the tile, (b) is the option that is local and independent of tile size.

Not recommended: (e) and a 2D threshold for any outline, because a single body's edge must not have islands and holes. They fit a density, which a scatter already has.

2. Does a weighting say where the edge lands (A) or the coverage (B)?

Recommendation: A. One meaning for a weighting across sizes, counts, tops and areas. B gives a plain band no softness and breaks continuity with the inner area for most weightings. The cost is that the right-hand curve is not what you type, which the labels, the flat example and the Coverage view answer.

3. Seeds and multi-range tops

Recommendation: adopt "a roll is a seed" as the rule for controls, tops and area bands, and build it for tops only when a tile needs a two-range top. When it does, prefer (g), the sum ranked through the combined odds, over (d), because the whole surface is what shows and (g) keeps the whole exact. Under (g) the parts are not drawn separately, so there is no number for them: each range's own odds do not hold on their own, and the guide would say that. This rests on one unmeasured point, how a reader judges an exact whole against exact parts, so treat it as a lean. Keep sin, cos and abs refused until the interval type has their images.

What the last two reviews changed

  • Seeds (round 3): "a roll is a seed" is one rule with a qualification, a control's sum exact and a top's sum approximate under (d). Byte-identity is not claimed without a build run. The measurement is described as the mean over seeds of each seed's largest gap, with the worst seeds. The middle option (g) was added. sin, cos and abs of a cloud stay refused until the interval type has their images. A and B: "nearly agree" became "are close", and B as an editor input transform that stores A.
  • The comparison (round 4): the P3 figure was wrong (0.1) and is now the measured 0.041 at three features. The criterion for rank versus independent rolls is stated as where a thing gets its neighbour relation, a field or a join, with the lone-stone precedent in the rock code, and it is called a second path for u. The rock claim is now measured over 200 stones. The (e) equivalence is stated for a uniform field. The walkthrough notes the finite-roll condition and the pooled mix of weightings. Node figures are labelled as Node.
  • Rock outlines (no Orin round, since no word or form changed): the section "Rock outlines: three ways" adds option (h), own wobble with no grading, and the rock recommendation moves from (c) to (h) on the measurements.

4. The area band's form

Unchanged by the above: at="low(inner .. outer)", a kind band over regions, containment checked seed-free and conservative as dt::band-outside, a pin refusing a band.

Open questions on the area form

each with a recommendation
  1. What does a plain band mean? Recommend no odds, as a plain top: the element uses its own roll, which for scatter and sites is uniform white noise, so it thins in a straight line. flat(a .. b) is the explicit ranked form. The alternative, a flat cloud for everything, would give [a .. b] a second meaning next to a plain top.
  2. Which roll for which element? Recommend white noise for things and sites (an independent thinning with exact counts) and smooth noise for a body's edge and a stone's outline (patches), each the element's own, as dirt and flagstones choose theirs today.
  3. How do a cliff and the grass beside it agree in the gap? Recommend the stack (foot(cliff)) and equal weightings, with no shared roll key.
  4. What is a stone's core? Recommend the cell's inscribed disc, as part of the stone's own inner area core ∪ (cell ∩ inner), with no control until a tile needs one. The alternatives are the stone's seat or a share of the cell.
  5. How many outline points, and is there a control? Recommend a spacing in mm along the footprint (about 2 mm at the reference square), internal, with no control until a tile needs one. The alternative is the rock's own setting, which would add a word.
  6. Straight lines or smoothing? Recommend straight lines, because wear, chips and cuts already smooth, and one mechanism is better than two. The polygon is star-shaped about the centre, so it is a fan of convex triangles.
  7. Is the depth inherited or recomputed per cell? Recommend inherited from the parent band, so outlines reach toward the inner area. Recomputed from the core would make the fringe uniform all round.
  8. Cliff, wall, columns and bricks. Recommend the free-standing cliff first, then the edge forms once the front is read from the area, then the wall. I did not read how the front, the wall's layouts, columns or bricks are made.
  9. How is containment checked, given wobble? Recommend seed-free and conservative by default (the inner lines grown by their wobble inside the outer lines shrunk by theirs), with a named seed as the fallback. This keeps check equal to build at every seed.
  10. The ranged within "[a .. b]". It breaks "the area never rolls". Recommend a ledger line to retire it, with a migration that dharmatiles fix performs: rewrite it as a band over two rings. Removing it breaks existing tiles, so the line is closed only after the migration exists.
  11. Is band one kind for areas and tops? Recommend yes: a top is a band over Length and an area a band over Region, one word and one set of rules. Doing it for tops is a cleanup and not needed for areas, so it can follow.
  12. Does a weighting say where the edge lands (A), or the coverage directly (B)? Recommend A: one meaning for a weighting, continuity with the inner area for every weighting, and a plain band that thins. The labels, the flat example and the Coverage view carry the explaining. If B is wanted, make it a way to write the same A curve in the editor.
  13. Should tops, and area bands, move to seeded rolls (a field per range)? Recommend adopting the rule "a roll is a seed" as the design for all three, and building it for tops only when a tile wants a two-range top or the dt::cloud refusals get in the way. Single-range tops stay byte-identical, so nothing has to move now. The cost is approximate odds for sums, about 0.08 at the feature counts of real dirt, and the guide must say so.
  14. Order of elements. Recommend cliff, then scatter, then dirt, then water and grass, each schema's "takes" column saying so.

What the reviews changed

Round 1 (the plain band): Orin's seven changes stand where this page does not say otherwise: a range of places is a new form, holes are stated, containment has its own code, the area never rolls, a band is named with let, containment names its seed, and each element's "takes" column decides. Round 2 (the cloud, the outline) follows.

  • The band is a kind (Band over Region), not a second-class value: it is named, passed and intersected, so the refusals become kind mistakes and the word band is registered.
  • A plain band has no odds, as a plain top has none. "A plain band is a flat cloud" gave [a .. b] a second meaning, so it is gone; flat(a .. b) is the ranked form.
  • The weighting acts on depth, not on the gap's area share, so the page says the density follows the depth curve and that two elements share the curve, not the counts. It also notes that the outlines are fixed per seed.
  • Containment is seed-free and conservative by default, so check equals build at every seed. A named seed is the fallback. Retiring the ranged within comes with a fix migration.
  • The outline polygon is defined properly: nearest-depth clamp along each ray, re-decomposed into convex parts after fit, joints shared with a standing neighbour taken whole by both, few points giving the same depth mix per stone, and one ranking function for fields and point lists.
  • The core is part of the stone's own inner area, not a constant, and the point spacing is a named constant.
