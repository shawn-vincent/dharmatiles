Leaf Library Review

A yes-and, adversarial second document on The Leaf Library: five ranked recommendations, 56 numbered options, and a section-by-section reading with the numbers worked out.

24 Nov 2026 · a review, not a plan · estimates are marked e, measured numbers come from the reviewed pages m, ideas nobody has built x

00What this review says

The library idea is right for the cluster level and wrong in three places. First, the merge cost the document wants to remove can be removed more cheaply by placement that never lets two entries cross, with the library still helping inside an entry. Second, the layered build, as written, produces a stair-stepped mesh whose flat treads the current overhang gate would count as steep overhang. Third, the upper levels (patch, square) cannot cover the area where most of the tile is: the rim ring alone is 36% of a 2×2 and 64% of a 1×1.

Two smaller findings recur. Derived data (distance grids, bit grids, per-system copies) is where the megabytes are, and it should be made at load, not shipped. And a library whose key is the engine's key and whose build time is allowed to grow without limit collides with the project's loop budget.

New evidence from the stone experiment. Cutting a convex stone's underside to fit its seat (1 to 3 planes fitted to what is below) did not reduce overlap, because stone overlap is lateral. Carving each stone by its neighbours cut overlap from 58% to 10%, at 2.5 times the triangles and far more time. The lesson for this page: carving is cheap in a field and expensive in a mesh, so the representation should be chosen by which operation the work needs. See O33 and thought experiment T8.

IRecommendations

Ranked by value divided by new machinery. R1 and R2 are cheap to test and change what the rest of the document needs. R3 is a prerequisite for the layered route and should be tested before anyone builds it.

R1 Make the tile crossing-free by placement; keep the library for what is inside an entry

What to do. Place clusters and ribbons so that their top-down shadows (the footprint at the widest height) stay at least 0.6 mm apart, the printer's own island rule. Then entries touch only the ground. The tile's merge becomes concatenation plus one local fusion of each entry's root to the terrain. Allow a small residual set of overlapping pairs and merge only those (O25).

Why. The document measures that the cost is crossings, and that grouping does not change their number. It does not ask whether crossings between entries are needed. The grass lab's own look wants dark seams between ribbons and air under every tip (REQ-GR-1), and gaps of 0.6 mm or more are legal islands. The scatter already places by placed_reach discs, so the machinery exists. Crossings then exist only inside an entry, where the library merged them once offline.

What it unlocks. No layered mesher, no bit grids, no new gate contract (Q18 stays closed), no tile-level 3D union of leaves. The structure also makes the contact table, the choice table and the Wang levels unnecessary for objects and rims, since clearance is a placement rule.

First experiment. Take the 1×1 and 2×2 meadow seeds from Leaf and Scatter. Place ribbons and tufts with shadow-disjoint placement (hull shadow at three heights, 0.6 mm clearance) from today's builder, no library. Build with the batch union.

Pass: 2×2 union at most 1.0 s native and 3 s in wasm (today 12.8 s in wasm), and bare dirt between blades 4% to 15%. Fail: dirt above 20%, or union above 3 s even with disjoint shells, which would show the cost is the terrain contact and not the crossings, and send the work to O19.

Main risk. Soil share. Disjoint curved ribbons pack at about 85% to 90% of their hull, plus 6% for the gaps, so bare soil lands near 15% to 20% e, above the sheet's median. The residual-overlap rule (O25) and wider ribbons are the fix, and the experiment measures how much they are needed.

R2 The library is a pre-warmed Shelf, not a second system

What to do. An entry is a Body with a ThingId, found through the Shelf the engine already has (dt-kit/src/things.rs, Bodies, Shelved). The library xtask only walks a list of ids and builds each through the normal builder, so the shelf holds what a live build would have made. A shelf miss builds live and puts the body on the shelf. Nothing is shipped that the builder could not rebuild.

Why. The standing rules say a second path is the defect and that mirrors are copies. A library built by a separate offline builder, with its own slices and grids, needs a parity test forever, or check and build disagree. A pre-warmed shelf is by construction the same output, and the authoring loop on a leaf keeps working: edit the leaf, the key changes, shelf misses build live, nothing waits on a library regeneration.

What it unlocks. Per-entry keys (an engine change invalidates only entries whose inputs changed, O6), a library that is a cache and can be dropped, and the browser's existing MemStore and IndexedDB route for fetching it.

First experiment. Build 24 tuft bodies through Bodies on a throwaway store, then build the meadow tile reading them from the shelf.

Pass: the second build reports kept equal to the number of tufts in the build record, bytes identical to a build with the shelf off, and the warm tile time within 10% of the 1.5 s measured for 24 prebuilt tufts. Fail: any byte difference, which means the body route is not the live route and needs fixing before anything is added to it.

Main risk. A body is built in its own frame and moved, and the project already found that a flower built at the origin and moved differs by a hair, which broke a repair budget. Tufts have the same hazard at the root fusion. The first experiment measures it directly.

R3 Settle how a layered solid becomes a mesh the gate accepts, before building it

What to do. Run one existing measurement. Take the layered meadow from Leaf and Scatter (569 ms, 75,000 triangles) and put its mesh through the current gate with the face-angle overhang test. Then decide between a sloped skin between layer outlines (O20), a level-set mesh with simplification (O21), or redefining the gate by layers (Q18, which Shawn must decide).

Why. Where an outline steps out by up to 0.286 mm from one layer to the next, a prism mesh has a flat downward tread of that width. The gate measures face normals, so each tread reads as a face at 90° or more and counts against the 0.1% budget. The document says treads come "from layer differences" and says the gate reads layers. Both cannot hold unless Q18 is changed. Leaf and Scatter's gate on the same mesh found overhang 0 mm², so this needs checking to see which mesh produced that number.

What it unlocks. It decides whether the whole layered family (slices, grids, bits) is usable at all, before the library is shaped around it.

First experiment. Count downward-facing triangle area in the 569 ms mesh, split by angle.

Pass: downward area above 65° at most 0.1% of the total downward-facing area with the existing gate unchanged. Fail: anything more. That shows the mesher needs the sloped skin, or the gate contract changes, and both are bigger decisions than the library.

Main risk. Skinning between outlines of different topology (a union of leaves that splits or merges between layers) is the hard part of the sloped skin. Level-set meshing sidesteps it at the cost of triangles (T2).

R4 Budget variety and size before choosing the levels

What to do. Drop the Square level and shrink the Patch level to a ribbon bundle. Write the repetition metric first (O34), set library sizes from it, and use bag draws (O31) so entries repeat as late as possible. Keep leaves, tufts and ribbons, and make ribbons deformable by plan bend rather than shelved in many curvatures (O29).

Why. Two numbers carry this. Most of the tile is within the rim ring, where patches and squares cannot go (T6). And with 150 tufts on a six-tile table, a 100-entry library with 8 distinguishable orientations repeats about 14 pairs e (T3). Library size is a variety decision, and the document treats it as a download decision.

What it unlocks. A library of a few hundred entries in a few megabytes, and a test that fails when a change makes tiles look stamped, which F7 currently has none of (the earlier page marks F7 "held; not shown").

First experiment. From the 2×2 plan used in Leaf and Scatter, draw 150 tufts from a library of N entries with 16 orientations and compute the metric over windows of 50 mm.

Pass: no entry id and orientation bin repeating within a 50 mm window, and at most 3 repeated pairs per 150 tufts, at N no greater than 300. Fail: a bound that needs more than about 600 entries, which makes the library a megabyte problem and points to morphs (O5) and half-tufts (O36) instead.

Main risk. The metric measures identical shapes, and the eye also sees family likeness. A blind test (O40) is the real judge, and it is Shawn's.

R5 Keep the first library small, as data, and derive everything else at load

What to do. Ship meshes (and, where the layered route is chosen later, polygon slices) as lossless data. Make distance grids, bit grids and per-rotation copies at load or on first use. Make no generated Rust until a profile names a hot loop, and never include_bytes! of the bulk data. Key per entry by content (O6, O52).

Why. Derived grids dominate size: a 13 mm tuft at 0.1 mm over 25 layers is 420 KB, so 500 tufts are 210 MB, and 8 pre-rotated copies are 1.7 GB e (T1). They are derivable from the mesh in milliseconds. Shipping them is also a copy in the repository, against the project's standing rule. Compiling bulk data in makes every library edit a crate rebuild, against the warm build budget of under a minute, and every wasm build carries it.

What it unlocks. A cold clone that builds in minutes, a library regenerated per entry, and the freedom to change the grid resolution without regenerating anything.

First experiment. Measure the time to derive a 0.1 mm distance grid stack from one tuft mesh, and the compressed size of 24 meshes with vertices stored as integer multiples of 1/1024 mm (exact in float32).

Pass: grid derivation at most 5 ms per tuft in wasm and compressed meshes at most 40 KB each (so 500 tufts fit in 20 MB). Fail: derivation above 30 ms, which would justify shipping grids, but only for the entries the tile uses, fetched on demand (O50).

Main risk. Unpacking cost on first use in the browser adds up on a cold start. Per-family lazy loading (O50) bounds it.

IIIAppendix: the adversarial review

A reading of the document by section, then the thought experiments with their numbers, then the assumptions I challenge. Original sections keep their numbers (00 to 17). Where I cite O or R numbers they refer to this page.

a. Section by section

00 Summary

Holds. The three load-bearing ideas are the right ones, and the move from "build" to "lookup" is what made the walls fast.

Wrong or unproven. The summary sets 12.8 s for a 2×2 against 0.57 s for a 1×1, which are different tile sizes. The same-size table in section 07 gives 1,675 ms against 569 ms at 1×1 (2.9×) and 12,780 ms against 2,267 ms at 2×2 (5.6×), so the layered route is a clear gain but not a factor of 22. The layered 2×2 is 2.3 s, over the 1 s the walls set as the aim. The statement that leaves "cross each other and print fine" is true for printing and the merge is the problem, which R1 addresses by not making the crossing.

Yes, and. The summary could say what each of the three ideas buys separately: idea (1) is about the 20 ms data steps and the leaf bodies and does not move the 1,675 ms; idea (2) moves it to 569 ms; idea (3) is what lets the build skip a check.

01 Sources and 02 The printer's floor

Holds. I checked the arithmetic: 0.45 / (2 tan 17.5°) is 0.71 mm and the 15° value is 1.7 mm; 0.2 tan 55° is 0.286 mm and the 65° value is 0.429 mm; 350 × 350 × 50 layers is 6.1 million bits, 760 KB.

Wrong. "Ten separate 8 mm leaves are 400 island-layers" counts an 8 mm leaf as 40 layers tall. A sward blade is capped at 3.5 mm (17 layers), so the example is 170, and 40 only for 8 mm tall tufts. The ratio of 10 to 1 stands.

Yes, and. The gap rule (0.2 to 0.6 mm not made) is the only floor that does not survive union (T5), so it is the single check the library cannot give up. It is also the rule that makes R1's 0.6 mm clearance natural.

03 How grass and leaves read

Holds. Rhythm, clumps and negative space argue for ribbons and tufts as entries, and against patches of uniform cover.

Wrong or unproven. The fringe row at every object and the rim steering mean that the tile's most visible grass is exactly where a pre-made large entry cannot go. T6 puts numbers on it.

Yes, and. The fringe is a row of upright tufts, which are separated by design and clear of one another: a case where R1's rule is already the look.

04 The leaf

Holds. The two-space split (outline against section), and lean and arch as one budget, are correct and checked against the sheet.

Wrong or unproven. "Triangles per leaf" quotes 12 to 32 for a flat blade, against 828 today. That is the right order of magnitude for a closed strip, but a leaf that is certified for the overhang and the gate needs a wedge and a rooted foot, so the realistic count is the 140 to 220 row, and a meadow budget should use that.

Yes, and. Entries could store the leaf's 2D outline as a short polyline and rebuild the section on load (O9). A polyline rotates and scales exactly, which a grid does not (O11).

05 Arrangement

Holds. The flow, ribbons and height order are the strongest part of the earlier design.

Wrong or unproven. Height order (each root one layer above the highest leaf it overlaps upstream) is incompatible with a fixed library of ribbons unless the ribbons carry their own height offsets. A ribbon placed next to another at a different height has to be lifted, and lifting changes where it meets the ground: a stretch up (allowed) or a raised root (a new case for the wedge and the root rule). The document does not say which.

Yes, and. Under R1, height order stops mattering between entries, since they no longer overlap. It remains inside an entry, where the library sets it once.

06 The words

Holds. The two refusals (no build-route word, no silent clamp) are the right ones and a keyed shelf honours both.

Wrong or unproven. The page uses words that collide with the project's: level (the project has level= on ground and a levels parts file), version (the document header carries version="1.0.0"), and cell (Field's cell is square_mm over cells_per_square, not a 0.1 mm grid). None is in the tile language, but any that leaks into docs or the build record would be a second job for one word. The registry test would not catch it, because these are not words the language registers.

Yes, and. Name the library's concepts once in a glossary that the review (an orin pass) reads before anything is built.

07 Where the time goes

Holds. The measured table is the most useful in the document.

Wrong or unproven. The batch union grows 7.6× for 5× the leaves (1,675 to 12,780 ms), so it is superlinear, while the layered route grows 4× (569 to 2,267 ms) and is close to linear in area. Both growths mean a 3×3 behaves worse than either figure suggests: roughly 40 s and 5 s e. The note that "everything before the solid is data and takes about 20 ms" also says the library can only help with the solid. A library that saves the leaf bodies saves 4 ms.

Yes, and. The 14.3 and 52 ms for sites and clusters is the second cost after the solid, and it grows with the number of clusters. A shelf-based library with fewer, larger entries (ribbons) reduces it too.

08 What the walls taught

Holds. Moving work offline, a piece knowing its own changes, and choice tables tuned offline are real lessons.

Wrong or unproven. "Library build time and download size were allowed to grow without limit, and that freedom was the whole win" conflicts with the project's loop rule (warm under a minute, everything about ten minutes). Stones were made once and not edited much; vegetation is where the look is iterated, at the leaf and tuft level, which is exactly the library's source. O1 and O48 resolve it. The 0.3 ms window was a lookup of a finished wall, and its limit (straight walls only) applies to a window on a meadow in a stronger form, because a meadow must bend around clearings and objects. The new stone finding (undersides cut to fit, no gain; neighbour carving 58% to 10% at 2.5× triangles) says overlap is lateral, and lateral overlap among leaves is the crossing.

Yes, and. The walls' lesson "a precomputed whole beats a part" assumes the whole can be reused; here the rim ring and fringe make it reusable on a third of a tile at most (T6).

09 The library

Holds. Five levels nesting with a fallback is a sound frame. The table of operations and their guarantees is correct for overhang: turn, mirror and stretch up keep it.

Wrong or unproven.

  • "Turn about the vertical: always" holds for a mesh and not for a distance grid, which has to be resampled or stored per rotation (O11).
  • "Stretch up: always" keeps the angle but changes where layer boundaries fall, so stored slices stop matching; the document half-notes this with "a + b·z" formulas.
  • Uniform scale "for overhang always": scale also changes the clearances, so the gap floor (0.6 mm) and the line floor change with it. The certified range's smallest scale covers the line floor; the gap floor between entries is not mentioned (T5).
  • The certified range "proven by interval arithmetic over the basis" is likely to collapse, because normals are nonlinear in the basis coefficients and interval arithmetic widens at each product (T9). The open question about dense verification should be answered yes (O44).
  • The size estimate "tens of megabytes" is true for meshes and false for the grids in the entry list: 420 KB per tuft at 0.1 mm, so 500 tufts are 210 MB (T1).
  • The Square level cannot cover the rim (T6), and its edge variants (Wang) need matching neighbours across tile boundaries the tile never sees.
  • The earlier page rules that variants are off until a tile asks, as the held answer to F7 (stamped repetition). This document makes variants the default at every level and does not say it overrides that ruling, though its header says the earlier rulings stand unless marked.
  • "Key is the engine's key" means an engine change invalidates the whole library. O6 gives per-entry keys.

Yes, and. A library entry's key is already half-written: ThingId is the canonical form of what a body is made from, and make_thing_key adds format, engine, tier, system and printer. The library is that, pre-filled (R2).

10 Compiled entries

Holds. The document is honest that code usually does not beat data.

Wrong or unproven. include_bytes! for bulk data makes each library edit a recompile of the engine crate and makes the wasm bundle carry it. That is against the warm build budget. The project also says library tiles are read from disk with "nothing compiled in"; extending that to vegetation entries is the consistent route (O16).

Yes, and. If any code is generated, make it the choice trees only (O15), because they are small and need a compiler check.

11 Making the solid

Holds. Layers as the gate's unit is a strong idea, and my T5 shows why: overhang, line floor and lost parts all hold under union at layer level.

Wrong or unproven.

  • A stack of layer prisms has a flat tread wherever an outline steps out. The current gate reads face angles, so treads read as steep overhang. Leaf and Scatter reported overhang 0 on the layered meadow, which suggests its mesh was not the stair mesh or its gate was the layer gate. Either way the contract is Q18, and the document asks it as a question while also treating the layered route as the default. R3 tests it.
  • Meshing "straight from the layers" will produce triangles in proportion to perimeter × layers ÷ segment length. At a 0.1 mm cell that is millions of triangles unless the outline is simplified (T2), and simplification costs overhang margin (O42).
  • "Occupancy bits: jagged at the cell size" is the same problem stated mildly.
  • "No solid: the slicer merges" changes more than the gate. Per-triangle paint by material gives overlapping shells ambiguous owners, and slicers differ in how they treat overlapping meshes.

Yes, and. The mesh does not need to be the layers. It needs to slice to the same polygons at the layer mid-planes (0.1, 0.3, 0.5 mm). A mesh that does that with sloped walls is the smoothest and has the fewest triangles (O20, O21).

12 Further out

Holds. Growth in layers and bits at printer resolution are coherent, and the document says which settled rules they touch.

Wrong or unproven. Wave-function collapse with printable rules cannot make every solution printable if adjacency checks only overhang: the gap floor and clearance to non-library objects are non-local. The shader preview splits seen from printed; the document names it. Distillation breaks determinism across platforms and puts weights into the key, which the project's golden hashes do not allow without a new rule.

Yes, and. A shader preview over the same distance fields is the draft tier of O46 if the tier is the resolution and not a separate renderer.

13 Algorithm families, 14 Tests and failure modes

Holds. The F7 call-out is correct: reuse is the library's own failure mode.

Wrong or unproven. The page says variety "must be measured" and gives two example metrics but no thresholds or test. O34 and O38 fill that in. The acceptance scenes S0 to S4 do not include a view across tiles, which is where library repetition shows (six tiles on a table).

Yes, and. Add an acceptance scene S5: six tiles adjacent, seeds 1 to 6, no repeated (entry, orientation) pair within 50 mm, and a blind pick by Shawn (O40).

15 Tried and failed, 16 Open questions

Holds. The failures list is useful and the library keeps its lessons.

Wrong or unproven. The earlier page lists, as tried and failed, "a five-level vegetation system with 57 new words: accreting, a second language beside the old". This document proposes five levels again. The words differ (it adds none to the tile language), but the structural risk is the same: a second system next to the builder. R2 answers it by making the library the shelf and not a system.

Answers to the new open questions: size, as data; the library ships as data, never as generated code except choice trees; first library is leaves, tufts and ribbons; variety is measured by O34 and judged by O40; pieces come from today's builder (R2) and from harvest (O7); the layered solid is the route only if R3 passes; the preview is the same route at a coarser tier (O46); a certified range may be verified densely with a stated bound (O44).

b. Thought experiments

T1 What does the entry list cost in bytes?

Question. How large is the library if each entry carries its distance grid at 0.1 mm for every layer?

Setup. A tuft 13 mm across, 5 mm tall: 130 × 130 cells, 25 layers, one byte per cell. A square: 350 × 350, 25 layers.

Numbers. One tuft is 422 KB. 100 tufts are 42 MB; 500 are 211 MB; 1,000 are 422 MB. With 8 pre-rotated copies (O11), 500 tufts are 1.7 GB. One square is 3.1 MB as int8 and 383 KB as bits; 1,000 square variants are 3 GB as int8. Compression helps by perhaps 3 to 5× on smooth distance data e. All of it is derivable from the mesh.

Conclusion. "Tens of megabytes" holds only for meshes. Ship meshes and derive grids (R5, O8).

T2 How many triangles does contour meshing give?

Question. What does the cell-based route cost in triangles, compared with the 75,000 measured for the layered 1×1?

Setup. 280 blades a square, each outline 28 mm round, 17 layers, two triangles per outline segment per layer, no merging of hidden walls. Outline segment length 0.1, 0.2 or 0.4 mm.

Numbers. 2.7 million, 1.3 million, 0.67 million triangles for the three segment lengths. The polygon route (20 points per outline) gives 190,000 before overlap hides some. Hidden walls reduce all of these, perhaps by half (the document says nearly half of a union's triangles are hidden walls) e, so 0.3 to 1.3 million for the cell route against about 100,000 for the polygon route.

Conclusion. The cell route needs a simplifier before it can meet the budget of 150,000 a square, and the simplifier uses the margin T4 spends. Polygon layers (O9) or sloped skin (O20) avoid the problem. The measured 75,000 is the number to beat.

T3 How much repetition does a library of N entries give?

Question. On a table of six 1×1 tiles with 25 tufts each, how many pairs of identical-looking tufts are there?

Setup. 150 tufts. Each draws an entry from N and an orientation from R = 8 visibly different orientations (rotation by 45° steps plus mirror give 16, and I assume half are noticed). Expected identical pairs is n² ÷ (2 N R).

Numbers. N = 50: 28 pairs. N = 100: 14. N = 300: 4.7. N = 1,000: 1.4. With bag draws (O31) and arbitrary-angle rotation (O35), R is much larger and the same N gives about a quarter of those pairs e.

Conclusion. Size by variety, not download. About 300 entries with bag draws is likely enough for tufts, and ribbons need fewer if they bend (O29). The estimate counts exact repeats. Family resemblance is not counted and only a blind test settles it (O40).

T4 What does a 0.1 mm grid do to the overhang guarantee?

Question. Does a certified entry stay certified after it is turned into cells?

Setup. The allowed step is 0.286 mm (55°) per 0.2 mm layer. A cell edge is quantised to at most 0.05 mm of error either way.

Numbers. 0.05 ÷ 0.286 is 17% of the step. A step of 0.286 mm can read as 0.236 to 0.336 mm after quantisation, which is 49.8° to 59.2°.

Conclusion. The "never checks" promise is false for grids unless the margin is taken (O42) or the entry is certified at about 0.24 mm. Polygons carry no such error. If a grid route is chosen, run the cheap per-layer gate on every build (O45) and report any failure loudly.

T5 Which printability checks survive a union?

Question. If two entries each pass a check, does their union?

Setup. Work on layer polygons A and B. Overhang: layer k of the union is inside the grown layer k−1 of A or of B, so inside the grown union. Line floor: opening of a union contains the union of the openings, and is contained in the union, so it equals it. Lost parts: a component of the union touches the layer below if any of its parts did. Gap floor: closing is not preserved, because the union can bring A's edge to within 0.2 to 0.6 mm of B's edge.

Conclusion. Three of four checks hold under union and one does not. That is a strong and checkable fact, and it is the one thing "the build never checks what it could not have broken" misses. R1 turns the gap floor into a placement clearance of 0.6 mm, and O25 handles the pairs that touch on purpose.

T6 How much of a tile can a patch or square cover?

Question. If an entry cannot go within 7 mm of the rim (the document's clearing and rim figure), what share is left?

Setup. A 1×1 is 35 mm: interior 21 × 21. A 2×2 is 70 mm: interior 56 × 56. Clearings take about 10% more, and fringes at rocks, walls and trunks take more again.

Numbers. Interior share is 36% for a 1×1 (21² ÷ 35²) and 64% for a 2×2, so the rim ring is 64% and 36% of the tile. After 10% for clearings, about 30% and 58%.

Conclusion. On the commonest tile, a patch or square can cover under a third of the area, and the rest depends on tufts and ribbons placed one by one. The upper levels cut the cost of at most that third, so tufts and ribbons are where the speed has to come from (R1).

T7 Is the grid route itself fast?

Question. Does the per-cell minimum over copies run in the time the document hopes?

Setup. A 2×2 at 0.1 mm is 700 × 700 cells; 25 layers; the sum of entry footprints is about 3.6× the tile area (the lab's overlap).

Numbers. 44 million cell operations. At 16 int8 lanes per SIMD instruction and about 0.5 G instructions/s in wasm e, about 6 ms of arithmetic plus memory traffic, so 50 to 150 ms in practice. Contour and meshing are the larger part.

Conclusion. The minimum is cheap. The cost sits in the contour and the mesh (T2), so the document's risk is mis-placed: it dwells on the merge and not on the surface extraction.

T8 What does the stone finding say about vegetation?

Question. Cutting a stone's underside to its seat did not reduce overlap, because overlap is lateral. Carving by neighbours cut it from 58% to 10% but at 2.5× triangles. What follows for leaves?

Setup. Leaves overlap laterally in plan more than under one another, and overlap by crossing. A mesh pays per crossing; a field pays per cell, and a carve is a mask (AND-NOT of a grown neighbour).

Numbers. The stone result implies about 2.5× the triangles for 5.8× less overlap in a mesh. In a field a carve is one more pass of 44 million cell operations at about 100 ms e, and the triangle count follows from the final surface only.

Conclusion. Pick the representation by what the work needs. If the work is to remove overlap, do it in the field (O26, O33). If it must remain a mesh, avoid the overlap in placement (R1). The seat-cutting result also says not to build a cut-to-seat step for leaf roots beyond the 0.3 mm sink already in the design.

T9 What does it cost to regenerate and certify the library?

Question. Does library regeneration fit the loop budget?

Setup. 1,000 tufts built by the live builder at about 0.2 s each (a tuft of about 11 leaves; Stage 3's target is under 2,000 triangles). Certification at the corners of an 8-control range: 2⁸ = 256 corners, each through the real gate at about 50 ms. Four to six targets (two systems, two or three printers).

Numbers. Building: 1,000 × 0.2 s = 200 s. Certifying: 1,000 × 256 × 0.05 s = 12,800 s, 3.6 hours on one core, about 20 minutes on 12 cores. Times four to six targets: 1.5 to 2 hours on 12 cores e. Interval arithmetic would be quicker, but it is loose: a product of intervals widens at each multiplication, so a certified box is a fraction of the true one.

Conclusion. Regeneration cannot sit in a check or a merge. It has to be incremental (O6), parallel and timed (O53), with the live builder covering misses (O48). Fewer controls per family (O5) shrinks 2ⁿ quickly.
