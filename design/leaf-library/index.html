The Leaf Library

Grass, leaves and small plants for printed terrain, built from a library of precomputed pieces: one place for everything the project knows about them, and everything the stone-wall experiments taught about making them fast.

24 Nov 2026 · a design reference, not a plan · extends Leaf and Scatter (17 Nov) · nothing here has been built in the engine

00Summary

Grass is a scatter of leaves. A leaf is one bent strip with an outline and a section. Clusters of leaves make tufts, ribbons, rosettes, ferns and flowers. Leaf and Scatter settled that model, measured it against the reference sheet, and found the real cost: not making leaves, but merging thousands of overlapping leaves into one printable solid (12.8 s for a 2×2 meadow in the browser, against 0.57 s for a 1×1 built as stacked 2D layers).

On 23 and 24 Nov the stone walls went from 1.6 s to 9 ms (true 3D placement) and 0.3 ms (a window on a precomputed wall) by moving work out of the build and into a library made once. Every stone in the library knows its own changes and what each one guarantees; a wall only chooses, copies and checks a flat base. The same move fits vegetation better than stone, because leaves that cross each other look fine and print fine.

The library this page describes has entries at five levels (leaf, cluster, ribbon, patch, square). An entry is not only a mesh: it carries metadata, certified ranges of its own controls, its slices at every printed layer, small distance and occupancy grids, contact tables and, where it pays, generated code. A tile's build becomes a short program of lookups, copies and per-layer merges, with the 3D boolean gone from the build.

The three load-bearing ideas. (1) Precompute everything that does not depend on the tile, at every level of grouping. (2) Build the solid the way it prints, as 2D layers, so overlap costs nothing. (3) Keep every guarantee a property of the library entry or of the step rule, so the build never checks what it could not have broken.

02The printer's floor

Every rule here comes from library/printers/default.dtile and the project's standing rules. They do not scale with the tile: a leaf that passes on DungeonBlocks (35 mm squares) can fail on OpenLOCK, where the same leaf prints at 0.73 of its size and the line does not shrink.

The gate is a gate: no threshold is loosened and no geometry is culled to pass it. Fixes are made where the geometry is made.

Physical level of detail

The field guide's sharpest idea: the representation depends on the printed size of a feature, not on the camera. Tiny grass becomes relief, larger grass becomes wedges, hero grass becomes swept strips, detailed leaves become fused clusters. The same semantic plant compiles differently for 0.25, 0.4 and 0.6 mm nozzles. Paint matters as well: folds, ridges and dark gaps have to survive primer, wash and drybrush.

Printing as islands

Every separate leaf is a separate island in every layer it crosses: a lift, a travel and a possible string. Ten separate 8 mm leaves are 400 island-layers; the same ten fused at the root are 40. Fusion is a printing decision as well as a look.

03How grass and leaves read

Grass is rhythm and clumps, not a census of blades

  • Clumps matter more than independent blades; neighbours share a direction.
  • A few tall or strongly bent hero blades sell a patch; interiors can be simpler.
  • Negative space between clumps is part of the design; even coverage reads as static.
  • Terrain steers it: blades lean with a slope, gather at water, shelter behind rocks.
  • Miniature sculptors use far fewer, far broader blades than real grass: the target is closer to cartoon grass made solid than to a meadow.

Leaves are outline, fold and pose

In order of what the eye takes first: the outline, the 3D pose, the central fold, the large lobes, then the major veins. Fine veins and texture vanish at this scale.

The fringe

At every object on the sheet the open mat is low and a row of upright tufts stands at its foot: 9 to 13 mm at rocks, walls and trunks, 14 to 22 at water, 5 to 8 at logs, the row 6 to 12 mm out, leaning toward the object and tallest beside it. This reverses today's grass, which presses blades low beside a rock.

04The leaf

One thing, built directly: a strip with a centre line, a width profile and a cross-section, bent. The field guide calls it the sculptable strip; Leaf and Scatter splits its controls along the line the printer draws between two spaces.

Nothing in one space fixes a failure in the other: a bend cannot widen a tip. Lean and arch are one budget: the underside leans by lean at the root and by lean + arch at the tip, free to 55°, wedged past it, an error if any of it goes below the ground. A lobed leaf is a cluster of leaves that fuse (a maple fans from the heel; an oak sits along a midrib).

05Arrangement

Flow

Direction is the largest signal on the sheet. It is one smooth angle field over the tile, written by the author as a number, a range or a formula. The engine then wraps it once round each clearing (grass parts upstream and rejoins downstream) and steers it at the rim over a 9 to 15 mm ramp, choosing the turn side once per stretch. Bending each blade instead made starbursts. Meanders are in phase, about 15 mm long and 10 to 30° wide, over a regional drift, so a 2×2 shows at least three directions.

Ribbons

The grass lab's defining unit: blades along one streamline, 15 to 30 mm long and 2 to 3 mm apart, each rooted half a blade past the last, each tip over the next base, dark seams between ribbons. A ribbon is a cluster placed along the flow's curve, at=along(flow). Blue-noise blades have no ribbons.

Tufts

8 to 14 leaves from one root; spread 40° (tight) to 140° (open fan), or all round for a rosette; about six a square, 10 to 13 mm across, centres 14 to 17 mm apart. The tuft is a fountain: inner leaves stand at about 30°, outer ones lean to 72° and are 38% shorter. That correlation needs the leaf to know its turn from the fan's axis ($turn). The root is one fused body to about 5 mm up.

Arrangement families from the field guide

Rosette (inner leaves short and upright, outer longer and flatter), fan (a shared base, one preferred plane), paired or opposite, whorl, repeated leaflets (fern), reed or cattail (long straps plus a seed head). Arrange tuft centres, not blades; layer the cover as soil relief, short fused grass, sparse tufts and occasional hero weeds.

Clearings and the rim

Clearings are eye-shaped lenses along the local flow, 1.3 to 1.8 a square, 13 mm apart, 7 mm from the rim, about 10% of the tile. Leaves meeting a clearing, the rim or an object are shortened, never sliced; at a clearing the last blades stand separately on the dirt, lapping 0.8 to 3.2 mm onto it.

Height order

Each leaf's root rises one layer over the highest leaf it overlaps upstream, never at random. Random heights read as jitter (F13).

Overlap, by height

A slicer prints overlapping slices once, so overlap adds no plastic. It matters for looks: roots should fuse, middles should read as separate blades. Leaf and Scatter's proposed cap: anything at the root layers (under 0.6 mm), at most 40% of the printed area covered twice or more from 0.6 to 2 mm, at most 25% above. The default meadow measured 51% to 73% in the middle layers.

06The words

Leaf and Scatter brought the first reading's 57 new words down to eleven, plus one $ name. A library changes none of them. Authors write leaf, tuft, scatter; the library is the engine's shelf, filled ahead of time.

WordKindOne meaning

Two things the language must not say. The build route: a word whose value sends a leaf to a different mesher has two jobs, so the engine picks the route from the values and nobody writes it. And the silent clamp: a written value the builder quietly changes is a checker and a runtime that disagree. A clamp is reported, never silent.

A library keeps both promises if an entry is found by the canonical form of what it was made from, exactly as the shelf finds a body today, and if a request outside every entry's certified range builds fresh or is reported, never snapped.

Everything before the solid is data and takes about 20 ms. The cost is crossings: where two leaves' surfaces cut each other, a 3D boolean has to find the cut and make new triangles. Grouping leaves, building them one at a time or by ribbon does not change the number of crossings. In the layered build, the only boolean left is the merge of layer prisms (471 ms of the 569); meshing straight from the layer polygons removes it.

Triangles: about 0.4 million a square today; 75,000 for the layered 1×1; 137,000 for the batch union. Nearly half of a union's triangles are thin side walls nobody sees.

Lessons that carry to vegetation

  1. Move the work out of the build. Every large gain came from computing something once, offline, that the build had been computing every time. Library build time and download size were allowed to grow without limit, and that freedom was the whole win.
  2. A piece knows its own changes. Each library stone carries the operations it allows (turn about z, mirror, uniform scale, stretch up, squash across, a stretch across up to a stored limit) and what each keeps true. The build never asks whether a copy is printable.
  3. Piano samples. Each version stretches a little; past about ±15% the next version takes over. Sizes need versions, not unbounded stretching.
  4. Held by the base. A library stone faces down past 55° only at its flat base, so "held" is decided on that base alone, exactly. The full overhang measure stays as a slow verifier outside the timed path.
  5. Overlap is cheap to place and costly to merge. Placement's stones overlapped 40 to 58% of their volume: free in the browser, expensive in Manifold. Vegetation overlaps more, so the merge is the first problem, not the last.
  6. A precomputed whole beats a precomputed part. The fastest wall (0.3 ms) was a window cut from long walls made offline, already one closed mesh. Its limits were variety and straightness.
  7. The look comes from the pieces. Fast placement read worse than slow placement because it picked worse stones, not because it was fast. Choice tables tuned offline, and pieces taken from approved builds, are where the look lives.

09The library

A library of vegetation pieces, made offline by an xtask, versioned with the engine, keyed like the shelf, and shipped as data beside the program and the browser engine.

Versions like piano samples

A family is a set of versions across size; each version covers a band with a little stretch or morph; the next version takes over at the band's edge. With a morph basis shared across a family (same mesh layout), versions can crossfade instead of jumping.

Sizes, as far as the walls tell

The stone library was 180 stones in 0.21 MB compressed; its height-map form 9 MB compressed; the precomputed walls 38 MB. A vegetation library at five levels, with per-layer grids, is likely in the tens of megabytes before compression e. The browser keeps builds in IndexedDB already; the library would be fetched once and kept the same way.

10Compiled entries

An entry can be something the build runs, not only something it reads. This is partial evaluation: fix most of a generator's inputs offline and keep a small residual program.

Where code beats data

  • Usually it does not. A tight generic kernel over well-laid-out arrays runs as fast as specialised code, and data compresses better for the browser.
  • Decision trees. A choice table tuned offline over millions of trials becomes nested branches with constants inlined.
  • Emitters. One piece's triangles written with every index constant; the transform applied as it writes.
  • Specialised clippers. Clipping against a known set of planes, unrolled.
  • Straight-line morphs. One family's basis as a fixed sequence of multiply-adds.

How it would ship

An xtask writes Rust: const tables, a few generated functions, and include_bytes! for bulk data. The compiler checks it, it is versioned with the engine, and its key is the engine's key, so a cache entry made with an old library is never read by a new one. The browser engine compiles the same source to wasm.

Certified, not sampled

"By construction" holds only if a certified range is proven: by interval arithmetic over the morph basis, by convexity of the constraint in the controls, or by exhaustive checking on a grid fine enough that nothing between grid points can fail (with the bound stated). A range checked at samples is "usually", and must say so.

Why layers: the printer works in layers, the gate judges in layers, and in 2D a merge of overlapping regions is a cheap, robust operation. The terrain stays the stack mesher's; grass layers sit on it. A layer mesher needs the stack mesher's discipline: one closed mesh by construction, each crossing cached per edge and shared, vertices rounded to float32 once.

The layers are also the gate

Each check reads the same layer polygons: the line floor (erode by half a line and grow back), the gap floor (the same on the complement), overhang (each layer inside the one below grown by 0.286 mm, and by 0.429 for the band), lost parts (every component overlaps the layer below), one body by volume. On the measured meadow the overhang was 0 and the lost-part check found 12 floating tip layers, which the tip floor removes.

12Further out

Ideas raised in discussion, each a bigger departure. Several touch a settled rule; those are marked.

Growth in layers

Write every generator as a rule from layer k−1 to layer k. A blade is a region that drifts along its lean and shrinks to its tip; a tuft is regions splitting from one root; a stone widens and narrows. If a layer may reach at most 0.286 mm past the one below, the overhang holds by construction, there is no wedge to add, and overlap is simply one region. The work is area × layers, suited to a GPU. It is what the grass lab's blade in whole lines and layers was reaching for.

Bits at printer resolution

A square at 0.1 × 0.1 × 0.2 mm is about 6 million bits, 750 KB. Placing, merging, the overhang step, islands and overlap caps become word-wide AND, OR and shift, in a few milliseconds, with no floating-point edge cases. The mesh is the last step.

Wave-function collapse with printable rules

Learn tiles and adjacency rules from approved builds; check offline that every allowed pair is printable where it meets. Overhang is local, so every solution the solver finds is printable. A tile is a constraint puzzle over a grammar; printability is a type rule on joins.

One world, many windows

Make one huge meadow offline; a tile is a window on it; rocks, water and paths are local edits. The seed chooses where to cut.

A shader preview

Draw the tile on the GPU from distance fields, each pixel stepping along its ray. With domain repetition a pixel tests only the blades in its own cell, so a million blades cost what a thousand do. The editor shows changes at once; the exact mesh is made only at Export.

Distillation

Train a small model on thousands of slow, approved builds; it produces layered occupancy or height and depth maps in milliseconds with the look built in. A model gives no guarantee, so it is followed by a step that adds support under anything past the limit. Adding is the allowed direction; removing would be culling.

Toolpaths

Precompute each piece as the lines a nozzle lays; a tile is paths composed. The most radical and least portable route; it is where the printing truth lives.

Settled rules these touch. "No solid" changes the gate's contract (Leaf and Scatter's Q18). A support-adding pass must add, never cut. A shader preview splits what is seen from what is printed, which the editor's rule that the stage shows the print would have to allow. Each is Shawn's call.

The field guide's exploration program

  1. One printable strip, with straight, V-fold and lens sections; a printed matrix.
  2. Five or six blade archetypes.
  3. A tuft grammar: 5 to 12 blades, a shared root, deliberate negative space; raw against boolean against distance-field union.
  4. Physical level of detail: one patch as relief, fused mass, wedges and strips, printed at several scales.
  5. A leaf outline system with smooth, serrated and lobed outlines.
  6. Rosettes and fans.
  7. Terrain-aware distribution.
  8. A printability compiler: one profile applied to every primitive.
  9. Style profiles (comic, miniature resin, botanical, rugged).
  10. A full tile benchmark: mesh size, time, slicing, print success, painted readability.

Open-source pieces

OpenAlea ADEL and CNW-Grass (grass morphology), PlantGL and L-Py (plant geometry, L-systems), Helios (plant simulation), GPUOpen's Bézier blades (a compact blade), yomotsu/vegetation and genart-dev/plugin-plants (seeded generators and presets), Runions et al. (venation), lyon (2D outlines and tessellation in Rust), Manifold (booleans, distance-field meshing, cross-sections), Blender Geometry Nodes (Poisson-disk distribution). None of them makes printable miniature vegetation; the new layer is the compilation from a semantic plant into a fused, support-aware printed solid.

A library raises one of them above the rest: F7, stamped repetition. Every level of reuse (versions, clusters, patches, squares, windows) is a chance to see the same thing twice. Variety must be measured, not assumed: for example, the largest matching patch between two tiles, or the most common repeated cluster within one.

The field guide's failure modes

Needle forest · green cone (fusion that kills negative space) · cookie-cutter leaf · biological overkill · unprintable elegance · clone field · noise soup · slicer surprise.

Metrics

Recognition at tabletop distance; silhouette per triangle and per cubic millimetre; support-free print success; durability after handling; paint readability; mesh health; generation time and memory; variation quality across seeds. To these the library adds: library size, library build time, load time, and the share of a tile built from prebuilt pieces.

17Glossary

Tile
the whole printed piece
Square
one 35 mm unit (25.4 on OpenLOCK)
Leaf
one bent strip; a blade is a narrow leaf
Cluster
a thing made of things: a tuft, a rosette, a ribbon, a flower
Ribbon
leaves shingled along one streamline of the flow
Sward
the open cover of combed grass
Fringe
the row of upright tufts at an object's foot
Flow
the angle field that heads every placed thing
Wedge
solid under a leaf leaning past 55°, raked at 45°
Entry
one item in the library, at any level
Version
one entry of a family, covering a band of sizes
Certified range
the box of controls over which an entry is proven printable
Morph basis
a rest shape and a few directions a family varies along
Layered build
the solid made as 0.2 mm layers merged in 2D
Physical level of detail
a switch of representation by printed feature size
dmm
a design millimetre: 1 mm on DungeonBlocks, 0.73 mm on OpenLOCK
