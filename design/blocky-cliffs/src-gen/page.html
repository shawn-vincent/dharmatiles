Blocky Cliff Generator

It makes a small cliff formation from one seed: four to eleven rounded stones, stood and piled the way the reference sheets show them, in about a millisecond, drawing it included. Below, the generator, then the algorithm in nine steps with the picture each step leaves on the seed above and what it costs. It is for judging the coarse character: shapes, sizes, arrangement, proportion. Lengths are in figure heights (fh): a formation is about two high.

Step 1

The numbers

The seed alone decides a formation. One stream of random numbers, in a fixed order, first picks a family (25 draws in 52 are columns, 17 tiers, 10 slabs) and then draws the numbers of the formation from that family's ranges, measured on the references. There are about eighteen, each named for what it means: the envelope (the highest top, the length, how far tops fall toward the far end, where the peak is); how many stones stand on the ground and how unequal they are; base, how high they stand as a share of the envelope; how far neighbouring tops must differ; how many stones are laid on, how often one lies across a joint, whether they go on the lowest top or the highest; how far each stands back from what carries it; a lean or a dip; an opening; how many stones stand in front.

The picker offers the three families, not the seven kinds of the sheets, because the kinds are regions of these numbers and not different algorithms. Columns reach pillars (base near 1, few stones laid on), a plateau wall (low fall, small step, a shared small dip) and a gate (an opening). Tiers reach blocks (base about 0.6) and courses (base about 0.3, deep, set back by more) along one range. Slabs lean the standing stones into fins, or dip every bed into a ramp. A draw between two regions is a formation of both characters; the numbers are never drawn outside the ranges the sheets show.

Step 2

Ground

A row of stones stands along the back edge, cut from the length into unequal widths, none thin. Each is as high as base times the envelope lets it be, nudged so that no two neighbours' tops are level (they differ by at least step); a few, by a cubed draw, rise to the envelope itself and so stand alone. Each stands a little forward of the back wall by its own amount.

Two settings change the row. With a lean, the first stone is the end stone (upright on the cut side, leaning on its far side, since the cut plane is a fact) and the others are parallel slabs leaning toward the cut end. With an opening, the row is two piers and a middle stone set far back and low, so that lintels can lie over the gap.

Each stone is first a plain box with a place (a spec): its length along the cliff, depth, height and where it stands. Rounded shapes come later.

Step 3

Front

Against the wall at the cut end stands a buttress, hard against it, 0.55 to 0.8 as tall as the wall behind it, and a toe in front of it, 0.3 to 0.55 high; sometimes a second block beside the buttress. The number of these, 0 to 3, is one setting. Each is measured from the wall that is actually behind it, so it stands against what is there. They are lower than the envelope and stand in front of it, so the pile does not place them.

Step 4

The pile

One rule makes every stone that is not on the ground: lay it where there is most room under the envelope. It goes on one top, or, with chance bond, across the joint between two neighbouring tops, bearing on both. A stone that is laid on one top stands back from it by setback and is shorter by up to twice that, at the cut-end side or the far side. Across a joint, the lower top is first raised to the higher, so the stone lies on both. It is as high as the room is, or, where the room would hold two, about half of it, so stacks come two and three deep. Stack says where a single stone goes: 0 on the lowest top, which fills a course before starting the next; 1 under the highest envelope, which builds a tower.

Every stone is then checked: it must stand. Its support is the tops directly under its footprint, no more than 0.03 fh below it (a top lower than that is not support; bear() closes only a small gap). It passes if the middle half of its footprint, a quarter of its length and depth either side of its centre, is all over its support, so it stands out past it by a quarter of its length at most; or if it bears under the outer eighth at both ends of its long side, as a lintel or a bridge does, which any stone over a gap must. If not, a short top under it is raised to meet it (by up to 0.25 fh, if nothing is on it); failing that, it is slid the smallest way that passes, then shortened and narrowed; and if nothing passes it is not laid. This one check is what keeps overhangs reasonable. A gate's lintel is laid first, across both piers; if it cannot be, the opening is shut.

The same rule makes the caps of pillars, the stones of a stair, the bonded blocks of a tier set, the beds on a ramp and the lintels of a gate. Laying stops at pile stones, when no top has room, or at eleven stones in all.

Step 5

Shapes

Each spec becomes a stone. It finds which of its top edges are exposed and which are against or under another stone: exposed edges get a broad bevel and touching edges a nearly sharp one, so joints stay creases. Stones are shaped by place: front stones are wedges, a side is battered only above the top of its neighbour, a stone that carries another keeps a flat top, and one that is clear slopes its top. One in three has a shoulder cut or a corner knocked off, and some tall ones many sides. With a dip every top slopes the same way and the beds of laid stones follow it. The stone itself is a rounded box (step 8) carrying a handful of numbers: batter, dip, lean and cuts.

Step 6

Bearing

A stone that is off the ground must rest on something. Its underside is sampled; the part with no stone top within 0.01 fh below it is its overhang. Where there is stone close below that part, the stone is built taller, its top where it was and its bottom lower, until three quarters of the overhang rests 0.015 fh into the stone below. It is done only if that is at most 0.25 fh, so a real overhang, such as a gate's lintel over its opening, stays one. Most stones already bear; this catches the small gaps the pile leaves under a stone that lies across two tops of different height, marked.

Step 7

Fusing

Where two stones touch, the joint is either left as two rounded edges make it or fused. The chance is measured on the references: an upright joint 0.20 in front, 0.07 behind; a bed between courses 0.30 and 0.04; a bed under a resting stone 0.03; times 2 in the far third of the length, 0.7 elsewhere, times a softness drawn once for the formation; none under 0.4 fh long.

A fused joint is made by growing the two stones toward each other until they overlap: an upright joint, each sideways by its edge radius and 0.01; a bed, the upper downward by the two edge radii and 0.01. Their flat faces then run on as one face. Nothing is added or cut, and inside corners stay sharp. marks the stones grown. "Separate" shows the stones as built.

Step 8

Meshes

A stone is the surface of a 9 × 9 × 9 lattice, three steps round each edge, every point pushed out to a true rounded box (0.07 fh on the top and upright edges, less under a resting stone and at a ground stone's foot, broad faces swelling 3.5 percent), cut softly by its shoulder and corner planes, then battered, tilted, sheared and turned. Only the outer edges are rounded: nothing is ever rounded where two surfaces meet in an inside corner.

Step 9

Drawing

The stones of a formation are joined into one geometry with one shared material, and the plate and the figure are made once and kept. The renderer is resized only when the picture's size changes. Lighting is for viewing only; nothing on a stone is red or orange, which mean overhang in this project.
