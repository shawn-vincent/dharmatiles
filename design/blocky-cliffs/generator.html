Blocky Cliff Generator

It makes a small cliff formation from one seed: four to eleven rounded stones, piled the way the reference sheets pile them, in about a millisecond, drawing it included. Below, the generator, then the algorithm in nine steps with the picture each step leaves on the seed above and what it costs. It is for judging the coarse character: shapes, sizes, arrangement, proportion. Lengths are in figure heights (fh): a formation is about two high.

Step 1

The numbers

The seed alone decides a formation. One stream of random numbers, in a fixed order, first picks a kind (22 draws in 52 are pillars, 10 blocks, 10 leaning slabs or dipping beds, 7 courses, 3 gates) and then draws the numbers that kind needs from ranges measured on the references: the highest top, the length along the cliff, how many stones along the back, how far the tops fall toward the far end (the envelope), where the peak is, how many ranks stand in front.

Step 2

The back mass

A row of stones stands along the back edge. There are three families, and the kind in the picker is a preset: a draw of their parameters in the mix the references show. Columns (pillars, plateau walls, gates) are columns of unequal width under the envelope, neighbours' tops stepping apart or sharing one plane (a plateau), with a cleft, a tilt that leans every column toward the cut end, and an opening: a share of the length left open between two piers under a lintel course, so a gate is columns with an opening, and a tilt carries them toward leaning slabs. Tiers (blocks, courses) are tiers each shorter and set back, joints staggered, down to a stair of whole courses. Beds are thick beds lying tight on one another on a dip. Leaning slabs, graded with an end stone, keep a rule of their own.

Each stone is first a plain box with a place (a spec): its length along the cliff, depth, height and where it stands. Rounded shapes come later.

Step 3

The front cluster

Against the wall at the cut end stands a buttress, hard against it, 0.55 to 0.8 as tall as the wall behind it, and a toe in front of it, 0.3 to 0.45 as tall again; sometimes a second block, or a small stone tucked into the corner. Each is measured from the wall that is actually behind it, so it stands against what is there.

Step 4

The pile

Stones are then laid on the tops, and they add height: a block spanning a joint and bearing on both stones under it, or an upper block on the highest top, nearly as wide as what carries it, flush at the front and set back on one side; on a broad block a second beside the first. A tall pillar may be split by a level joint. Whatever the pile has added past the envelope by more than a tenth is pressed down in proportion, and no more than eleven stones are kept.

Step 5

Shapes

Each spec becomes a stone. It finds which of its top edges are exposed and which are against or under another stone: exposed edges get a broad bevel and touching edges a nearly sharp one, so joints stay creases. Stones are shaped by place: front stones are wedges, a side is battered only above the top of its neighbour, a stone that carries another keeps a flat top, one in four has a shoulder cut or a corner knocked off, one in ten tall stones a spire. The stone itself is a rounded box (step 8) carrying a handful of numbers: batter, dip, lean, taper and turn.

Step 6

Bearing

A stone that is off the ground must rest on something. Its underside is sampled; the part with no stone top within 0.01 fh below it is its overhang. Where there is stone close below that part, the stone is built taller, its top where it was and its bottom lower, until three quarters of the overhang rests 0.015 fh into the stone below. It is done only if that is at most 0.25 fh, so a real overhang, such as a gate's lintel over its opening, stays one. Most stones already bear; this is a guard that catches what the pile leaves floating, marked.

Step 7

Fusing

Where two stones touch, the joint is either left as two rounded edges make it or fused. The chance is measured on the references: an upright joint 0.20 in front, 0.07 behind; a bed between courses 0.30 and 0.04; a bed under a resting stone 0.03; times 2 in the far third of the length, 0.7 elsewhere, times a softness drawn once for the formation; none under 0.4 fh long.

A fused joint is made by growing the two stones toward each other until they overlap: an upright joint, each sideways by its edge radius and 0.01; a bed, the upper downward by the two edge radii and 0.01. Their flat faces then run on as one face. Nothing is added or cut, and inside corners stay sharp. marks the stones grown. "Separate" shows the stones as built.

Step 8

Meshes

A stone is the surface of a 9 × 9 × 9 lattice, three steps round each edge, every point pushed out to a true rounded box (0.07 fh on the top and upright edges, less under a resting stone and at a ground stone's foot, broad faces swelling 3.5 percent), cut softly by its shoulder and corner planes, then battered, tilted, sheared and turned. Only the outer edges are rounded: nothing is ever rounded where two surfaces meet in an inside corner.

Step 9

Drawing

The stones of a formation are joined into one geometry with one shared material, and the plate and the figure are made once and kept. The renderer is resized only when the picture's size changes. Lighting is for viewing only; nothing on a stone is red or orange, which mean overhang in this project.
