Chapter 13

A generator

The grammar of chapter 11, running. It is about 250 lines: one function for a stone, one for a formation. It is a sketch to test the rules against the sheets, not production geometry.

Make one

Proposed Choose a class, then change any number. Drag to turn the view; scrolling scrolls the page; pinch to zoom. The post is one figure high.

Does it measure like the sheets?

Measured 200 formations were drawn with every number taken from the proposed ranges and classes in the counted proportion. Each was then measured the way the traces were, from the references' camera: stone sizes, and the relations between touching stones.

The relations are the harder test, and the table gives each one for both, stacking included. Where the generator misses, the miss is left showing:

  • its stones are wider and lower as seen than the sheets', because a front cluster and a pile add squat stones faster than the pillars add tall ones;
  • its lower stone is in front less often than the sheets';
  • fewer of its stones bear on two;
  • it puts a cap on about twice as many formations as were traced with one.

The numbers do not say whether a draw looks right. The pictures show the limits: flat chamfers where the sheets are softly rounded, no kinks on edges, stiff dipping beds, and no niche or shelter.

References: Measured from the traces. Generator: Proposed rules, measured on the true solids as the camera would see them. "Foot nearer" compares the lowest point of each outline in the picture; on the traces a hidden foot ends where the stone in front hides it.

The whole of it

formation(numbers, seed):                         every stone is first a plain box with a place
  1 back mass   by class, along the back edge:
       pillars   columns of unequal width under the envelope; a plateau run on one
                 sloping plane; a cleft; steps of stepMin elsewhere; some stand proud
       blocks    a first tier nearly level, shorter tiers on it, each set back, joints staggered
       courses   tiers of the whole footprint, each lying on the one below, set back on two sides
       slabs     parallel leaning slabs of even thickness over a block at the cut end
       beds      one thick dipping bed, thinner and shorter beds tight on it
       gate      two heavy piers, a set-back wall, a thick cap from each pier
  2 front       a chunky buttress hard against the wall at the cut end; a toe in front of
                or beside it; sometimes a second block along the wall, a stone tucked in
                the corner, a pillar applied over a joint
  3 pile        0 to 3 stones laid on the tops, adding height: a summit cap on the highest
                top, a block spanning a joint (the two tops are first made level), a second
                storey on a cap
  4 shape       each stone finds which of its top edges are exposed, then
       exposed edge: a wide bevel, and on one front edge in three a chamfer of a third to
                     half the depth; edge against a neighbour: nearly sharp
       front stones: a wedge top rising into the wall, a battered front and far side
       tall stones:  narrowing upward; one in ten a spire; some many-sided or with a prow
       piled stones: tight on what carries them, a little thicker at one end
       one block or front stone in six carries a shoulder: a second small box on its top
  5 count       if more than eleven stones came out, drop a piled stone or a column and draw again

stone(box, numbers):
  each corner of the box replaced by the points its two top bevels and its upright chamfer leave
  move each face in at the top (batter), scale, tilt the top, shear, turn
  return the convex hull
