Where joints die

Estimated Four habits account for nearly all of them.

  1. Two pillars, one foot. A groove runs down from a step in the skyline, softens at a third to a half of the height, often at a dimple or a notch, and below that the two pillars are one face to the plate. S1, S2, S4, S6, and more faintly T3, T12, F, L, P.
  2. A crack that stops in a face. A line from the head of a stone that runs a third of the way down and ends: T8, T12, C. Or a short groove with a notch in the middle of a broad face, joined to nothing: T6, T10, T13, T17.
  3. A bed that shows at the free edge only. A level joint is a notch at the outer edge of a pillar or block and fades inward: S5, S6, S10, S12, T15.
  4. Round the corner. E's stair is crisp along the front; on the cut end its two lower courses are one face.

By kind, by class, by place

Measured Upright joints and beds between courses die five times as often as the bed under a resting stone or the contact of a front stone with what stands behind it. Place matters as much as kind: in the far third of a formation one contact in four is not open, against one in fifteen elsewhere, and among front upright joints at the far end it is about one in two. Front joints die three times as often as back ones. The class of formation matters little. The sheets differ: S1–S16 and A–P are softer than T1–T20.

Measured Front and back for A–P were called by eye; for the other two sheets a contact is front when it reaches the plate or lies in the lowest two fifths of the formation's height. Thirds of length and height are of the formation's outline as seen. "Other" is mostly a toe or a front block against the stone behind it.

How far a dying line runs, and how it ends

Measured A joint that dies shows a line on about half its contact (usually 0.3 to 0.75) and none on 0.13 to 0.73 fh, typically 0.37. These are long contacts: 0.87 fh against 0.52 for an open joint. Short joints do not die.

Measured The line is lost toward the foot in half of them and part way across a face in two fifths; the top, the back and the cut end share the rest. Six in ten fade gradually, the line faint for about a quarter of a figure height before it is gone; four in ten stop short, often in a notch. Where the line is gone, the outline still shows two stones in half: a step in the skyline, a waist, or a change of plane. In the other half it reads as a crack in one stone.

Measured Open joints weaken downward too. Of upright joints that reach the plate, are open, and of those are faint in their last stretch. The measured line in the lowest fifth is as strong as in the top fifth.

2 · How much, and where

A fusing contact has no groove on 0.3 to 0.7 of its length, evenly drawn. On an upright joint that stretch is at the foot in six of ten, in the middle or lower part of the face (the groove stopping short of a junction) in three, at the top in one. On a bed the groove stays at the free outer edge and the stretch with none is inward, or round the corner on the cut end.

3 · How the groove ends

Six in ten: the groove's depth falls evenly to nothing over 0.15 to 0.5 fh (typically 0.25). Four in ten: it holds its depth and ends within 0.05 fh in a notch about twice the groove's width. Beyond the end, one draw in two keeps the two stones' tops apart, so the outline still steps; the other sets them on one plane.

4 · What stays open

Every bed under a resting stone, its whole length. Every contact between a front stone and the stone behind it. Clefts. Back joints by the cut end. An open upright joint that reaches the plate still tapers: full depth over its upper two thirds, falling to 0.5 to 0.8 of it at the foot, and to a hairline in three of ten.

5 · No line at all

Proposed By these numbers, applied to the 510 contacts traced here (9.8 to a formation), a formation of nine contacts has fewer than one that fuses, 0.8 on average: about one formation in two has at least one, one in five has two or more, and about a twentieth (5 percent) of all its joint length carries no groove, as in the references. The generator has 5.1 contacts a formation, some hidden behind a front stone. It fuses 0.69 joints per formation: about one formation in two has a fused joint and one in seven two or more.

How the generator draws a fused joint

Proposed A joint that fuses is made when the stones are built: the two stones are grown toward each other until they overlap, and the stones are simply a union. Nothing is added and nothing is cut. On an upright joint each stone grows sideways by its edge radius and a little more (about 0.08 fh), so each stone's flat face runs on across the other's rounded edge and the two faces are one, with no seam. On a bed the upper stone grows downward by the two edge radii and a little more (about 0.12 fh), so its front face runs down over the lower stone's rounded top edge. Where the faces are not on one plane the overlap leaves a sharp inside corner. No inside corner is ever rounded.

Proposed A joint that has not fused is left as the two rounded stones make it: where they touch, the rounded edges leave a seam or a shallow vertical depression, and that is as it should be. A fused joint is fused along its whole length: the places and ends of the rule (foot, middle, top; ramp or notch) are not drawn, because a stone cannot grow along part of a joint. The chance that a joint fuses is the rule's, and about 0.7 joints in a formation do.

Proposed Bearing on a base. A stone that carries another keeps a flat top under it, so what it carries bears on it all over. A stone laid across two tops of different height is placed on the higher; a guard then looks at each raised stone from below: the part of its underside with no stone top within 0.01 fh below it is its overhang, and where there is stone close below that part the stone is built taller, its top where the design put it and its bottom lower, until three quarters of the overhang rests 0.015 fh into the stone below. It is done only when that is at most 0.25 fh, so a real overhang (a gate's lintel over its opening) stays an overhang, and never from the sky to the ground. Every laid stone first passes the support check of chapter 14 (out past its support by a quarter of its length at most, or on support at both ends), and a short top under it is raised to meet it, so the guard has little to do. Over 400 formations, 64 of 857 raised stones are under half supported where they are placed and none floats; after the guard 25 are, the real overhangs (lintels over an opening). The guard makes 9 in 100 raised stones taller, by 0.05 fh at the median (0.07 at the ninth decile, 0.25 at most).

Proposed Printing is not decided here. A rounded stone set on another leaves a lip and a thin slot at every bed that has not fused; the references do not fuse for this reason (their beds under resting stones are open 97 percent of the time), and a printing rule for beds is not yet decided.
