Chapter 12

The grammar

Everything above, folded into one small set: one stone shaped by where it stands, stones standing on the ground, a front cluster, stones laid on them by one rule, and an envelope every stone reads.

Vocabulary and productions

Proposed Read each line as "is made of". Square brackets give how many.

A formation is fully described by about eighteen numbers and a seed: six for the envelope and the footprint, six for the stones that stand and the stones laid on them, three for a tilt or an opening, one for the front and one for the bevel. A stone's own shape is not drawn freely: it follows from which of its edges are exposed and whether it stands in front, on the ground, or on another stone. The four old kinds of back mass are not productions: they are regions of these numbers (below).

Eight rules, each from what was seen to what it does

The classes are regions, not types

Proposed Each class of chapter 1 is this grammar with a few numbers changed. Nothing else differs: the same stone, the same envelope, the same rules, and one rule for laying stones on. The generator draws its numbers from three families, each a range of the same numbers, so a draw between two classes is a formation of both characters.

Two readings fit the sheets. Seven types (pillars, wall, blocks, leaners, ramp, courses, gates): safe, since every result looks like a reference, but T18 (a pillar wall with a stair in front) is a new type, and each type is its own code. One family with a few dials: mixed cases come free; the risk is corners of the dial space no reference occupies, and the grain chart in chapter 1 shows the references do fill the space between the classes thinly. The generator takes the dials, and three families as ranges of them, because the kinds differ in one or two numbers: courses are blocks with a lower base and a deeper set-back; a ramp is tiers on a dip; a gate is columns with an opening; a plateau wall is columns with a small step and a shared dip.

What stays undecided

Reconstructed One camera angle leaves these open. The grammar takes the simpler reading of each and says so.

  • Is it ranks at all? Ranks are a reading of depth. The same pictures fit two ranks and a resting stone where this page reads three ranks. The hierarchy of chapter 10 is the simplest account, and the pictures cannot refute it.
  • The niche and the shelter. J and K are single examples of forms the grammar does not make. Each could be written as a production (a slot with a chockstone; a long roof on a set-back wall), but one example fixes no range.
  • How deep is a stone? Depth shows only at tops. Where tops show it is 0.7 to 1.1 of the length for pillars and blocks (M, H, O). For slabs it is the tread, a third of the length or less.

Estimated One reading has been settled against the simpler one. A stair's treads are stones lying on stones, not tall columns whose tops show: the cut ends of E and I show the bed joints, and upper treads are shorter along the cliff as well as set back.
