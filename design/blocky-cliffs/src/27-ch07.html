Chapter 7

Neighbours

The style lives more in how stones sit against each other than in the stones. 493 pairs of touching stones were found in the traces: 314 side by side, 179 with one resting on the other.

Six ways two stones meet

Estimated Every contact in the four sheets is one of these. The first three make up nearly all of them; the last three appear once or twice in a formation at most.

  1. Abut. Side by side on the ground, faces touching along an upright joint, tops at different levels.
  2. Stand in front. A lower stone against the face of a taller one, hiding its foot.
  3. Rest on. One stone on another's top, a level joint between, the upper one the same size or smaller and set back.
  4. Bridge. A cap on two stones with a recess under it.
  5. Jut. A cap wider than what carries it, overhanging by a little.
  6. Prop. A leaning stone against an upright one, a wedge of space between them at the foot or the head.

The joint itself is always the same: two bevels meeting in a shallow V. Stones touch; they never overlap into each other and never stand apart, except at a cleft.

Neighbours are never level

Measured For the 314 side-by-side pairs, the difference between the two tops: median 0.37 fh, usual range 0.05 to 0.95. Only 9 percent are within 0.05 fh, which is the tracing error; 17 percent within 0.1.

This is the rule that most separates these cliffs from masonry. A wall's courses share a level; here a shared level is the exception, and where one appears (the tops of T12, the treads of E) it belongs to one long stone, not to two.

Proposed Hold every pair of neighbours at least 0.12 fh apart at the top, and draw the rest of the difference from the envelope.

The lower stone stands in front

Measured In 81 percent of side-by-side pairs the lower stone is the one nearer the camera. Height grows with distance from the viewer: a formation is a stair in depth even when its face is a wall.

Estimated The exceptions are of one kind: a tall stone at the far left end standing level with or in front of its neighbour (J's left wall, S6's left block). Nothing tall ever stands at the right front.

Measured Neighbours also differ in size. The smaller of a pair is typically 0.53 of the larger, the same for side-by-side and for resting pairs; about one pair in nine is within 10 percent of equal.

Resting, bridging, jutting

Measured A third of all stones (35 percent) rest on another stone and not on the plate. Twenty were traced as caps, in 17 of the 52 formations.

Estimated A resting stone is set back from the face of the stone under it, or flush; where it juts (13 formations) it does so by 0.05 to 0.2 fh and toward the front left, over the figure. It always has more than half its length on its support. Three formations (K, T15, S9) bridge a recess; in each the bridge is two stones meeting over the gap, not one beam.

Upper stones are no smaller than lower ones as a rule: a cap is often the biggest stone in the formation (B, K, S9). What changes with height is thickness: caps are squat, 0.5 fh, on pillars twice that.

The space between

Estimated Eight formations open a real gap in the mass: an upright cleft between two pillars (H, T13, T19, S6), or a recess under caps (J, K, T15, S9). Elsewhere the stones are packed with no space but the joint.

A cleft is as tall as the stones beside it, 0.05 to 0.15 fh wide, and widens slightly upward. A recess is one stone wide, about a figure high, and has a back wall: you never see through.

The larger negative shape is the same in every formation: the empty front left of the plate, bounded by the cliff's face and floor. The cliff is composed against it.
