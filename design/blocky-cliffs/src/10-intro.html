Blocky Cliff Atlas

A geometric study of four reference sheets of stylised cliffs built from large, smooth, block-like stones: what the stones are, how they sit against each other, what varies and what never does, and the smallest set of rules that would make more of them.

    The answer in brief

    The smallest set that explains the four sheets and makes more of them:

    1. One stone. A box with its four upright edges chamfered and its top bevelled, a little narrower above than below, with one flat top. Slab, block, pillar, leaner, cap and toe are this stone at different proportions and places.
    2. Three levels. A formation is 1 to 4 rows from front to back; a row is 1 to 4 stones side by side; a stone stands from the ground up and is cut level at most twice. Four to eleven stones in all, none under a twentieth of the whole.
    3. Two things every stone reads. An envelope that gives its height (low at the front and the far end, highest at the back corner by the cut end, about 1.75 figure heights), and a grain that gives its lean (none, or one lean of 10 to 30 degrees shared by all).
    4. Four rules between neighbours. Joints are closed. Neighbouring tops are never level (0.12 figure heights apart or more). The lower stone stands in front. Nothing juts except a cap, and a cap by 0.2 at most.
    5. Chance in a fixed order. Class, envelope, lean and bevel are drawn once for the formation; widths are drawn per stone within a third of their row's mean; heights and steps are not drawn but follow from the envelope and the rules; corners move 2 percent.
