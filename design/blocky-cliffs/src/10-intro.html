Blocky Cliff Atlas

A geometric study of four reference sheets of stylised cliffs built from large, smooth, block-like stones: what the stones are, how they sit against each other, what varies and what never does, and the smallest set of rules that would make more of them.

    The answer in brief

    The smallest set that explains the four sheets and makes more of them:

    1. One stone, shaped by its place. A chiselled block: flat faces and a flat top, a shallow chamfer along each exposed edge, at the top lying nearly in the top plane (0.02 to 0.15 figure heights deep into the top on the front and cut-end edges, dropping only 0.01 to 0.04 down the side; none or a hair elsewhere), an angled plane across a corner now and then, a bigger one under any exposed bottom edge (a lintel, a cap's overhang, a bridge: 0.05 to 0.16 deep, rising 0.04 to 0.1), a broad one on an exposed upright edge (0.04 to 0.14), and only a hair on an edge that touches another stone or the ground, so a stone sitting flush keeps a crisp bottom joint. Where a joint has fused, the two stones are grown toward each other at construction so that they overlap and their faces run on as one; nothing is added or cut, and inside corners are sharp (chapter 8). Front stones are wedges; tall ones narrow upward. Slab, block, pillar, leaner, cap and toe are this stone at different proportions and places.
    2. Stones standing, and stones laid on them. A row of stones stands along the back edge, each as high as a share of the envelope. A front cluster stands against it at the cut end in ranks, each 0.55 to 0.8 as tall as the one behind: a buttress and a toe. Then stones are laid by one rule, where there is most room under the envelope, on one top or across the joint between two. Pillars, a plateau wall, tiers of blocks, a stair of courses, leaning slabs, dipping beds and a gate are regions of the same numbers: how high the ground stones stand, how far a laid stone is set back, a lean, a dip, an opening. A third of all stones rest on others. Four to eleven stones as traced, nine in ten over a twentieth of the whole.
    3. Two things every stone reads. An envelope that gives its height: highest near the back corner by the cut end (about 1.75 figure heights), lower toward the front and, by anything from a little to three quarters, toward the far end. And a grain: every stone upright, or one set of parallel leaning slabs (15 to 25 degrees) or dipping beds (20 to 32) standing beside upright stones.
    4. Relations between neighbours. Joints are closed, and nine in ten show a line from end to end. One contact in twelve is fused along part of its length (about one formation in two has one): the groove fades out, usually toward the foot, over about half the contact, and the two stones share one surface beyond it. Upright joints at the front and the far end fuse most; the bed under a resting stone almost never does (chapter 8). Tops step by 0.12 figure heights or more, except on a plateau, where neighbours share a small step and one dip (about one pair in five). The lower stone usually stands in front (83 percent). Laid stones stand a little back from what carries them, and a stone that lies across two is built taller on the lower so that it bears on both.
    5. Chance in a fixed order. Family, envelope, base, lean and bevel are drawn once for the formation; widths are drawn per stone and are unequal; heights follow from the envelope and the rules; stones are laid by one rule.

    This set makes the common forms. It does not make two that each appear once: J's niche and K's shelter. And everything about depth rests on one camera angle.

    About eighteen numbers and a seed. Chapter 12 states the grammar, chapter 14 runs it and measures its output against the traces on sizes and on relations, chapter 15 lists every range with how sure it is.
