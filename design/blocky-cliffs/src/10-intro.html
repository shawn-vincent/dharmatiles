Blocky Cliff Atlas

A geometric study of four reference sheets of stylised cliffs built from large, smooth, block-like stones: what the stones are, how they sit against each other, what varies and what never does, and the smallest set of rules that would make more of them.

    The answer in brief

    The smallest set that explains the four sheets and makes more of them:

    1. One stone, shaped by its place. A rounded box: every edge and corner rounded by about 0.07 figure heights whatever the stone's size, broad faces slightly swollen, one near-flat top. Under a resting stone the rounding is smaller, so every bed joint reads as a groove. Where a joint has fused, the two stones are grown toward each other at construction so that they overlap and their faces run on as one; nothing is added or cut, and inside corners are sharp (chapter 8). Front stones are wedges; tall ones narrow upward. Slab, block, pillar, leaner, cap and toe are this stone at different proportions and places.
    2. A pile in three parts. A back mass along the back edge (pillars, tiers of blocks, a stair of courses, leaning slabs, dipping beds, or a gate). A front cluster against it in ranks, each rank 0.55 to 0.8 as tall as the one behind: a toe, a buttress, sometimes a main pillar. And the top of the wall is stones of its own: an upper block as wide as what carries it, a block across a joint, a pillar split by a level joint. A third of all stones rest on others. Four to eleven stones as traced, nine in ten over a twentieth of the whole.
    3. Two things every stone reads. An envelope that gives its height: highest near the back corner by the cut end (about 1.75 figure heights), lower toward the front and, by anything from a little to three quarters, toward the far end. And a grain: every stone upright, or one set of parallel leaning slabs (15 to 25 degrees) or dipping beds (20 to 32) standing beside upright stones.
    4. Relations between neighbours. Joints are closed, but for a rare cleft, and nine in ten show a line from end to end. One contact in twelve is fused along part of its length (about one formation in two has one): the groove fades out, usually toward the foot, over about half the contact, and the two stones share one surface beyond it. Upright joints at the front and the far end fuse most; the bed under a resting stone almost never does (chapter 8). Tops step by 0.12 figure heights or more, except on a plateau, where a run of two to four neighbours shares one top plane (about one pair in five). The lower stone usually stands in front (83 percent). Piled stones sit flush with the face of what carries them, and half of them bear on two; an upper block may stand forward of a set-back wall by up to 0.38.
    5. Chance in a fixed order. Class, envelope, lean and bevel are drawn once for the formation; widths are drawn per stone and are unequal, the widest typically 2.5 times the narrowest; heights and steps are not drawn but follow from the envelope and the rules; corners move 2 percent.

    This set makes the common forms. It does not make two that each appear once: J's niche and K's shelter. And everything about depth rests on one camera angle.
