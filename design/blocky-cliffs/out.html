Blocky Cliff Atlas

A geometric study of four reference sheets of stylised cliffs built from large, smooth, block-like stones: what the stones are, how they sit against each other, what varies and what never does, and the smallest set of rules that would make more of them.

    The answer in brief

    The smallest set that explains the four sheets and makes more of them:

    1. One stone. A box with its four upright edges chamfered and its top bevelled, a little narrower above than below, with one flat top. Slab, block, pillar, leaner, cap and toe are this stone at different proportions and places.
    2. Three levels. A formation is 1 to 5 rows from front to back; a row is 1 to 4 stones side by side; a stone stands from the ground up, or is one of two to four in a stack. Four to eleven stones as traced, none under a fiftieth of the whole and nine in ten over a twentieth.
    3. Two things every stone reads. An envelope that gives its height: highest near the back corner by the cut end (about 1.75 figure heights), lower toward the front and, by anything from a little to three quarters, toward the far end. And a grain: every stone upright, or one leaning set (10 to 30 degrees, fanning) standing beside upright stones, with an upright one closing the cut end.
    4. Relations between neighbours. Joints are closed, but for a rare cleft. Tops step by 0.12 figure heights or more, except on a plateau, where a run of two to four neighbours shares one top plane (about one pair in five). The lower stone usually stands in front (83 percent). A smaller stone may rest set back across a joint; an upper block may stand forward of a set-back wall by up to 0.38.
    5. Chance in a fixed order. Class, envelope, lean and bevel are drawn once for the formation; widths are drawn per stone and are unequal, the widest typically 2.5 times the narrowest; heights and steps are not drawn but follow from the envelope and the rules; corners move 2 percent.

    This set makes the common forms. It does not make two that each appear once: J's niche and K's shelter. And everything about depth rests on one camera angle.

    About eighteen numbers and a seed. Chapter 11 states the grammar, chapter 13 runs it and measures its output against the traces on sizes and on relations, chapter 14 lists every range with how sure it is.

    Chapter 1

    The four sheets

    Fifty-two formations are the whole of the evidence. Every one was cropped, named, and its major stones outlined by hand: 352 stones in all. Every measured number on this page comes from those outlines.

    Look at any formation

    Pick a sheet, then a cell. Blue lines are the stones as traced; green patches are the top faces that show. Point at a stone to read what was measured from its outline. Redrawn takes the photograph away and leaves the traced stones as flat shapes: the formation as the measurements see it. Lengths are in figure heights as seen (fh): the stone's size in the picture divided by the figure's foot-to-head height in the same cell.

    Measured Outlines are good to about 2 percent of a cell's width (roughly 0.05 fh). Block bottoms run 1 to 2 percent low. Where a stone runs behind the figure or another stone its outline is closed by a guess. What is traced is a mass, not always one stone: where a groove is faint a traced mass may be two (about 25 places, among them T2's right flank, T5's left sliver, K's right pier, T6's fourth pillar, S15's left slab), and T7 and T14, traced as 11 each, show about twice that if every groove is counted. Counts on this page are of traced masses and lean low; widths lean high.

    What the scale really is

    The figure's round base gives a ruler that lies flat on the plate. Its top rim is a circle, so the ellipse it makes in the picture tells how far above the horizon the camera sits (15 to 21 degrees), and with that the plate's two visible edges can be laid flat and measured in base diameters.

    Measured The plate is 2.4 to 2.8 base diameters on a side in the A–P sheets, about 2.3 by 3.0 in the sheet of twenty (a rectangle), and 1.8 to 2.1 in the sheet of sixteen.

    Estimated A 28 to 32 mm figure stands on a 25 mm base. On that reading the plates are 45 to 70 mm across, not one 35 mm square: a 25 mm base would cover seven tenths of a 35 mm square's side, and here it covers four tenths. This much is sure. Which size the plates are is not: the three sheets disagree with each other by a third, so the plate was never drawn to a fixed scale against the figure.

    So the page measures in figure heights. The figure is drawn the same in every cell, it stands upright like the cliff faces, and it is the scale a player reads the cliff against.

    Five kinds of formation

    Estimated Sorted by eye into five classes by what the big joints do. The classes are names for regions of one family, not separate designs: chapter 9 shows what they share, and the scatter below shows they run into each other.

    Where the sources disagree

    The smooth A–P sheet and the textured one show the same sixteen compositions, but the textured sheet breaks faces the smooth one leaves whole. The smooth sheet is taken as the form; the textured one only confirms the masses.

    The older sculpting guides in the same folder describe a different rock. Where they differ from the four sheets, the sheets win:

    Chapter 2

    Silhouettes and mass

    Seen whole, every formation is the same object: a wedge of stone about two figures high whose top climbs to one back corner, with a foot that steps down toward the viewer. The classes of chapter 1 differ in how that wedge is cut, not in the wedge.

    Fifty-two outlines on one frame

    Each formation's outline, scaled to its own figure and set down with its lowest right corner on the same point. Choose a class to see how little the class changes the outline.

    Measured Fifty of the 52 stand 1.82 to 2.29 fh high as seen (median 1.96; the middle four fifths 1.86 to 2.22). The two others are A at 1.15, the only low formation in four sheets, and T5 at 2.42. Width as seen runs 1.3 to 2.1 fh and follows the sheet more than the form: the sheet of twenty has wider plates.

    Measured The outline fills 68 to 81 percent of its bounding box (median 74). A box would fill 100, a triangle 50: the mass is a box with one upper corner bitten off, always the left one as seen.

    The skyline climbs to the right

    The top edge of each outline, read from left to right across its width.

    Measured The highest point sits 0.60 to 0.89 of the way across in four formations out of five (median 0.76). It is right of the middle in 50 of 52; in J and O it is at 0.47, and in A, T5, T15 and S8 between 0.5 and 0.6. In the picture, right means two things at once: toward the back of the plate and toward the end where the cliff is cut off flat. The summit is usually near that corner and sometimes at mid length.

    Reconstructed The camera flatters the far end. It looks from the near corner, so the far end of the cliff is the farthest point in view and reads about 0.2 to 0.35 fh higher than it is. The far end as seen is 0.5 to 0.84 of the peak (median 0.62). A wall whose skyline looks level (H, T10, T12) falls by about a fifth of its height; a typical formation falls by a half or more.

    Measured The skyline is a stair, not a slope: neighbouring stones' tops differ by a median 0.37 fh, and fewer than one pair in ten is level (chapter 7).

    Measured The left end starts between 0.4 and 1.7 fh (usually 1.0 to 1.6), never at the ground: the cliff's far end is a stone with a face of its own, not a taper to nothing.

    The envelope

    Reconstructed One camera angle shows the outline; the solid behind it has to be inferred. The treads of E and I, the flat cut ends, and the way the lower stone usually stands in front of the taller (83 percent of neighbouring pairs, chapter 7) all agree on one solid: a height that rises from front to back and from the far end to the cut end, both at once.

    Proposed Three numbers set it. front: the front row's height as a share of the back row's (0.2 to 0.45 as a rule, up to 0.85 in the one formation in eight with a tall stone at the right front). fall: how much lower the far end is than the peak in true height (0.15 to 0.35 for a wall, 0.35 to 0.75 otherwise). peak: where along the cliff the summit is (at the cut end, or up to mid length). Every stone's top is then held near this surface; neighbours step off it, or a run of them shares a plane.

    The cliff takes the back 40 to 60 percent of the plate and leaves the front for the figure. It is flush with the plate at the back and at the cut end, where it would meet the next tile.

    How it meets the ground and the plate's edge

    Estimated Three things recur at the foot.

    • Stones stand in the ground. No bottom edge shows a bevel or a gap against the plate; faces run straight into it.
    • The foot is wider than the head. Low front stones are the most battered: their sides close by a median 23 degrees toward the top, against about zero for pillars (chapter 6).
    • The end is cut. At the right of E, I, L and F the cliff stops on a flat upright plane at the plate's edge, as if sawn. The far end and the front are never cut: they end in stones.

    Why it reads as one cliff

    The same twelve stones, three ways. Only the first is a cliff. What holds it together is not the stones' shapes, which are the same in all three, but four relations: shared upright direction, tight joints, tops held to one envelope, and the low stones in front.

    Chapter 3

    Anatomy of one stone

    Close up, every major stone in the four sheets is the same thing: a box whose edges have all been planed off. Nothing is carved in and nothing sticks out.

    Reading a stone

    Estimated From the smooth sheet at three times its size. The numbered parts are on every stone.

    1. Top face. One flat plane. Its outline is a rectangle with its corners cut: five to eight sides, never four, never round.
    2. Top bevel. A band all round the top, 8 to 15 percent of the stone's width, flat or faintly rounded. It catches the light: on the sheets it is the brightest strip of every stone.
    3. Main faces. Two show: one toward the figure, one toward the cut end. Flat and upright, or closing a few degrees toward the top.
    4. Upright chamfer. The edge between two main faces is a third, narrow face, 5 to 25 percent of the width. On M's columns it is so wide that the top is nearly a regular octagon.
    5. Joint. Where two stones meet, their bevels make a V. It widens toward the top, because both stones close a little as they rise.
    6. Kink. An edge is not ruled: over its length it steps aside once or twice by 2 to 5 percent, with a soft notch. This is the largest feature left out of the model below.

    The edges between all of these are soft, a rounding of 2 to 3 percent of the width. There is no sharp arris anywhere, and no concave face.

    Built in seven moves

    Proposed A box, and six things done to it. Each move is one or two numbers. The order matters only for the last.

    True shape

    Proposed The same stone from the front, from the cut end and from above, with nothing foreshortened. The stone is described by its box (length, depth, height) and by shares of its shortest side for everything planed off it, so one description fits a toe and a pillar.

    The top view is the one the references never give and the one that most fixes the look: an eight-sided top, four long edges and four short ones, with the short ones unequal.

    Chapter 5

    Proportions

    Sizes of the 352 traced stones and the 52 formations, in figure heights as seen. The stones are big against the figure: the typical one is over half a figure wide and three quarters of a figure high as seen.

    What a traced size means

    The camera looks from the plate's near corner, 15 to 21 degrees above level. A stone standing square to the plate shows two of its sides at once, each turned about 45 degrees.

    Reconstructed So a traced width is about 0.71 × (length + depth) of the stone: 1.41 times its mean side. A traced face height is 0.93 to 0.97 of the true height. A stone's height ÷ width as seen, times 1.5, is its true height over its mean side. Stones turned on the plate (A's blocks sit about 20 degrees off square) move this by up to 15 percent either way.

    Charts below give the numbers as seen. The last table gives them turned into true sizes with these factors.

    Width and face height

    Measured Width is the steadier of the two: the middle half of stones are 0.46 to 0.73 fh wide, whatever their kind. Face height is what tells the kinds apart: it runs from 0.13 (a thin slab) to 1.86 (a pillar the height of the cliff).

    Measured The two are nearly unrelated across all stones (r = 0.11). A stone's width says nothing about its height. Kinds are therefore not sizes: they are bands of height ÷ width, drawn as the grey rays.

    The kinds overlap. Block runs into pillar at about 1.5 and into slab at about 0.7: one family, not six.

    How the mass is shared out

    Measured No stone is small. The typical stone is 13 percent of its formation's outline; nine in ten are over 5 percent. The largest stone takes 21 to 40 percent (median 26) and the second largest is typically 0.78 of it.

    Ranked by size, the shares fall in a gentle straight line, not a steep curve: there is no boss stone with a crowd of small ones, and no rubble. This is the "large, chunky" of the style put as a number: four to eleven traced masses, nearly all between a twentieth and two fifths of the whole.

    Top faces

    Measured A top face shows on four stones in five. It is a fifth to a tenth of what is seen of a stone, and two fifths of a slab. Its long edge is close to level across the picture: within 3 degrees below to 12 above for nine in ten, with a lean toward rising to the right, the way the skyline climbs.

    Estimated Tops are flat planes, never domes or points. Pillars in F, L and D carry a top that slopes 20 to 35 degrees toward the front: a second, steeper kind of top, on a minority of stones (seen in F, L, D, T3 and T9).

    The numbers

    Measured As seen, in fh, from the traces. Usual range is the 10th to 90th percentile. Reconstructed True sizes: width ÷ 1.41 for the mean side, face height ÷ 0.95. With a 30 mm figure, 1 fh is 30 mm: the typical stone is about 12 mm on a side and 24 mm high, and the cliff about 55 mm.

    Chapter 6

    Faces, edges, corners

    How much is planed off, how upright the sides are, and what the top does. These are the numbers that separate this style from a sharp low-poly rock on one side and a river boulder on the other.

    How wide the bevel is

    Estimated Read from the smooth sheet at three times its size, as a share of the stone's shorter side. Top bevels: 0.08 to 0.15 on pillars and blocks (H, A, O), up to 0.2 on caps and slabs (K, E). Upright chamfers vary more, and vary by formation: about 0.06 in A, 0.12 in H and O, 0.25 in M.

    Proposed One bevel number per formation, 0.10 to 0.22, with each stone within a fifth of it. Below 0.06 the stones turn into cut blocks; above 0.3 the top face shrinks to a patch and the stones turn into pebbles. The row shows both ends.

    in the style: 0.10, 0.16 and 0.22. Dimmed: outside it.

    How upright the sides are

    Measured Each stone's two sides were read off its outline (the edges within 40 degrees of upright) and averaged for its tilt. Nine stones in ten are between 4 degrees left and 9 degrees right of upright; the median is 2 to the right, the way the skyline climbs. Stones traced as leaners have a median of 7 and reach 26.

    Measured Inside one formation the tilts span 12.6 degrees at the median, and 12 or more in 28 of 52. Some of that is tracing error (about 5 degrees a stone), but not all: Estimated every leaning formation holds two sets, the leaners and upright stones in front of or beside them (P, T9, T16, S14, N, G), the leaners fan (P's four fins lean about 20, 15, 12 and 7 degrees toward the cut end), and D, S6 and T13 prop one leaner against an upright. No formation is one mass sheared over.

    Measured The difference between the two sides is the closing: positive when the stone narrows upward. Pillars: median 0, usual range 11 opening to 9 closing. Blocks close by 4. Toes close by 23, and by up to 40.

    These read 5 degrees either way from tracing error and from hidden edges, so a pillar's "opening" is noise around zero. What survives the error: pillars are parallel-sided, toes are wedges, and nothing overhangs its own foot by more than a few degrees.

    Corners of an outline

    Measured A traced outline has 8 to 14 corners (median 11). A sharp box seen from its corner has 6. The other five are bevels showing in the silhouette: every second corner of the box is cut.

    Measured Outlines fill 94 percent of their own convex hull at the median, and toes, which nothing hides, fill 99 to 100. The stones are convex; the dents in other outlines are where a stone in front hides part of one behind.

    Four tops

    Estimated A top is always one plane. What varies is its tilt.

    • Level, within 5 degrees: slabs, caps, most blocks.
    • Tipped, 5 to 12 degrees, rising toward the back or the peak: most pillars.
    • Shed, 20 to 35 degrees down toward the front: some pillars and front blocks in F, L, D, G, T3. It turns the top into a third main face.
    • Carried: on a leaner the top slopes up the way the stone leans, by about half the lean.

    There is no gable, no point and no dome. A stone has one top.

    Count of faces

    Proposed The whole solid has 6 main faces, 4 upright chamfers, 4 top bevels (and 4 more below on a cap), and small triangles where three of these meet: 18 to 26 faces. From the references' camera 3 main faces, 1 chamfer and 2 bevels show.

    Estimated About one main face in three carries a single faint crease, a change of plane of a few degrees running from a corner (A's front block, K's caps). No face carries more than two. The model gets this for free: moving each corner by 2 percent leaves the four corners of a face out of plane, and the face folds along one diagonal.

    Chapter 7

    Neighbours

    The style lives more in how stones sit against each other than in the stones. 494 pairs of touching stones were found in the traces: 320 side by side, 174 with one resting on the other.

    Six ways two stones meet

    Estimated Every contact in the four sheets is one of these. The first three make up nearly all of them; the last three appear once or twice in a formation at most.

    1. Abut. Side by side on the ground, faces touching along an upright joint, tops stepped or on one plane.
    2. Stand in front. A lower stone against the face of a taller one, hiding its foot.
    3. Rest on. One stone on the tops of one or two others, a level joint between, the upper one smaller and set back.
    4. Bridge. Caps from two piers meeting over a recess.
    5. Oversail. An upper block standing forward of a set-back wall under it.
    6. Prop. A leaning stone against an upright one, a wedge of space between them at the foot or the head.

    The joint itself is always the same: two bevels meeting in a shallow V. Stones touch; they never overlap into each other and stand apart only at a cleft.

    Most neighbours step; one pair in five shares a top

    Measured For the 320 side-by-side pairs, the difference between the two tops as seen: median 0.37 fh. But 9 percent are within 0.05 fh (the tracing error) and 21 percent within 0.12, and 39 of the 52 formations hold at least one such pair. Level neighbours are not an accident: they come in runs. H's two towers agree to 0.01; T4, T12 and T20 are flat-topped walls; F and L each carry one sloping top plane across two stones.

    Reconstructed "As seen" is not true height. The camera looks down 15 to 21 degrees, so a top half a figure further away reads about 0.15 fh higher. Where both stones' feet show, true heights can be read off (22 pairs): 14 percent within 0.05 and 23 percent within 0.12, the same picture. Along the cliff the far end is the far corner, so a skyline that is level as seen is, in true shape, a plane falling toward the far end by roughly 7 to 14 degrees.

    Proposed Two cases, not one rule. A plateau: a run of two to four neighbours sharing one top plane, in about one formation in three. Everywhere else, a step of at least 0.12 fh.

    The lower stone usually stands in front

    Measured Which of two neighbours is nearer was read from their feet: on the ground the nearer stone's lowest point is lower in the picture, and a stone standing behind another ends where that one hides it. In 83 percent of the 286 pairs where the feet differ, the stone with the lower top is the nearer one. Part of that is the camera lifting whatever is further away; among pairs whose tops differ by more than 0.3 fh, more than that lift, the share is .

    Measured The stone whose foot is nearest of all tops out under half the formation's height in 46 of 52. The six others matter: in A, D, F and L a stone 0.6 to 0.85 of the full height stands at the right front. Low in front is the habit, not a law.

    Measured Neighbours differ in size. The smaller of a pair is typically 0.53 of the larger; about one pair in nine is within 10 percent of equal. Within one formation the widest stone is typically 2.5 times the narrowest as seen, and over 3.6 times in one formation in ten.

    Resting, oversailing, bridging

    Measured A third of all traced stones (34 percent) rest on another stone. Of those 121, 64 rest on two or more: the usual resting stone lies across a joint. Stacks run four stones deep in C, I, T2, T7, T14, S7 and S11.

    Estimated Three forms, in order of how often they appear.

    • Rest on. A smaller block or a thin slab set back from the face, over one joint (A's slab, E's top block, D's three slabs, T12's).
    • Oversail. An upper block standing forward of a set-back wall beneath it, by 0.2 to 0.38 fh. It is as common at the far end as by the peak (10 of the 20 traced caps are in the left half): D, O, S3, S5, S10, T4. The block is thick, 0.35 to 0.9 fh.
    • Bridge. Caps from two piers meeting over a recess (T15, S9). The piers are unequal and so are the caps.

    Estimated Two formations are read here against a plainer alternative. B's large upper block is taken as resting on the two front pillars: its underside rounds in above both and neither shows a top face in front of it, which a block standing behind them would leave in view. K's two caps are taken as lying on one set-back wall, which shows under the left cap in both the smooth and the textured sheet; there is no free pier on the left.

    The space between

    Estimated Eight formations open a real gap in the mass: an upright cleft between two pillars (H, T13, T19, S6), or a recess (J, K, T15, S9). Elsewhere the stones are packed with no space but the joint.

    The recesses are three different things. T15 and S9 are gates: caps over a set-back wall between piers. K is a shelter: a roof of two stones, each about 0.9 fh long, standing forward of a set-back wall along more than a figure height. J is a niche: an upright slot 0.25 fh wide and 1.2 high between a wall and a block, with a small stone wedged across its head. One example each of the last two is too little to write a rule from.

    A cleft is as tall as the stones beside it, 0.05 to 0.15 fh wide, and widens slightly upward. A recess is one stone wide, about a figure high, and has a back wall: you never see through.

    The larger negative shape is the same in every formation: the empty front left of the plate, bounded by the cliff's face and floor. The cliff is composed against it.

    Chapter 8

    Repeated arrangements

    Between one stone and the whole cliff there are a handful of small groupings that come back again and again. A formation is one foot, one body, and sometimes a head.

    Estimated Cells listed by eye. Proposed The grey pictures are generated, one per arrangement.

    How they combine

    Measured Counted from the tracing labels and the chapter 1 classes. The foot is the commonest part; the head is optional in every class; clefts and overhangs are rare.

    Measured In of 52 formations the stone whose foot is nearest (lowest in the picture) tops out below half the formation's height. The exceptions with a tall stone there are A, D, F and L.

    Estimated Arrangements nest only one way: a body may be two bodies side by side (T18: a pillar wall and a stair; D: a wall with slabs on it), but a foot never has a foot and a head never has a head.

    Chapter 9

    Hierarchy

    Is a formation one mass cut into stones, or stones piled into a mass? Neither. The evidence fits a shallow hierarchy of three levels better than either, though one camera angle cannot prove it.

    Three explanations, tested

    Each model predicts something the traces can check.

    Cut from one block. Joints would run through, and the two sides of a joint would share a top. Measured 21 percent of neighbours are within 0.12 fh; the rest step.

    Piled up. Stones would overlap, leave holes, and any stone could be anywhere. Measured The outline is 74 percent full with joints closed, and the lower stone is in front 83 percent of the time.

    Rows of columns under one surface. The plate's back half is divided once into rows, each row once into stones; each stone rises from the ground to its own height near a shared envelope; a tall one may be cut across by level joints, and a few stones rest across two. This predicts tight packing, mostly stepped tops, low stones in front, and a skyline that climbs. All four are measured. It does not by itself predict plateaus or resting stones: those are added productions (chapter 11).

    The three levels

    Proposed Formation: one envelope, one grain, one bevel. Row: 1 to 5 bands from front to back, each a band of height. Stone: 1 to 4 per row, each a column or a stack of up to four.

    Reconstructed This cannot be shown false from the pictures. Rows are a reading of depth, and depth is what one camera angle hides: a formation read here as three rows could be two rows and a stone resting further back. The hierarchy is the simplest account that fits, not a finding.

    Pull the rows apart to see them. Each row is drawn in its own grey. In plan the rows are bands and the stones tile them with no gap: the division is done on the ground, and height is added after.

    Measured There is no sign of a fourth level among the traced masses. If they were themselves groups of smaller stones, sizes would fall into two clusters. The shares of chapter 5 have one hump, and the smallest traced mass is still 2 percent of its formation.     Estimated The classes of chapter 1 are this one structure with different counts: pillars are few rows and several stones per row; courses are many rows of one or two long stones; blocks are built in tiers; leaners add a leaning set beside upright stones; gates set one column of the back row back and bridge it.

    Chapter 10

    Randomness

    The variation is real but narrow, and most of it is not free. A few numbers are drawn once for the whole formation and every stone inherits them; a few are drawn per stone inside tight bounds; and the relations between neighbours are not drawn at all. They are rules that the draws must then satisfy.

    Where the variation lives

    Measured For each property, how much of the spread across all 352 stones is spread between formations (their averages differ) and how much is inside a formation.

    Width is the most a formation's own: a third of its spread is between formations, so a formation has a stone size. Height and height ÷ width are a quarter. Closing is almost wholly inside: it follows the stone's role, not the formation.

    Tilt needs care. Its standard spread inside a formation is 4 degrees, close to the tracing error, but the full span inside a formation is 12 degrees or more in 28 of 52. Both fit two sets of stones, an upright one and a leaning one, better than one shared upright (chapter 6).

    What shape the spread has

    Measured Width has one hump with a longer tail to the right (skew 0.8): most stones near 0.55 fh, a few long slabs. Face height is broad and flat-topped (flatter than a bell: excess kurtosis −0.7): from 0.3 to 1.4 every height is nearly as common as any other. Step between neighbours piles up near zero and thins out steadily to 1.5.

    Proposed Three different draws fit these.

    • Width: an even draw about the row's mean, ±60 percent, with nothing under 0.3 fh. Within a formation the widest stone is typically 2.5 times the narrowest as seen. Slabs are not a tail of this draw; they are rows of one stone.
    • Height: not drawn. It is read off the envelope, which sweeps evenly from low front to high back, and that sweep is what makes the flat top. Each stone then moves ±8 percent.
    • Step: not drawn either. It is whatever the envelope gives, with a floor of 0.12, except on a plateau, where it is nothing. The pile of pairs near zero in the chart is the plateaus.

    What goes with what

    Measured Across all stones almost nothing is tied to anything else. A stone's width does not predict its height, its size does not predict where it stands, and upper stones are neither smaller nor slimmer than lower ones. Each of these would be a visible habit if it were there (small stones on top is the habit of a rubble heap), and none is.

    The ties that exist are structural, not statistical: height with depth into the plate (the lower stone is in front, 83 percent), and the peak with the cut end (right of the middle in 50 of 52).

    What stays undecided

    Reconstructed One camera angle leaves these open. The grammar takes the simpler reading of each and says so.

    • Does a tread run under the next step? A stair slab may be a thin stone lying on the one below, or a tall column whose top shows. Both look the same from the front. Columns are used: they need no stacking order.
    • What is behind? No sheet shows the back or the far end. They are taken as flat and flush with the plate, like the cut end, because a cliff tile would meet a neighbour or the table edge there.
    • Is it rows at all? Rows are a reading of depth. The same pictures fit two rows and a resting stone where this page reads three rows. The hierarchy of chapter 9 is the simplest account, and the pictures cannot refute it.
    • The niche and the shelter. J and K are single examples of forms the grammar does not make. Each could be written as a production (a slot with a chockstone; a long roof on a set-back wall), but one example fixes no range.
    • How deep is a stone? Depth shows only at tops. Where tops show it is 0.7 to 1.1 of the length for pillars and blocks (M, H, O). For slabs it is the tread, a third of the length or less.
    Chapter 12

    Sensitivity and the style's edges

    One formation, one number changed at a time. Pictures left at full strength are inside the style as the four sheets show it; dimmed ones have left it, and the caption says for where.

    Proposed The base is a pillar formation of three stones along and two rows (seed 13), or a block formation in tiers where the number only matters there. Everything not named is held. The in-style ranges are judgements against the 52 references, not measurements.

    Which numbers the style is sensitive to

    Proposed Ranked by how little change it takes to leave the style.

    1. Each stone's own lean or turn. A few degrees drawn per stone is enough (chapter 10). The tightest bound of all.
    2. Bevel. In the style over a factor of two (0.10 to 0.22); outside it within a step either way.
    3. Open joints and offsets. 0.06 fh of slack and the mass falls apart into posts.
    4. Level tops. One run sharing a top is in the style; every top level reads as masonry.
    5. Stone count. Wide: 4 to 11. Two is a monolith and twenty is a wall.
    6. Height, fall, front, peak. Widest: the sheets themselves span most of the range. A shows that half height stays in the family, H and T12 that a wall with almost no fall does, F and L that a tall stone at the right front does.

    The order is the grammar's order turned over: the numbers decided last and lowest (chapter 10's scales 4 and 5) are the ones with no room, and the numbers decided first have the most.

    What breaks it

    • A lean, turn or bevel drawn per stone; a whole mass sheared over, feet and all.
    • Every top level; joints in line both ways.
    • Open joints; stones standing apart.
    • Thin stones alike in a strict stair (organ pipes).
    • A lid wider than its shaft all round (a hammerhead).
    • Stones under a fiftieth of the whole; more than eleven.
    • Sharp edges, or bevels over a third of the stone.
    • A top that is a point, a gable or a dome.
    • An oversail past 0.4 fh with nothing under it.
    • A far end at the ground.

    What this study is unsure of

    • Depth. One camera angle. Every depth, the back of every formation, and the row structure itself are inferred.
    • True heights. The camera lifts what is further away. Steps and "level" are given as seen; only 22 pairs could be corrected.
    • The plate. 1.8 to 3 figure bases across depending on the sheet, so sizes relative to a 35 mm square cannot be fixed from these pictures.
    • Lean. The measured tilt underreads strong leans; the by-eye figures rest on about ten formations.
    • Counts. They are of traced masses. About 25 may each be two stones; T7 and T14 would double if every groove counted.
    • Rare things. Gates (2), a shelter (1), a niche (1), clefts (4), the low formation (1): too few to give ranges. The shelter and the niche are not in the grammar.
    • Edges. Kinks and the soft rounding of the bevels were seen and described but not measured or modelled.
    • The sheets may not be independent. T and S share a recipe (foot, block, pillars, cap) more strongly than A–P; 52 formations are fewer than 52 separate designs.
