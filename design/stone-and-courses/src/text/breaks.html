What fails, and why

  • A groove has a roof. Wear opens a groove along every joint, and the stone above is a flat ceiling over it. The rock keeps a held bed sharp, but a chip is a cut with its own roof: chips and chunks are off in every wall because they fail the gate.
  • Cells the rock cannot save. The spires, the lips, the tilted rows and the courses of D, J and R fail with every stage at 0 or nearly: their cells already hang. The rock is held to no cuts on them.
  • Tilted and waved rows. Every piece of every stone has its bed on a different facet, so thin slivers appear everywhere. Row planes draw correctly and blocks follow them; most walls with dip or wave do not pass.
  • No seal. Stones meet exactly at their planes. Where two outlines differ by a hair, the union leaves thin down-facing slivers that count as overhang.
  • Convex cells. A stone with a hole, or a ring, is not reachable. A lip stone never rises, so a tall lip is not reachable.
  • Planes cannot be named. They are drawn at random; a document cannot give a wall a known rake or a step in its rows.
  • Photographs not yet reached. No wall reproduces the fine surface of the references: cracks, lichen, staining.

The Preset list marks the two walls meant to be refused (thin courses; wear and chips turned up); the sweep checks that the gate refuses them.
