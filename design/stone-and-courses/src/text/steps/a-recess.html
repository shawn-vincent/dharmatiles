Step 5a (L2) Recess: each stone stands back

After the stones are made, carried up and joined, each stone with a free front edge is set back by an amount of its own.

The amount is drawn from the range of recess by the graded rule, along the course: the stones are ranked by a smooth wobble along the front (recess bumps per square, the same unit as the front band), and each rank is mapped through the recess weighting into the range. It is the one graded rule that also draws the crest, the front band and the stone outline; four callers, one function. A stone that has been joined (step 5) counts once and moves as one. A stone’s front edges move back by its amount: its cell is cut by a plane parallel to each. A head joint that this opens, where a neighbour now stands further back, becomes a free face; gaps between stones are fine. A lip row keeps its own rule, and recess does not apply to it.

The front band (step 3a) and recess are two things. The band is a line: a cut across the stones of a course. Recess is a stone property: whole stones stepped back. Both stand behind the straight front, so a stone is cut by the band first and recessed after, and where they differ the further back wins.

There is no offset for the whole row and nothing accumulates from one course to the next, so there is no stay behind for recess; a wall that should lean gets its lean from the region’s front, not from recess. A free face opened by a head joint can overhang a little, which the gate counts like any other. A stone above stands on the tops of the stones below as before (step 8); one that loses its support is dropped.

Why. A row set back by one constant amount is a smooth ledge; a stone recessed by its own amount gives the broken front of real rock, and a joined stone moving as one stays a single stone.
