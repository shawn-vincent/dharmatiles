Step 2 (L1) The row planes

Every row boundary is drawn for the whole wall before a single stone exists.

Row heights are drawn between the two ends of height (with height skew) until they reach the highest crest, then scaled to fit it exactly. Each boundary between two rows is a plane through the whole wall: lean (side to side, the slope along the wall) and tilt (front to back, the slope into it) turn it about the middle of the footprint, each drawn from its own range in degrees across the planes up the wall by one weighting, so the rows run from a low angle to a high one (a range may be negative); wave adds two waves and a little noise on top. The ground plane stays flat.

One rule for how far. Each row keeps its planned height at the middle of the footprint, and is nowhere thinner than a floor of 0.2 of the thinnest planned row. A row may thin toward one end as long as it keeps its full height somewhere. The ranges are held to the angles that keep this true before anything is drawn (no scaling afterwards), and the card says when a range was limited, to what, and the floor in mm. On preset L the floor is about 2 mm and lean 0..8 is limited to 0..6.9 degrees.

A row thinner than a stone can be (4 mm at the 35 mm square) leaves no hole and no stone on air: the stone there is lifted to 4 mm over its bed, the row above takes its bed from the highest top under it, and a stone that would stand above the crest in less than 4 mm is dropped, as before. So a tilted wall stays one body. A plane is laid on a grid of flat triangles, so every stone that stands on one triangle shares exactly that plane. The stone sites sit on a grid set by the middle of width; regularity decides how far they are shaken from it; from one row to the next, bond is the chance a site moves and shift is how far.

Why. A stone that follows a plane has a flat bed exactly on the top below it, which is what keeps stacked stones from hanging in air.
