Step 8 (L4) The rock

Every stone becomes the rock: its cell, weathered by six stages, each of which does nothing at 0.

Goal

Make every stone the rock: the same weathered stone as the Weathered Stone page, filling the cell that steps 3 to 7 made, so a wall is stones and not prisms. Walls and cliffs share one stone; nothing about weathering is written twice.

How

Each stone’s cell is handed to the rock together with the rock’s settings (the six stages of Weathered Stone, each with a strength, zero meaning off) and a seed. The rock hands back the stone’s surface as a mesh. The wall’s own bevels, facets, tilts, chips and joint grooves are gone: the groove between two stones is the rock’s wear, opening where a surface meets a face a neighbour holds. The editor’s The rock panel is the rock’s own: a strength slider for each stage, its settings folded under the caret, a switch, Solo, All on and All off, a number that shows each stage’s status, and the build icon at the corner of the picture. The code is one copy, the same files the other page loads.

The rock

Takes in
a region, as an area and a top (the language’s words, as for a layer or a cliff): one or more convex parts, each an area (the stone’s outline in plan, in the stone’s own frame: +z up, origin at its footprint centre and its lowest bed; every edge a free face or a joint a neighbour holds), a bed under it and a top over it (each a plane, flat or sloped; the bed the seat on the ground, held when it rests on a stone; the top held when a stone stands on it) and any other planes it is already cut by. The envelope is the area carried up from the bed to the top. For the rock alone the region is a box (width, breadth, height), which is the same thing written the other way: a rectangle, a seat and a top at the height. One meaning, two forms (flagged in the language check). Then the settings (the six stages and patch) and a seed. The strata’s lean is the bed’s, read by the rock.
Hands on
a closed mesh of the stone in its own frame (a manifold: no edge is shared by four faces), and a report: its pieces, the cracks and shaves refused, the loose parts and specks, the overhang, the time of each step
  • Zero is the cell. Every stage at 0 returns the cell itself, flat faces and all; a stage at 0 is not in the list, so it cannot read anything.
  • Held is one meaning. A face is held when something outside the stone holds it: the seat, or a joint the wall marked. A held face is never outer, never cut by a chip, pocket or grain (the cuts stage follows the rule below), never exposed when a piece falls. A stone is held, and cannot fall away, by a held face.
  • The cuts stage on a held face: one rule, every course. A head joint (a held face that is vertical, between neighbours of one course: nothing bears on it) and a top are free faces and are cut like an outer face; gaps between stones are welcome, and a top that is cut only leaves less to stand on. A bed (stone on stone) may have its edges and corners cut, never its bearing core: 0.7 of the outline it bears on. Every cut must open to the air: its face meets a surface face or a head joint along an edge. A cut that meets a bed makes a face that points down, so its elevation is the bottom cuts’ draw (−55 to −25 degrees) clamped to 45 degrees from vertical (the gate counts 55). A stone with no held joint (the rock alone) meets none of this and is unchanged. The other stages keep their rules: a held bed that faces down is not rounded, no chip sits on its edge, and grain and pockets fade out on any face that leans down past 37 degrees.
  • The cuts stage has three limits, not three shares: preserveTop, preserveSides, preserveBottom. Each says how much of the stone, measured in from that face, no cut may reach: no cut that touches a top face goes deeper into it than the share allows. The units are a share of the stone’s height for the top and the bottom and of its half-width for the sides (each is measured along the direction a cut goes in from that face, which is the stone’s own size that way). 100% leaves every face of that kind alone; 0% (the default) lets cuts go as deep as they like. Every cut is drawn as before (the count, the angles, the same random spread; the slots are shared between top, sides and bottom 8, 12 and 16 as they always were, a fixed split that is no longer a control). A cut that goes deeper into a face than its limit allows is shortened where it crosses in, not refused: turned away from the top (down, to 45 degrees at most) or from the bottom (up) in steps of 10 degrees until it no longer reaches past the limit there, so a long side cut loses its end at that face; a cut limited by the sides is redrawn shallower, its depth halved up to three times; only then is it refused and counted. A side cut is limited by preserveSides and by the top or bottom limit only at the end that touches the face. A cut counts as touching a face when the material it takes reaches the face’s plane, so with all three at 100% every cut is refused and the stage is the stone as it was, the same as cuts at 0. The bed’s outline (below) wins over preserveBottom: the cuts that bring the bed inside its support are mandatory and are not limited by it; only the ordinary cuts are.
  • The bed carries bear: the outline it must lie within. This is the one input the rock takes for a stone on a stone beyond its area: a convex polygon in plan, in the stone’s own frame (the same word the cuts already use for the part of a bed that something bears on). The wall gives it as the convex hull of the cut tops below, intersected with the part: a hull is convex, so it is one polygon and no part of the support is thrown away. The area is still the stone’s footprint; the stone does not shrink to fit. The cuts stage, in the same pass as the bottom cuts, adds one cut for every edge of the outline: through that edge at the bed’s height, rising outward at an elevation drawn from the bottom cuts’ spread (25 to 55 degrees below horizontal) and clamped at 30, so the bed ends inside the outline and the stone widens above it. These cuts are mandatory: the seat floor does not stop them, only degenerate geometry does, and a refusal is counted. A cut that climbs past the top takes that part of the top; an edge outside the area cuts nothing. No bear on the bed, no change.
  • Courses are built in order. A stone stands on what is left of the tops below it, so the stones are built course by course (the stones of one course in parallel, the workers idle between courses), and each course hands the next its cut tops. The outline a stone’s bed bears on is the convex hull of the upward faces of the built meshes it rests on (within the sampling step of their top planes, opened by 0.3 mm so jags and slivers go), intersected with each part of its area; its bed sinks that far into the stone below so the two touch where a sampled top is a little under its plane. A stone with too little to stand on is not built: a hull under 4 mm² or 15% of its own cell (half of a cell under 8 mm²). Four mm² is about twenty line widths square, the least a slicer can lay as one island of its own; 15% is under the rock’s own seat rule (a quarter), because neighbours also touch a stone. It is decided stone by stone: only the stone with nothing to stand on goes, and a stone whose supports were all dropped loses its support in turn. A dropped stone is a gap in the wall, counted in the build record; a part the hull does not reach is left out of its stone. A stone the envelope has cut thin (step 6) must also stand on real support: the part of its footprint at least the minimum stone height high must be over the cut tops below it (15% of itself, 1.5 mm² at least), so a piece that is only the bed’s sink over a gap, held by nothing, is not built and no stone is left floating.
  • Everything is keyed. A stone’s weathering depends only on its seed (from the wall’s seed, its course and its index), its cell and the settings: moving a neighbour does not change it.
  • Size is the cell’s box. The rock reads size from the cell’s bounding box, so the reach of a cut (bite), the pieces that fall and wear’s exposure scale with the stone, as they do on the other page.

What the wall adds

Area and top. The wall hands each stone its own outline: the Voronoi cell of its column, an irregular polygon in plan, not a box (with the graded stages on, the outline of step 7a, cut at the front by step 3a’s band, as convex parts); the bed and top are the planes under it and where it stops (a stone that rises through several rows stops at that row’s plane). Held faces. An edge on a neighbour or a merged edge, the top when a stone stands on it, the bed when it rests on a stone, are held joints; a bed on the ground is the seat; the edge two pieces of one stone share (a stone on a tilted plane is cut along its planes) is a crack inside the stone, found by the cell and not held; every other face (a free face, a chamfer of step 9) is surface. Overlap. A held plane stands 0.15 mm further out, so neighbours overlap and the union has nothing coincident. Parallel. The stones are built in up to as many workers as the machine has cores less one (this browser: 11), a stone always in the same worker so its pieces are kept there. Draft first. The cells are drawn at once, and each stone replaces its cell as it arrives. Only what changed. A stone is keyed by its cell, its settings, its seed and the quality; a slider rebuilds the stones whose key it changes, and the page keeps the rest. A wall-wide slider changes every key, but a stage that works after the pieces (chips, pockets, grain, wear) reuses each stone’s pieces in its worker, so only the sampling runs again.

Shrink-wrapped support

Preset L as a 2×1 wall, cuts at their preset strength, draft, every top free. Before: a cell reaching past the stones under it was cut along their outlines, so the joints below were carried up and the upper courses broke into many small stones (a 1×1 of preset L, seed 1: 8, 9, 13 and 21 blocks in courses 0 to 3, the median area falling from 89 to 15 mm²). After: a cell stays one block, so every course is its eight stones (8, 8, 8, 8; medians 89, 92, 79, 71 mm²; none under 12 mm², over seeds 1 to 3 and, at preset L, to five courses), and each stone bears on the shrink wrap of what is under it.

The support. A stone’s bed bears on the convex hull of the cut tops below it (from their built meshes), intersected with its own cell; holes and gaps between the supports inside the hull are bridged. A hull is convex, so there is no largest-piece limit: the outline is one polygon and none of the support is thrown away. The cuts at the bottom bring the bed inside that outline, as before. Rising stones: a stone that rises through a row keeps its site in the row as a ghost, so the cells next to it end on their bisectors with it, whole and convex, and are not cut into wedges round its corners (preset L at rise 0.6: no block under 12 mm²; before, up to five a course).

The gate and bridges. The gate does not know about bridges: a bridge’s underside is flat and faces down, so it counts it as red. A bridge here is the part of a stone’s bed inside its hull that is not over a support (a hole or gap in the support). Over the 114-build sweep (19 presets, both systems, 3 seeds, draft): the gate as it is passes 22; with the bridged faces left out it passes 72 (66 before this change). Every build is one body. The median bridged area is 2.7 mm² and at most 603 mm² (presets L and B have 32 gaps); the longest bridge, measured as the narrow way across a gap, is 3.1 mm at the median and 6.8 mm at most. Preset L, 1×1: 540 mm² bridged over 32 gaps, 5.8 mm longest; the gate sees 12.0% red, and 0.005% red and 0.026% in the orange band with the bridges left out. Presets L, B, K, A, F, O and M pass 6 of 6 builds without the bridges; presets J, C, Lf, P, R and the tilted-row forms still fail as before. Stones not built: a median of 0 a build, a mean of 0.2, at most 6; 101 of the 114 builds drop none (before: mean 3.7, 68 drop none). Preserve top 0, 50, 80 and 100% on presets L, B and K (3 seeds, draft): stones not built 0.4, 0.4, 0 and 0; the gate as it is passes 0, 0, 0 and 9 of 9 builds (at 100% the tops are whole and there is nothing to bridge); with the bridges left out 9 of 9 at every setting. Preset L, 1×1, one thread: draft rock 747 ms, union 1049 ms (before 790 and 1052), 144,874 triangles; fine rock 2083 ms, union 2616 ms (before 2388 and 2716). The painting of a bridge in the viewer is computed with the stones and is the page’s own: the gate verdict is unchanged.

Preserve top at 0, 50 and 80%

Over three seeds of presets L, B and K (draft, one tile each): stones not built per build, 18.2 at 0%, 17.0 at 50%, 7.9 at 80% and 0 at 100%; the gate passes 8, 7, 8 and 6 of the 9 builds, every build one body. The defaults (all three at 0%) limit no cut. Why stones are still dropped at 80%. One build of preset L (seed 1) at 80%: 12 stones not built, all in the top course. The course below has flat tops (the supports under them have 60 to 90 mm² each in courses 0 and 1), but course 2 has many sliver cells (3 to 11 mm²) and their tops are cut away entirely (one has a single triangle left), and the course-3 stones lie across 4 to 7 of those slivers: of 12, nine have cells under 25 mm², and for all twelve the cell overlaps its supports’ cells (so it is not the cells above missing the ones below) but the flat top left under it is 0 to 6 mm² against 2 to 8 needed. Not the largest-convex-piece rule (it only shapes the bed’s outline), not the minimum measured against the wrong area (it is the cell’s), not the bed sinking (it changes nothing at the tops). Two real findings. (1) A defect, fixed: the 4 mm² floor was larger than the stone’s own cell for cells under 4 mm², so a fully supported sliver was always dropped; a small cell now needs half of itself (the mean drops per build over the sweep went from 5.4 to 3.7, and 68 of 114 builds drop none). (2) The layout: a preserve is a depth, and a shallow cap is wide: a facet 15 degrees off flat that goes 2 mm deep runs 7 mm across, so 20% of the height leaves about 75% of the flat top on stones of ordinary size (measured: 46% flat at 0%, 49% at 50%, 75% at 80%, 91% at 100%) and none on a sliver. The proposed fix is to merge cells under about 12 mm² into a neighbour in step 3 (they are the stones that cannot keep a top), or to measure preserve top as the share of the top’s area left flat. Why the gate got worse as the top was preserved. The red and orange faces are all at the height of a bed (course boundaries), 100% of them, at every setting. With the top preserved fewer stones are dropped (28 built of 51 at 0%, all 51 at 100%), so there are more stone-on-stone beds, each with the small overhang a bed has where it meets a sampled top; the faces per bed did not change. A first version that refused a long side cut, and then tilted it toward the top, ate the flat top and passed 3 of 9 at 100%; shortening it away from the face passes 6 of 9.

One body. Two builds of the 114 (preset F db seed 3, preset R db seed 1) had a second body, a sliver 0.7 mm across: a top stone the envelope had cut down to the thin slab of its bed’s sink, over a gap. The cause was the crest planes: in a steep valley the chords’ extensions cut below the crest and left almost nothing of the stone, and a stone left empty was kept uncut. Now the planes are the one of three that takes least off the stone, a stone the planes empty is not built, and a stone the envelope has cut thin must stand on real support. Sweep of 114 builds: as it is 20, with bridges left out 76, every build one body; stones the rock drops, mean 0.57 (preset M 5.0 to 1.3: its steep crest lowered the tops it stands on); median draft rock 500 ms, union 550 ms.

Printing

The union of the meshes is what the gate judges. The preset rock (cuts, a little grain and wear) passes where the cells pass; chips and chunks do not: a chip is a plane and an ellipsoid and the ellipsoid’s underside is a roof, and a chunk set back under the one above is a ledge. Both are in the panel, off in every preset, and the wall marked “refuses” in the Preset list loads one. See where the model breaks for the numbers.
