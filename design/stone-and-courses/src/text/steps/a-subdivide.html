Step 3 (L2) Subdividing a row

A row is cut into stones by dividing the area the rows below left it: each site owns the cell closer to it than to any other.

The area a row may fill is the footprint moved in by the row’s setback (the ends and the back by no more than the tile margin); a lip row moves out instead. Each site owns the part of that area nearer to it than to any other site, cut off exactly at the area’s edge. An L is first cut into convex parts. Neighbouring cells overlap by {{OVL}} mm at each joint, so the union has no coincident faces. A stone that carries on up through the row keeps its footprint (step 4): its site stays as a ghost, so the cells round it stay whole. A cell is one Voronoi cell, not cut along the stones under it (a lip row is the exception); what it stands on is the rock’s business (step 8). A cell with too little support is dropped, and so is one that the crest leaves too short, so the upper courses form a stepped mound under the crest.

Why. Cells that fill the area exactly give joints only where two stones meet.
