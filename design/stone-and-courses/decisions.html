Setback becomes recess, per stone

18 Nov 2026

Tried
A setback that stepped the whole row back by one amount from the row below, so a wall leaned or terraced.
Chosen
Each stone with a free front edge is recessed by an amount of its own, after rise and span, drawn from the range along the course by the same graded rule as the crest, the front band and the outline (a recess weighting and bumps). A joined stone moves as one; a head joint opened beside a stone that stands further back becomes a free face; a lip row is not recessed. Nothing accumulates, so a wall that should lean takes its lean from the region’s front.
Why
A whole row set back by a constant is a smooth ledge. Orin ruled the front band (a cut across stones) and recess (whole stones) are two things, one rule with two carriers, and that a changed meaning under the old name is a defect: the word is now recess and the spec version is 2, so an old spec is refused, not read wrongly.

Step 1 (L0) The footprint and the crest

It fixes where the wall may stand and how high it may rise.

Goal

Fix the box the wall must fill: where it stands, and how high it rises. Everything later is measured against it.

How

The tile is a square of 35 mm (DungeonBlocks) or 25.4 mm (OpenLOCK, 0.7257 of it). A wall of Run 2x1 is two squares long. The wall keeps 0.6 mm (EDGE_MARGIN, a constant of the tile) inside the tile edge so two tiles side by side never touch.

The footprint is the rectangle that is left: from that margin at the sides, from 9 mm behind the front edge, back by depth (22 mm by default). A preset with a wing makes the footprint an L, so a corner can be built.

The crest is the height of the top as you walk along the wall. It is graded, the same rule that draws the front band and a stone’s outline: a smooth wobble along the wall with a bumps control (2.5 a square, about one every 14 mm), ranked (sort by value, rank (i + 0.5) / N), and each rank mapped through a weighting’s quantile to a place in the band between the two ends of top. The crest therefore uses its whole band, ends included, whatever the seed (top 11 to 64 reaches 11.9 to 63.1 dmm on a 1×1; the value noise it replaces reached 28 to 55 on one seed and 43 to 56 on another), and the wobble only decides where the highs and lows fall. The weighting is flat by default (the band used evenly); low, high, bell, peak and ends share the heights as they do for the front band. The dirt is 3.4 mm thick; the crest is measured above it.

Takes in
top, depth, Run, Scale, a wing (J presets)
Hands on
a footprint polygon, the crest function, the tile size

Examples

The front of the footprint is 9 mm behind the tile edge (preset front), the depth is 22 mm, the margin is 0.6 mm. On OpenLOCK every length is 0.7257 of its DungeonBlocks value; the 0.45 mm printer line does not shrink, so the same wall has thinner features relative to the line.

Nothing is printed yet. The margin is what makes tiles fit: the tile plane is the limit the gate and the printer both respect.

With the band off the target is the plain wall as before. The setback of each course is not drawn.

Step 2 (L1) The row planes

It draws every row boundary for the whole wall before a single stone exists.

Goal

Decide where every row of stones starts and stops before a single stone exists, so that stones of any width and height can follow the same lines.

How

First the rows. Row heights are drawn one at a time between the two ends of height (the thinnest and the thickest a row may be) until they reach the highest crest height (the larger end of top), and are scaled together to fit it exactly, so there are courses up to the top of the arch; where the crest is lower, the stones of the upper courses are built inside the envelope and left out if too short (step 6). The rows are then stretched a little so they add up exactly.

Then the planes. Each boundary between two rows is a plane through the whole wall. It starts level at the sum of the rows below it. dip tilts it by up to that many degrees in a random direction. wave adds two random sinusoids of random direction and a wavelength between 30 and 90 mm, plus a third as much value noise. The ground plane stays flat.

No two planes may come closer than 0.6 of the row's height anywhere in the wall. If they would, the upper plane's tilt and waves are scaled down until they don't. A plane is not a smooth surface: it is laid on a grid of flat 12 mm triangles, so every stone that stands on one triangle shares exactly the same plane.

Last, the sites: the centres of the stones. They sit on a grid with spacing set by the middle of width. regularity decides how far they are shaken from the grid (0.45 of the spacing at 0, none at 1) and how far every other row is offset (half a stone at 1). From one row to the next, bond is the chance a site is moved instead of carried straight up; shift is how far it moves.

Takes in
height, width, regularity, bond, shift, dip, wave, the crest
Hands on
the row planes; the sites of every row
Deep numbers: the constants
  • row height drawn with the weighted range height (third number: bias), never under 4 mm; the last row is dropped if that brings the sum nearer to the crest mean; then all rows are scaled by mean crest / sum;
  • plane facets are 12 mm triangles on a grid anchored at (-90, -90) mm;
  • a plane's tilt magnitude is a uniform draw between 0 and tan(dip); the two waves have amplitude 0.7 and 0.4 of wave times a draw of 0.5 to 1.5;
  • carried: probability (1 - bond) x (0.4 + 1.2 x a draw), at most 1; bond runs from its first number at the foot to its second at the crest;
  • a moved site stays inside the footprint and at least half a spacing from another.

Printing

Beds and tops that follow a tilted plane are overhangs of up to the tilt. The gate says how far this can go: tilts and waves run into trouble when stones no longer meet exactly (see where the model breaks).

Step 3 (L2) Subdividing a row

It cuts each row into stones by dividing the area the rows below left it.

Goal

Cut each row into stones so that they fill the row exactly, with joints only where two stones meet.

How

The area a row may fill is the footprint, moved in by the row's setback: the face moves back by a draw between the two numbers of setback each row; the ends and the back move back at most 0.6 mm. A lip row moves out instead.

Each site owns the part of that area closer to it than to any other site (a Voronoi cell). The cell is cut off exactly at the area's edge. There are no ghost sites and no mirrors: a cell's outer edge lies on the edge of the area, and its corners belong to cells. If the area is not convex (an L), it is first cut into convex parts and each cell is clipped by each part.

The cells of one row overlap their neighbours by 0.15 mm at each joint, so the union later has no coincident faces.

A row above is cut in the area the rows below left it: a stone that carries on up through this row keeps its footprint here (step 4), so the cells round it stay whole and convex: the stone’s site stays in the row as a ghost (it makes no cell), and the cells next to it end on their bisectors with it instead of being cut into wedges round its corners.

A cell stays one block, its Voronoi cell: it is not cut along the stones under it, so a course is as many stones as it has sites (the same eight, a 1×1, in every course) and no stone is broken into little pieces by the joints below. What it stands on is the rock’s business (step 8: the stone bears on the convex hull of the tops below it, and holes and gaps inside the hull are bridged). A cell with under 0.4 mm² of overlap with the cells under it, or under 1.5 mm² of support, is dropped. A lip row, which stands out on purpose, is still cut along its supports. A cell whose bed is within 3 mm of the crest everywhere over its footprint (the crest is read along the whole stone, not at its centre) is not built; the stone under it is the top of the wall there. The upper courses exist only under the high parts of the crest, so the courses form a stepped mound under it.

Takes in
the sites, the area of the row (footprint moved by setback or lip), the stones that rise into it, the supports under it
Hands on
convex cells with a tag on every edge: joint (shared with a neighbour) or free (open to the air)
Deep numbers: how much of the footprint the cells fill

On course 0 the cells cover 100% of the footprint (measured on a 0.7 mm grid, 8 seeds). Course 1 covers 94 to 100%, course 2 89 to 100% (preset F stays at 100%): higher rows lose area only to the setback, to the stones that rise, and to cells under 1.0 mm² (these are skipped).

Printing

The cuts that keep a stone on its supports are the first printing rule: a stone is never wider than what it stands on, except where a later step chamfers it (step 9).

Step 3a (L2) The front band

The front of a course is a band between today's front and one depth behind it, drawn course by course by the graded rule.

Goal

Give the front of the cliff a depth: instead of one straight line every course is cut to, the front of a course is an area band between today’s front (the inner line) and a second line depth behind it (the outer line), and each course draws its own edge across the band from its own keyed seed, so courses differ. Depth 0 is today’s straight front, exactly.

How

One rule draws every outline from a weighting (this step, along a line, and step 7a, round a circle). Make a smooth wobble along the outline, with a bumps control for how many wobbles. Rank it: sort the points by wobble value and give point i of N the rank (i + 0.5) / N. Map each rank through the weighting’s quantile to get t, a place in the gap from 0 to 1. A point’s distance is inner + gap × t. The weighting says how far and bumps says how many, so every edge has exactly the weighting’s mix of distances; only where they fall changes. It is the rule a dirt top already uses (Band::rolled, a ranked smooth field, with bumpSize as the how-many control), bent along a line.

Here the inner line is the course’s front as steps 1 to 3 made it (the footprint’s front edge moved by the course’s setback), the gap is depth, and the wobble runs along the front with bumps per square bumps for every 35 mm. The points are the nodes of one line shared by every course (about six to a bump); the edge between them is straight. The seed is the wall’s, the step and the course, so a course’s edge never depends on another course’s.

Where it is applied. The cells of step 3 stay the convex cells they are; each stone is cut to its course’s edge when its cell is handed to the rock (step 8). The cut leaves a front that is a polyline, so the stone’s area may not be convex: it is split into convex parts, which the rock already takes, the edge two parts share being a crack inside the stone (never a joint with a neighbour). A joint is never moved: it is trimmed where the front now meets it, on its own line.

Overhang

A course may reach out beyond the course below it. Where it does, its bed has nothing under it, and the gate sees a flat roof. The rule stay behind the course below (on by default) says a course never stands in front of the course under it where the unbanded wall does not either: node by node the edge is the larger of the course’s own line and the one below it, and because every course’s line has the same nodes, each edge is above the one under it everywhere, not only at the nodes. A lip course, which stands out on purpose, is left alone. The cost is honest: the running maximum means the upper courses stand further back, so over a tall wall the front trends toward the outer line and the variation shrinks. With the rule off each course draws its own line and overhangs where it is further out than the one below.

Each stone’s bed edge also needs the 48 degree mouth the wall cuts along a free edge (step 9’s rule), so a bed never starts in front of the rounded top of the stone under it; a stone with a new free edge gets that facet along it.

Step 4 (L2) Rise: a stone carries on

A stone rolls, again and again, to carry on up through the next row.

Goal

Let some stones be tall: one stone that stands through several rows, beside stones of one row.

How

Every stone starts in one row. It then rolls against rise: if the roll succeeds, the stone carries on into the next row, and rolls again. It stops at the first failed roll or at the top of the wall. At 0 every stone is one row; near 1 stones run all the way up.

A stone that rises keeps its footprint in the rows above (step 3 cuts the other cells round it) and its top is the top plane of the last row it reaches. A stone on a lip row never rises.

Takes in
the cells of a row, rise
Hands on
each stone's last row; a list of footprints that the rows above must leave alone
Deep numbers: what rise does to the count

Each stone rolls once per row it reaches, so with rise r the chance a stone spans k rows is rk-1(1-r). At 0.6 about 4 stones in 10 carry on at least once, and 1 in 6 reaches three rows. Preset L has 171 solids and a 153 ms union at rise 0; preset T (rise 0.6, span 0.3) has 115 and 80 ms.

Printing

A tall stone is one convex piece, so it is never a bridge. Beside it, the stones of the rows above rest on the stones below as before.

Step 5 (L2) Span: neighbours join

Neighbours that stand between the same two planes roll to become one stone.

Goal

Let neighbours of one height join into one wide stone, down to a near slab with a few joints.

How

After the stones have risen, two neighbours may join if they start on the same row and end on the same row. Each such pair rolls against span. A pair that joins loses the joint between them: that edge is held (a joint), but it gets no groove, and it is no longer a free or a joint edge for the later steps.

The two cells stay two convex solids that overlap by 0.15 mm, so a wide stone is still made of convex pieces. At 0 every pair stays apart; at 1 every pair that can join does.

Takes in
the stones of a row with their first and last rows, span
Hands on
larger stones: groups of cells whose shared edges are marked joined
Deep numbers: what span does to the count

Pairs are tested once each, left to right, so a chain of three can all join. A joined pair is still two convex solids, so the union time does not fall with span; the rock’s work does (no groove on a joined edge). Preset S (rise 0.7, span 0.7) has 81 solids and a 52 ms union.

Printing

A wide stone has fewer joints, so fewer V gaps; it is also made of pieces that touch, not of one convex solid, so the union has to join them. The gate sees the result.

Step 6 (L2) Building a stone

Each stone becomes a solid between the plane under it and the plane over it.

Goal

Turn every cell into a solid: a stone with a flat bed on the plane under it and a top on the plane over it.

How

A cell is a polygon. The stone is the prism of that polygon, from its bed to its top. The bed is the top of whatever is under it. The top is the bed plus the row's height (or the sum of the rows it rises through). wander changes each stone's height at random by up to its own share of the row; at 0 every stone ends exactly on its plane. A stone is never under 4 mm tall.

Every stone is given its top when it is built: its row’s top plane (or, for a stone that rises, the top plane of the last row it reaches) intersected with the cliff’s envelope, the footprint extruded up to the crest. The crest reaches the stone as planes it must lie under, one of three sets, whichever takes least off the stone: chords of the crest across its footprint (about every 2 mm), the one chord between its ends, or a flat plane at the lowest crest under it, each lowered by however far it stands above the crest anywhere on it (chords follow a hump closely but, in a steep valley, their extensions cut below the crest and can leave nothing of the stone); so the stone never rises above the crest (the sampled mesh is at most 0.5 mm over it, the sampling step) and follows it from below; a stone the planes would leave empty is not built; a part of a top lowered this way is a free surface, not a held joint. The stone is built inside that top and nothing is cut off it afterwards. A stone whose height, its top less its bed averaged along its footprint, is under the minimum stone height (4 mm times the scale, the least a row may be) is not built.

If the planes are tilted or waved, the prism is cut by the plane under it and the plane over it. A stone that stands on more than one 12 mm facet of a plane is made of one piece for each facet, each cut by that facet's own plane. The stone above is cut by the same facets, so its bed is exactly the top below it. There is no sink and no seal: stones meet exactly at the planes. The 0.15 mm overlap between neighbours at a joint (step 3) stays.

The cell

This prism (or its pieces on a tilted plane) is the stone’s region: its area (the Voronoi outline of its column on the ground, irregular in plan) carried up from its bed to its top, the plane where the stone stops. Step 8 hands that area and top to the rock, which works inside it as the Weathered Stone page works inside a box. Why the stones read square: every front face is the footprint’s one straight front line between two row planes, so from the front each stone is a rectangle; its irregular outline shows only in plan (cells fill 0.77 of their bounding box) and at the ends, and the rock then only breaks its corners. The editor draws the cells as soon as they are made (every stage of the rock at 0 is the cell, exactly), then each stone as its rock arrives.

Step 7 (L3) Joints

It decides which faces of a stone are joints and which neighbours are one stone; it cuts nothing.

Goal

Decide, for every face of every stone, whether it is a joint shared with a neighbour or open to the air. Nothing is cut here.

How

Step 3 already marked every edge of every cell: a joint edge is shared with a neighbour, a free edge is open to the air, and an edge between two cells that step 5 merged is joined, not a joint at all. This step only reads those marks and hands them on. The neighbours touch behind the face: each cell overlaps the next by 0.15 mm in plan, so the union has no coincident faces.

The V you see in a finished wall is not carved here. It is the groove the rock’s wear opens in step 8 where a stone’s surface meets a joint face: a joint face is held by the neighbour, so the rock never cuts it and wear rounds only its edge with the surface. That keeps this step simple: change which faces are joints and the weathering follows.

Nothing is cut, so nothing here can fail. The overlap of 0.15 mm keeps neighbouring stones one body; where they overlap they are not a joint at all.

Step 7a (L4) The stone outline

Each stone's footprint lies between a core and its cell, drawn by the same graded rule round a circle; joints never move.

Goal

Draw each stone’s own footprint instead of its whole cell: the outline lies between a core (a share of the disc that fits inside the cell) and the cell itself, so the stone is no longer the Voronoi polygon with flat faces. Strength 0 hands on the cell exactly, as today.

How

The same rule as step 3a, round a circle. Points are placed round the stone (every vertex and the free edges cut into pieces, about six to a bump, at least 24 round the stone); each has a wobble value, a smooth periodic function of its angle with bumps whole bumps round the stone, from the stone’s own keyed seed. The values are ranked ((i + 0.5) / N over the points that can move), mapped through the weighting’s quantile to t, and each point goes to core + (cell − core) × (1 − strength × (1 − t)) along its ray from the stone’s centre. t = 1 is the cell, t = 0 the core. The weighting says how far, bumps says how many.

Only free edges move. A face a neighbour holds (a joint) is never moved: its end points stay where they are, and the outline eases back to the cell over the last stretch before one so that two neighbours meet at the same corner. The outline is the area the rock is built in. It can be non-convex: it is split into convex parts (ear clipping, then neighbours joined while they stay convex), the rock’s own form for a cell of several parts, the edge two parts share a crack inside the stone, and the stone is cut at its row planes as before.

Held by what holds it

A stone is cut to what holds it: its area is intersected, in plan, with the union of the final areas of the stones it rests on (computed course by course from the ground up), so a bed is always over material and no stone hangs over a smaller one. The shared edge of the two is then a free edge on both, and each gets the joint’s lower mouth (step 9’s facet) along it. Two small rules in the rock keep the parts of one stone one stone: the corner cut of the cuts stage is refused where it would meet a crack between parts (the cap rule, as it already is for a held top or bed).

How it reads. The mix of distances is exactly the weighting’s, and the shapes are what each weighting is meant to give: low mostly small stones with a few long spikes out to the cell; high mostly full stones with a few notches in; ends deep notches and full spikes with little between; bell an even ragged edge half way between core and cell. Two limits. Only a stone’s free edges move, and in course 0 those are the front, the back and the two ends: stones in the middle of the wall are all joints and do not change. And at strength 1 and 6 bumps the outline is spiky (about six points to a bump); a lower strength, fewer bumps or a larger core is calmer.

Step 8 (L4) The rock

Every stone becomes the rock: its cell, weathered by six stages, each of which does nothing at 0.

Goal

Make every stone the rock: the same weathered stone as the Weathered Stone page, filling the cell that steps 3 to 7 made, so a wall is stones and not prisms. Walls and cliffs share one stone; nothing about weathering is written twice.

How

Each stone’s cell is handed to the rock together with the rock’s settings (the six stages of Weathered Stone, each with a strength, zero meaning off) and a seed. The rock hands back the stone’s surface as a mesh. The wall’s own bevels, facets, tilts, chips and joint grooves are gone: the groove between two stones is the rock’s wear, opening where a surface meets a face a neighbour holds. The editor’s The rock panel is the rock’s own: a strength slider for each stage, its settings folded under the caret, a switch, Solo, All on and All off, a number that shows each stage’s status, and the build icon at the corner of the picture. The code is one copy, the same files the other page loads.

The rock

Takes in
a region, as an area and a top (the language’s words, as for a layer or a cliff): one or more convex parts, each an area (the stone’s outline in plan, in the stone’s own frame: +z up, origin at its footprint centre and its lowest bed; every edge a free face or a joint a neighbour holds), a bed under it and a top over it (each a plane, flat or sloped; the bed the seat on the ground, held when it rests on a stone; the top held when a stone stands on it) and any other planes it is already cut by. The envelope is the area carried up from the bed to the top. For the rock alone the region is a box (width, breadth, height), which is the same thing written the other way: a rectangle, a seat and a top at the height. One meaning, two forms (flagged in the language check). Then the settings (the six stages and patch) and a seed. The strata’s lean is the bed’s, read by the rock.
Hands on
a closed mesh of the stone in its own frame (a manifold: no edge is shared by four faces), and a report: its pieces, the cracks and shaves refused, the loose parts and specks, the overhang, the time of each step
  • Zero is the cell. Every stage at 0 returns the cell itself, flat faces and all; a stage at 0 is not in the list, so it cannot read anything.
  • Held is one meaning. A face is held when something outside the stone holds it: the seat, or a joint the wall marked. A held face is never outer, never cut by a chip, pocket or grain (the cuts stage follows the rule below), never exposed when a piece falls. A stone is held, and cannot fall away, by a held face.
  • The cuts stage on a held face: one rule, every course. A head joint (a held face that is vertical, between neighbours of one course: nothing bears on it) and a top are free faces and are cut like an outer face; gaps between stones are welcome, and a top that is cut only leaves less to stand on. A bed (stone on stone) may have its edges and corners cut, never its bearing core: 0.7 of the outline it bears on. Every cut must open to the air: its face meets a surface face or a head joint along an edge. A cut that meets a bed makes a face that points down, so its elevation is the bottom cuts’ draw (−55 to −25 degrees) clamped to 45 degrees from vertical (the gate counts 55). A stone with no held joint (the rock alone) meets none of this and is unchanged. The other stages keep their rules: a held bed that faces down is not rounded, no chip sits on its edge, and grain and pockets fade out on any face that leans down past 37 degrees.
  • The cuts stage has three limits, not three shares: preserveTop, preserveSides, preserveBottom. Each says how much of the stone, measured in from that face, no cut may reach: no cut that touches a top face goes deeper into it than the share allows. The units are a share of the stone’s height for the top and the bottom and of its half-width for the sides (each is measured along the direction a cut goes in from that face, which is the stone’s own size that way). 100% leaves every face of that kind alone; 0% (the default) lets cuts go as deep as they like. Every cut is drawn as before (the count, the angles, the same random spread; the slots are shared between top, sides and bottom 8, 12 and 16 as they always were, a fixed split that is no longer a control). A cut that goes deeper into a face than its limit allows is shortened where it crosses in, not refused: turned away from the top (down, to 45 degrees at most) or from the bottom (up) in steps of 10 degrees until it no longer reaches past the limit there, so a long side cut loses its end at that face; a cut limited by the sides is redrawn shallower, its depth halved up to three times; only then is it refused and counted. A side cut is limited by preserveSides and by the top or bottom limit only at the end that touches the face. A cut counts as touching a face when the material it takes reaches the face’s plane, so with all three at 100% every cut is refused and the stage is the stone as it was, the same as cuts at 0. The bed’s outline (below) wins over preserveBottom: the cuts that bring the bed inside its support are mandatory and are not limited by it; only the ordinary cuts are.
  • The bed carries bear: the outline it must lie within. This is the one input the rock takes for a stone on a stone beyond its area: a convex polygon in plan, in the stone’s own frame (the same word the cuts already use for the part of a bed that something bears on). The wall gives it as the convex hull of the cut tops below, intersected with the part: a hull is convex, so it is one polygon and no part of the support is thrown away. The area is still the stone’s footprint; the stone does not shrink to fit. The cuts stage, in the same pass as the bottom cuts, adds one cut for every edge of the outline: through that edge at the bed’s height, rising outward at an elevation drawn from the bottom cuts’ spread (25 to 55 degrees below horizontal) and clamped at 30, so the bed ends inside the outline and the stone widens above it. These cuts are mandatory: the seat floor does not stop them, only degenerate geometry does, and a refusal is counted. A cut that climbs past the top takes that part of the top; an edge outside the area cuts nothing. No bear on the bed, no change.
  • Courses are built in order. A stone stands on what is left of the tops below it, so the stones are built course by course (the stones of one course in parallel, the workers idle between courses), and each course hands the next its cut tops. The outline a stone’s bed bears on is the convex hull of the upward faces of the built meshes it rests on (within the sampling step of their top planes, opened by 0.3 mm so jags and slivers go), intersected with each part of its area; its bed sinks that far into the stone below so the two touch where a sampled top is a little under its plane. A stone with too little to stand on is not built: a hull under 4 mm² or 15% of its own cell (half of a cell under 8 mm²). Four mm² is about twenty line widths square, the least a slicer can lay as one island of its own; 15% is under the rock’s own seat rule (a quarter), because neighbours also touch a stone. It is decided stone by stone: only the stone with nothing to stand on goes, and a stone whose supports were all dropped loses its support in turn. A dropped stone is a gap in the wall, counted in the build record; a part the hull does not reach is left out of its stone. A stone the envelope has cut thin (step 6) must also stand on real support: the part of its footprint at least the minimum stone height high must be over the cut tops below it (15% of itself, 1.5 mm² at least), so a piece that is only the bed’s sink over a gap, held by nothing, is not built and no stone is left floating.
  • Everything is keyed. A stone’s weathering depends only on its seed (from the wall’s seed, its course and its index), its cell and the settings: moving a neighbour does not change it.
  • Size is the cell’s box. The rock reads size from the cell’s bounding box, so the reach of a cut (bite), the pieces that fall and wear’s exposure scale with the stone, as they do on the other page.

What the wall adds

Area and top. The wall hands each stone its own outline: the Voronoi cell of its column, an irregular polygon in plan, not a box (with the graded stages on, the outline of step 7a, cut at the front by step 3a’s band, as convex parts); the bed and top are the planes under it and where it stops (a stone that rises through several rows stops at that row’s plane). Held faces. An edge on a neighbour or a merged edge, the top when a stone stands on it, the bed when it rests on a stone, are held joints; a bed on the ground is the seat; the edge two pieces of one stone share (a stone on a tilted plane is cut along its planes) is a crack inside the stone, found by the cell and not held; every other face (a free face, a chamfer of step 9) is surface. Overlap. A held plane stands 0.15 mm further out, so neighbours overlap and the union has nothing coincident. Parallel. The stones are built in up to as many workers as the machine has cores less one (this browser: 11), a stone always in the same worker so its pieces are kept there. Draft first. The cells are drawn at once, and each stone replaces its cell as it arrives. Only what changed. A stone is keyed by its cell, its settings, its seed and the quality; a slider rebuilds the stones whose key it changes, and the page keeps the rest. A wall-wide slider changes every key, but a stage that works after the pieces (chips, pockets, grain, wear) reuses each stone’s pieces in its worker, so only the sampling runs again.

Step 9 (L2 to L4) Fitting to what is below

Where a stone stands over a cut-back edge, it is chamfered so nothing hangs in air.

Goal

Make sure no stone hangs in air: where a stone stands over a stone that has been cut back, give it a chamfer to match.

Stacking on the cut tops

The rock cuts a stone’s top like any other free face, so the top a stone offers the next course is less than its cell. A stone is built after the stones it rests on, and it bears on the shrink wrap of what they leave: the convex hull of the upward faces of their built meshes, intersected with the stone’s own cell. Holes and gaps between the supports inside the hull are bridged, and the stone is one piece, not one piece for each stone under it. A hull is always convex, so the outline the bed must lie within (bear, step 8) is one convex polygon and nothing is thrown away. Wherever the cell reaches past the hull, the bottom-cut kind of cut, at an angle drawn the same way and never more than 30 degrees below horizontal, takes the bed in. A stone with nothing under it, or with a hull under 4 mm² or 15% of its cell (half of a cell under 8 mm²), is not built. A bridge’s underside is flat and faces down, so the gate counts it as overhang; the editor paints it blue and says so in the legend, and the gate popup lists it. Rise changes nothing here: with every top free the cuts reach the same faces whether stones carry on into the next row or not.

How

This is not a step of its own: it runs inside steps 3 to 8.

  • Beds settle. A stone's bed is the highest top of the stones under it, and all of them are raised to match, until none moves.
  • The mouth. At each free edge the stone looks at the stones under it. If their top stops short of its edge, the edge is chamfered at 48 degrees from vertical (53 if the stone below stops more than 0.6 mm short). If it stops more than 0.6 mm short and the bed would stay at least 1 mm², the edge is instead cut back vertically.
  • The top outline (R9). Wherever the top outline of a stone below has no other stone's top beyond it, the bed is chamfered from that outline at 51 degrees from vertical, so a corner the rock takes from the top edge of the stone below never leaves a flat ceiling.
  • Lips (R8). A lip stone is cut into pieces over its supports, each chamfered from its support's outline the same way.
  • Tilted planes. On a tilted bed the chamfer planes are tilted with it, and eased by the bed's slope so they stay within the overhang band.

If none of these can make the edge safe (an overhang over 1.2 mm that cannot be cut back), nothing is done, and the gate reports it.

Step 10 (L5) The foot band

A band of loose stones is scattered at the foot of the wall.

Goal

Scatter the loose stones that lie at the foot of a real cliff, and nowhere else.

How

The cliff itself only says where the band is: for every half millimetre along the first row, one cell of the band, weighted from 0.5 to 1.5 by how high the crest is there (tall parts shed more).

The scatter is a separate entry of the scene, not part of the cliff. scree stones for each square are placed in the band: sizes 1 to 7 mm (never under 2.6 mm), biggest first, each clear of the ones before it, reaching up to 5 mm out from the face.

Step 11 (L6) The union and the gate

The pieces are joined into one body, and the gate checks that it can print.

Goal

Join everything into one body and check, by numbers, that a printer can make it.

Every convex piece is turned into its hull, and all the hulls, the core behind the wall and the ground plate are united in one operation (Manifold). Then the gate reads the result:

  • watertight: the status of the manifold must be NoError;
  • one body: only one piece with any volume (a stone that touches the rest only along an edge would be a loose body);
  • overhang: the area of down-facing faces leaning more than 65 degrees from vertical must be at most 0.1% of the downward area, and the area between 55 and 65 degrees at most another 0.1%. The bed on the ground is not counted.

Nothing is culled and no threshold is ever changed to pass: a wall that fails is shown as failing. The line a printer lays down (0.45 mm) and its layer (0.2 mm) are also measured and shown, but not part of the verdict.

Where the model breaks (before the bridge measure)

The gate passes 50 of 114 builds across 19 presets, both systems and 3 seeds; the nine shipped presets pass 30 of 54. Flat rows are close; tilted and waved rows fail.

  • No seal, by Shawn’s choice. Stones meet exactly at their planes. Where two stones’ outlines differ by a hair (a chamfer or the rock’s wear on either), the union leaves thin down-facing slivers that count as overhang past 65 degrees: 64 builds fail on overhang and 0 on loose bodies; watertight never fails. Passes by preset (DungeonBlocks and OpenLOCK): L wall 1 and 0, B ragged wall 1 and 0, E terrace 3 and 0, M columns 2 and 1, G leaning 3 and 0, O chunky 3 and 3, F sheer wall 3 and 1, A low massif 3 and 1, N masonry wall 3 and 2, D cap course 1 and 0, K deep lip 1 and 0, Lf cantilevers 0 and 0, J corner 3 and 0, J corner, 2x2 2 and 2, C spire 1 and 0, P spire 0 and 0, T tall beside short 2 and 3, R fieldstone, tilted rows 0 and 0, S near slab 3 and 2.
  • A groove has a roof. Wear opens a groove along every joint, and the stone above the groove is a flat ceiling over it. The rock leaves a held bed sharp (the chamfer of step 9 is the roof), but a chip is a cut with its own roof: chips and chunks are off in every preset because they fail the gate (on preset L, chips 8 per cm: 1.6 % red; chunks 0.5: 2.2 % red and two bodies).
  • Cells the rock cannot save. The spires, the lips, the tilted rows and the courses of D, J and R fail the gate with every stage at 0: their cells already hang (the chamfers of step 9 were written to read the facets of the old step 8). The rock is held to no cuts on them.
  • Tilted and waved rows. Every piece of every stone has its bed on a different facet, so the slivers are everywhere: dip 3 or wave 1 and up pass in no build I measured. Row planes draw correctly and blocks follow them.
  • Tall stones that touch only along an edge are loose bodies; they fail whenever the roll makes one.
  • A lip stone never rises, so a tall lip is not reachable. A stone with a hole, or a ring, is not reachable: cells are convex.
  • Planes cannot be named. They are drawn at random; a document cannot give a wall a known rake or a step in its rows.
  • Cost. Many small stones are expensive: R takes 1659 ms for a 1×1 against 822 for L.
  • Photos not yet reached. No preset reproduces the fine surface of the references (cracks, lichen, wear); the rock makes broken faces, rounded edges and grain; the cracks, lichen and staining of the photographs are not reached.

WordsReused: height (a row’s minimum and maximum), bond, setback, regularity. New, one meaning each: rise (chance a stone carries on into the next row), span (chance neighbours of one height join), dip (tilt of the row planes), wave (waviness of the row planes). The wall’s dip leans the row planes; the rock’s strata dip is not a wall word (the courses are the strata, each stone takes its bed’s lean). These four wait for an Orin review before they enter the registry.

The plan and its questions

Build the engine in this order. Each stage has a small torture tile and the check that gates it.

  1. Footprint and crest, then the row planes. Torture tile: a J corner with dip 3. Check: planes never closer than 0.6 of a row.
  2. The partition. Torture tile: an L footprint. Check: cells cover 100% of course 0.
  3. Rise and span. Torture tile: preset T. Check: no cell reaches past its supports; no loose body.
  4. Prisms between planes, then joints. Torture tile: preset S. Check: every bed is exactly the top below it.
  5. Weathering, then fitting. Torture tile: forms L (lip) and E (ledges). Check: overhang budget.
  6. The union and the gate. Check: the nine shipped presets pass in every seed.
