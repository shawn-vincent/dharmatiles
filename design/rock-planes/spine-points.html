Spines as Points

A spine that spans layers, or a fuse that joins dots in a layer, becomes one dot at its midpoint, and the wall is the power diagram of all the dots. Does that one stone still fill what its spine was meant to fill? That depends on how far its neighbours were kept away when they were placed. This is a slice through the wall: layers run across, the dots of each layer sit on its middle line.

Why L/2

  1. Every point of the wall belongs to the stone whose dot is nearest. With weights, nearest means the smallest d² − w.
  2. After joining, the stone has one dot, at the midpoint. Each end of its segment is L/2 from that midpoint, where L is the segment's length.
  3. So the stone keeps an end only if no other dot is closer than L/2 to that end. A neighbour 7 mm from the top end beats a midpoint 9 mm away, and takes the top.
  4. A cell is convex. If it holds both ends, it holds the whole segment between them.
  5. So the rule, applied when the other dots are placed: keep every other dot at least L/2 from each end. The diagram itself never changes.
stone keeps end e ⇔ for every other dot q: |q − e|² − w_q ≥ (L/2)² − w_stone

Any point, not just the ends. The stone keeps a point P exactly when the disc centred on P that passes through the midpoint holds no other dot. L/2 is that disc for the ends. Reach past the ends moves P further out along the segment, and its disc grows with it.

Keeping dots a normal spacing r from the segment (the "Segment" rule) is the same thing when r ≥ L/2. It falls short once L/2 is larger than r: a span over more layers than about 2r/t + 1, or a fuse of more dots than fit in r.

What it costs

  • It fills only part of its dots' cells. Those cells are boxes reaching the layer faces. One convex cell around a midpoint tapers toward its ends, so it misses their corners, and the neighbours take them ("fills" in the cards: about 70% for a two-layer span, under half for four). Keeping the ends is necessary, not enough.
  • L/2 pushes neighbours back only near the ends. The stone reaches its ends and stays about as wide as before; the corners are still lost.
  • Reaching further makes a spike, not a flat top. The disc for a far point passes through the midpoint, so near the middle it is narrow and the side neighbours stay put; near the far point it is wide, so the next layer's dots there move aside. The stone grows a point toward the layer face. A flat cap at the face would need the dot above twice as far from the midpoint as the face is: half the next layer would be empty.
  • The stones round its ends grow. No dot may sit in the cleared discs, and the stone only takes the part of them nearer its midpoint (the spike). The rest goes to the dots on the discs' edges, so they grow: with nothing joined the largest stone is about 1.3 times the usual, a four-layer span 1.8, reaching to the layer faces about 2.6. Moving a cleared dot out to the disc's edge, off its layer line, instead of sliding it along the layer packs more dots round the edge and brings that to about 2.2. It cannot go away: the space has to belong to someone.
  • Weight fills more, and spills. A boundary moves out by w / 2d, where d is the distance to that neighbour: most toward the nearest dots (the sides), least toward the far ones (above and below), the opposite of what a tall stone wants. Try "Weight instead of keep-away": the stone fills more of its dots' cells and spreads into what would have been its neighbours' space ("spills").
  • Its top and bottom are pointed. A dot in the next layer up or down caps a point off flat.
  • Only one shape for the whole wall. Stretching all of space up before building the diagram (and back after) keeps every cell convex, and makes tall thin cells cheap. It works for a style whose spines all run one way, like Columns. A stretch for one stone alone would curve its boundaries.
  • The Midpoint rule treats the joined stone as a single dot from the start. It is shown for contrast: its neighbours sit right on the segment.
