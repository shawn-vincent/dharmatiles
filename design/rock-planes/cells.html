Rocks from cells

Option 3 for a concave shape or one with a hole, drawn as a slice through it. The planes don't make the rock by all cutting one block. They divide all of space into cells, and the rock is the group of cells that lie mostly inside the shape. A group of convex cells can have notches, so the rock can be concave. In 3D every line here is a plane and every cell is a convex block; the steps are the same.

Closer to Rock Planes: each plane keeps to its own face

The cells make bumps because a cell's outline is whichever planes happen to cross it, so a fracture plane or a plane from the far side can bound a cell at the rim. Rock Planes never makes bumps: every plane cuts everywhere, and the rock is what all of them leave. That only fails on a concave shape because a plane on one arm would cut through the other. So limit how far a plane reaches, and keep everything else.

  1. Each face has its own strip: the band over it, bounded at each corner by the line that splits the corner's angle, a few mm deep.
  2. A face's planes cut over its own strip and its neighbours' strips across outward corners, but never across an inward corner. Across outward corners this is the same as Rock Planes, so faces meet at clean edges. At an inward corner, the two faces' planes each stop at the corner's split line, so the notch stays sharp.
  3. The rest is Rock Planes: a touch point in the middle third of each face, a crown there, planes domed away from it, turned by Spread and sunk by Sink, every plane passing its own strips' touch points, and half the outward corners bevelled. Every point near the surface is cut by its own face's planes, so nothing pokes out, and the rock touches every face.

What it gives up: the rocks are no longer cut from one shared set of cells, so neighbours don't get matching faces (panel 5). In 3D a face's strip is the prism over it, cut at each edge by the plane that splits the edge's angle. Each plane removes a convex chip (its strip beyond the plane), and the rock is the target minus the chips: one Manifold difference.

In 3D

Each line here is a plane and each cell is a convex block, cut from a box around the shape. The program already has the pieces: a block cut by planes is how Rock Planes builds today, and joining solids is a Manifold union. Only cells near the shape matter, so you split only those and leave far-off space alone.

What it costs

  • Thin slivers: two nearly parallel planes leave a thin cell. With spikes removed, a sliver follows its neighbour; in 3D the test is a cell's thickness, and a thin cell follows the neighbour across its largest face.
  • Trimming to the target needs the target as a solid, and in 3D it is one more Manifold step (an intersection). Where a cell pokes out, the trimmed face is the target's own surface, as it is in Rock Planes.
  • The cell count grows fast with the number of planes, so fewer planes go much further than in Rock Planes.
  • Touching the shape and the bevel shares become rules about which cells are kept, not about how deep each plane sits.
  • An inside corner faces downward on one side, so the overhang gate needs looking at there.
  • A hole through the side has a roof, and the roof prints as an overhang. Its planes have to meet in a pointed arch, each roof face at least 35° from flat. A hole that goes straight down has no roof and is fine.
  • A ring can break: if fracture planes leave a thin part of the ring short of the keep share, the rock comes apart in two. Joining needs a check that the kept cells still form one piece.
