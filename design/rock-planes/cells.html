Rocks from hulls

Rock Planes for any shape, drawn as a slice through it. The shape is written as alternating convex hulls: its hull, minus the hulls of its hollows, plus the hulls of any bumps inside those. Rock Planes runs on every hull, cutting inward on rock and outward on hollows, so every plane is a whole plane on a convex piece.

Seed

How it works

  1. Hulls. Take the shape's convex hull. Each stretch of the outline that falls inside it is a hollow, closed by a mouth along the hull; each hole is a hollow with no mouth. Do the same to each hollow: what of its hull is not hollow is a bump of rock inside it. Stop after three levels.
  2. Planes on a rock hull, as in Rock Planes, along each face that is real surface (a mouth gets none): each the face's direction, domed away from that face's touch point by up to Tilt, turned by about Tilt and sunk by up to Sink. A crown sits on each touch point. A curved stretch gets a touch point every 10 mm or so instead.
  3. Planes on a hollow. Each flat face becomes a chain of one, two or three segments bowing into the rock, drawn by seed from that face's own stream. Sink moves it in by a half to a whole Sink, and Tilt bends it as it domes a rock face: each bend goes in by about Tilt's slope over its distance to the nearer end, and a single segment leans by about Tilt. The chain can be a V, a three-faceted bow, or one face leaning from an end. One end touches the shape, or both. The chain always bows into the rock, never toward the hollow, so the pocket stays convex. On a curved stretch each plane pivots on the nearest touch point it faces.
  4. Touching. A rock plane is pushed back out to every touch point of its hull; a hollow plane passes the one it pivots on. A rock piece is also kept inside its hull, and a pocket always takes its whole hollow (a plane that would cut into the hollow is pushed out to the hollow's corner) and reaches no more than Sink into the rock. So the rock never passes the shape.
  5. Chamfers. Chamfered of a rock hull's outward corners, drawn, get a chamfer cutting one to two Sinks into what its faces leave. A chamfer makes one draw from its own stream: a turn of up to half of Tilt either way, never more than a third of the angle between its two faces. If a touch point keeps it from biting a whole Sink, it takes the turn mirrored, no turn, or the limit away from either face, the first that bites (no second draw). So do Chamfered of the outward corners where a rock face meets a hollow's face (an L's arm tips), which neither hull owns: each is cut on its own, the corner alone, one to two Sinks into the rock that is there. Hollows' own corners get none, so inside corners stay sharp.
  6. The rock is the first hull's piece, minus each hollow's pocket, plus each bump's piece, in order.

Open questions

  • In 3D a hollow's ceiling is an overhang: hollow planes need the mirror of Rock Planes' rule, none facing up too steeply.
  • For a few shapes the hulls keep nesting; three levels covers rock-like shapes.
  • A pocket is convex and must hold its hollow, so a hollow face cannot bulge toward the hollow at a touch point. So a hollow face can bow into the rock (a V, three facets) but never out toward the hollow; a round hole comes out as a polygon around the circle, up to Sink bigger than it.
  • A pocket reaches up to Sink into the rock; where a hollow comes close to the outline, the wall between them gets thinner.
