Rock Planes

A rock as planes all around a shape. Each plane is put on the shape (on a face, along an edge or at a corner), turned a little at random, sunk a little and cut. That is the whole algorithm.

Planes

How many face planes are put around the shape. Chamfers come on top of these.
The share of the shape's edge length, and of its corners, that is chamfered, drawn; the rest stay sharp. A straight edge is chamfered whole; a round rim in pieces.
How far each plane is turned at random from the shape's own direction there, and how far a flat face's planes lean away from its touch point at the rim. At 0 flat faces stay flat.
How deep the planes bite (of the shape's size): a face plane sinks up to this, and a chamfer cuts one to two times this into what the faces leave.

How a rock is built

  1. The shape is picked in the menu, or by the seed itself when it says Random (the seed's first draw chooses one of the seven). It is the space the rock should fill (sizes in mm, at the 35 mm square drawn on the plate). The rock stays inside it and touches it on every side. The bottom is treated like every other side; there is no special case for the ground yet, and the rock is set down on its lowest point.
  2. Touch points. Each flat face of the shape gets one point, drawn in the middle third of the face; a sphere or an oval gets eight tilt around it, a cylinder one on the top, one on the bottom and four around the side. Every plane passes through or beyond every touch point (and every crown's patch, below), so the rock reaches each of them: it touches every face of the shape.
  3. Where the planes go. Chamfered of the edges' total length, and of the corners, drawn, are chamfered; the others stay sharp and get no plane. A straight edge is taken whole, in the drawn order, while that brings the length nearer the share; a round rim is cut into short pieces, each an edge of its own, so it is chamfered piece by piece. Planes face planes are thrown over the faces, each kept a spacing away from the others; then each chamfered corner gets one plane and each chamfered edge planes spaced along it. Face planes and chamfers never look at each other, so Chamfered never moves a face plane.
  4. Crowns. Each flat face gets one plane at its touch point: the face's plane turned by about Tilt about the point, with a patch around the point (15 to 35% of the way to the face's nearest edge, drawn) that no other face plane may cut (a chamfer may eat into it). Without it every plane that passes the touch point meets there and the face comes to a point like a low pyramid; with it the face's planes meet around a facet near its middle.
  5. Face planes start as the face's tangent, domed around the face's touch point by up to Tilt at the rim (so a flat face's planes lean away from it and do not all fall on one plane; at Tilt 0 the face stays flat), are turned at random by about Tilt and sunk by half to all of Sink. Each makes one draw from its own stream, keyed to its spot: a direction and a size, which Tilt scales, so raising Tilt turns the same plane further. A turn that would dip much deeper into the shape anywhere than at its own point is shortened just enough, never drawn again; then the plane is pushed back past the touch points.
  6. Chamfer planes start halfway between the edge's two faces, or as the average of a corner's faces. Each makes one draw from its own stream: a turn of up to half of Tilt, never more than a third of the angle between the faces it cuts, so it stays on its edge or corner. It is set against what the face planes leave and cuts one to two times Sink into it, stopping at any touch point. If that bites less than a Sink, the chamfer takes, in order, the turn mirrored, no turn, or the turn's limit away from each face it cuts: the first that bites a whole Sink, with no second draw. If a touch point keeps one from cutting that deep, the next sharp edge or corner in the drawn order is chamfered instead, so the share always holds.
  7. Kept inside. Where face planes meet outside the shape, each of them is sunk by how far the corner pokes out, as far as the touch points let it, and the planes are cut again (up to six rounds). Without it a sphere's planes, sunk only a little, meet past its surface between them.
  8. The rock is everything inside all the planes and inside the shape itself (the shape's own faces; a curved shape's are 200 of its tangent planes). It is one closed convex solid. Where the shape's own face shows, the rock touches it.

Every job draws from its own seeded stream: touch points, crowns, which edges and corners are chamfered, the spots, each face plane (keyed to its spot) and each chamfer (keyed to its edge or corner). So Tilt and Sink move no spot and only turn or sink the same planes, and Chamfered moves no face plane. Overhang paints orange past 55° and red past 65°, as in the program; with no ground case the underside is all overhang for now. A magenta face means the planes left a gap (a fault).
