How a rock is built

  1. The shape is the space the rock should fill (sizes in mm, at the 35 mm square drawn on the plate). Its bottom is cut at the Foot height and the rock is set down so that cut sits on the plate.
  2. Major faces. A cube, slab, pyramid or wedge uses its own faces, each tilted about its own centre. A sphere, oval or cylinder uses three joint sets: one near level (the top) and two upright ones roughly square to each other, each giving a face on both sides. Each direction is tilted at random (about Spread), its plane is put where the shape reaches furthest in that direction (the shape's support), then pushed in by up to Major cut.
  3. Fill faces. More directions, thrown at random and kept apart from each other and from the majors, until there are Faces in all. Each plane is put at the shape's support and pushed in by up to Fill cut. No direction points more than 50° below level, so no face overhangs past the 55° print limit.
  4. The rock is everything inside all the planes and above the foot: one closed convex solid. Because every plane starts at the shape's surface, the rock pokes out a little where planes meet and cuts in where they were pushed in. The numbers on the right say by how much.
  5. Chamfers and chips. Every edge of that solid whose faces turn by more than Sharp edge, except edges on the ground, gets one more plane across it, leaning toward one of its two faces and skewed a little so its width tapers along the edge. Some corners get a chip plane. Both obey the same 50° limit. Then the whole rock is cut by all of them at once.

Each step draws from its own seeded stream, so changing Faces keeps the major faces, and changing the chamfers keeps the planes. Overhang paints orange past 55° and red past 65°, as in the program.
