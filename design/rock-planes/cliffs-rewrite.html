Cliffs from Scratch

If the Cliffs from Spines engine were written again, knowing everything 288 rounds taught: what each stage should own, what it may promise the next, and how to make holes and overhang impossible by construction instead of chased. The look stays; pixel identity does not.

The short answer

Recommendation

Your four stages are right, with one change of order inside the third and one change of kind in the fourth.

  1. Envelope, as now.
  2. Masses: spines, every cell, then the masses settled (each bottom lowered onto the masses under it) and one contact graph (who touches, holds, overlaps whom, with the contact polygons). Nothing later adds a neighbour.
  3. Stones, bottom-up, each finished before anything above it is looked at, in one pass of flat cuts chosen together: the flare that guarantees support (from the finished stones under it), the bevels, the facets and Round, each a plane that faces down no more than 55°, but for the flare when Bevel is off, which faces down at 65° (rock is free in the band) to keep the most stone. Where a flare cuts under a bottom edge, the flare is that edge’s bevel, at 45° when Bevel is on; no second bevel is made there. No stone above exists yet, so there is nothing to keep for it: the keeps, guards and top-down carve go.
  4. Surface: weathering, flaking and cracks as depth fields applied to one closed mesh per stone, moving its vertices and splitting its edges, never cutting it. A hole cannot happen because the mesh's topology never opens; new overhang cannot happen because every field has a slope budget and a clearance from every other stone.

Expected: holes 0 by construction; flaking 68 s to about 1–3 s on Fieldstone (estimate); about 3.5 million triangles to about half a million; roughly 700 of the engine’s 2,230 lines gone. Do a one-day spike of stage 4 on Ashlar first, since it carries most of the risk and all of the speed.

Where the complexity sits

Holding, then carving the other way
Stones are held bottom-up (each finds what holds it among those placed before), but carved top-down, so that a stone’s top keeps flat what the carved stone above still needs. Between the two passes sit keeps, guards, the attempt statuses (bevel, pushed, taper, guard, down, bite), the joint drawback for a stone that spans a joint, and the bevel cap beside a flare. Most of the last five rounds of bevel fixes were fixes to this seam.
Flaking as a formula, meshed face by face
The flaked stone is “under every face’s plane pushed in by its field”, and each face is meshed on its own lattice, finding where another face takes over by bisection. Two faces meet along one curve but each draws it with its own chords, so slivers open along seams and at corners: the holes. Each round closed one class (corner cells, bevel tips, pushed-out bevels) and the cost grew.
Rules that exist to undo other rules
Flaking carries about a dozen exceptions: covered faces, the face-to-face tie, the rest rule, the parallel-face exception, narrow faces, leaning faces made shallower, faces with no headroom flat, up-facing bevels pushed out, the climb no steeper than 60°, the corner bend, the finer lattice on bevels. Nearly all of them stop a scoop from baring something it should not; they are one rule, “never dig past another stone’s surface or into what holds”, stated many times.
Cracks as polygon surgery
About 630 lines walk traces, cut each face into the convex pieces outside the grooves, and merge them back. Flaking then has to read the cracks’ cutters as more planes.

Your proposal, checked against the code

Envelope first holds
It reads nothing else and nothing reaches back into it.
All masses, then adjacency holds
Already nearly true: every cell is known before any stone is carved. What breaks it today is that bottoms are lowered inside the holding chain, against stones that may already be cut. Lowering is a mass-level act (it measures against tops and moves a plane), so it can be done on the masses, in the same bottom-up order, before any carving. Carving and support only ever remove material or lower a bottom into a known neighbour, so the contact graph made from the settled masses is a superset of every later contact: later stages ask it and never search.
Flare, bevels and facets in one pass holds, done bottom-up
Right that support belongs with the cuts that chop edges, since they decide what a stone above can stand on. Done bottom-up, the pass reads only the finished stones under the stone, which exist. It gathers every cut for the stone at once: the flare planes over its held places, a bevel for each seen edge, the facets, Round. Every one is a plane facing down no more than 55° (a flare with Bevel off: 65°, inside the band rock is free in), so none can make the stone less printable, and since they are chosen together no cut undoes another. The flare’s angle follows the bevel: with Bevel on it is 45° and is the bevel of the bottom edge it cuts, the bevel being made only on the bottom edges no flare reaches; with Bevel off it is 65°, as near level as the gate allows, to keep the most stone. A stone above that rests on a bevelled top rests on what is there: its 45° flare makes the upper side of the groove, the 45°-over-45° joint you already called fine.
Weathering, scoops and cracks never affect support holds
It can be guaranteed if all three become depth fields under the same limits (a slope budget per face, a clearance from every other stone, half the stone’s thickness) instead of cuts. Weathering as a field rounds each seen convex edge to its arc: a vertex near the edge moves in onto the rolling ball, so it is smooth with no planes to bevel again, rounds only what is seen, and stops where the arc would face down past the target.

Stage 4, in more detail

This is where the holes and the time are, so it gets the most care.

One closed mesh, never cut

Each finished stone is a convex polytope. Triangulate each face and split long edges by halving, with every new midpoint kept in one table by its edge, so two faces that share an edge share every point on it. That mesh is closed when it is made, and the only later change to its topology is the same halving, so it stays closed: there is no step at which a seam can open.

The fields

Each face has a depth: the deepest of its weathering (near a seen convex edge, how far the rolling ball of the drawn radius lies inside the edge), its scoops (the same lattice of paraboloids keyed by the face’s normal, so faces facing one way stay in step) and of its cracks’ V grooves (a groove is a depth that falls off the trace at the walls’ angles; a free end tapers by making it shallower). Bevels take no scoops of their own. Three limits apply to every depth:

  1. Slope budget. A face facing down at angle α may tilt no more than 65° − α (rock is free in the 55–65° band), so no new surface faces down past the target. This replaces the leaning-face and no-headroom rules.
  2. Clearance. A depth never reaches past another stone’s surface: along the face’s inward normal, it stops where it would cross into or out of a neighbour from the contact graph; and where a neighbour lies within the hold distance in front of the face, it is 0. One ray against a few convex neighbours per vertex. This one rule replaces covered faces, the face-to-face tie, the rest rule and its parallel exception, and the pocket at joints. It allows a scoop to bare air and never another stone.
  3. Thickness. A depth is at most half the stone’s own thickness along the normal, so a thin slab is never scooped through from both sides.

Weathering is smooth, so it needs no crease splitting; it is applied first, and the scoops and cracks are measured from the weathered surface, so a worn edge is still chipped. Each limit is a clamp, so the clamped field is then limited in slope across the face once (a two-pass distance on the face’s lattice), which replaces the climb no steeper than 60°.

Placing the vertices

A vertex on one face moves in along the normal by the depth. A vertex on an edge or a corner moves to where the displaced surfaces of its two or three faces meet (a 2×2 or 3×3 solve). Then each vertex is checked against its stone’s other displaced faces, and moved onto any it lies outside: that is how a scoop running off an edge chips it, the same “under every face pushed in” formula as today, sampled at points the mesh already has instead of meshed afresh.

Sharp where it should be

A ridge between two scoops, a crack’s bottom line and a chip’s edge are creases. Where an edge’s two ends are governed by different terms (two scoops, a scoop and a crack, a face and its neighbour), the edge is halved, the new point found on the crease by bisection along the edge, and the halving repeated a few levels. Halving keeps the mesh closed, and points land on the creases, so they stay sharp.

Cost

About one depth evaluation, a handful of neighbour planes and one clamp per vertex, against 54 depth evaluations per vertex today; vertices only as dense as the free surface needs (faces wholly inside another stone stay coarse). Estimate: Fieldstone at 0.5–1 mm in 1–3 s with about half a million triangles. The spike measures it.

Decisions

Flare and cuts decided
One pass of cuts per stone: flare, bevels, facets, Round. A flare under a bottom edge replaces that edge’s bevel.
Weathering decided
A depth field in stage 4 with flaking and cracks, under the same limits.
Flare angle decided
Set by the bevel. Bevel on: 45°, so the flare is the bottom edge’s bevel and the groove at a horizontal joint reads symmetric. Bevel off: 65°, as near level as the gate allows (rock is free in the 55–65° band), to keep as much stone as possible.
Exactness of chips and ridges
Option B finds them by splitting; close-ups may be a hair softer than today’s exact cuts. Option C is exact and much harder. Recommend B and judge by render.

Risks

  • Small resting places. The footprint-band prototype (23 Nov) found Fieldstone stones resting on 1–2 mm footprints, which an 0.8 mm band left with nothing. Bottom-up, only seen edges are bevelled (seen judged against the settled masses, so a top under a stone above is not seen), and lowering is done on masses, so a stone above does not lose its footprint to a bevel it cannot see. Watch Fieldstone’s count of stones resting nowhere.
  • Folds. Where a scoop eats a narrow face whole, its vertices land on the neighbour face; the mesh stays closed but can fold. The thickness clamp limits it and the ease-back undoes any triangle that turns over or faces down past 64.5°; what is left is a few mm² of hidden folds on Granite and Columns.
  • Speed. The surface pass runs on one thread, with no helpers: flake scars are convex cutters struck along the edges, grown from one another and laid in the bare patches of every seen face, each fitted to its room, and every cutter, scars and cracks alike, is taken out of its stone exactly, facet by facet, with no mesh lattice, marching or creases to find. Measured in the page with Flaking 0.5 to 1, it takes 0.66 to 1.0 s on Columns, Ashlar and Fieldstone, 1.3 to 1.4 s on Layered and 1.4 to 1.8 s on Granite, over the one-second mark on four walls (each flat face is now kept whole, a third of the triangles, with no repairs): a scar chips every stone it reaches, and no longer keeps off the cracks (they are cut over the flaking), so the cracked walls make a quarter to two thirds more scars (Granite seed 1: 2687).
  • The chain. Stage 3 is bottom-up, 49 levels on Fieldstone. It is about half a second today and stays so; stones in one level are independent if it ever needs threads.

  • Kinds: none added. A depth field is internal.
  • Second paths: removes one: support is decided once (stage 3), where today it is decided by holding and again by keeps during the carve, and scoops’ limits are one clamp instead of a dozen rules.
