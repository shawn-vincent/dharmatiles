Cliffs from Spines

A cliff as a population of rock masses. The envelope is cut into layers; spines are thrown in each, Width and Thickness apart; each grows until it meets its neighbours or the envelope. Every mass then becomes a faceted stone. Granite, Columns, Layered, Massive and Wall are settings of one engine; Random picks one of them.

How a cliff is built

  1. Ranges. Every setting drawn at random is a range: on its slider a handle under each end, the low end and the high end, and a grip above the band between them that moves the whole range (a number's slider is the same with one end); typed low..high (a number alone, 7, is the range 7..7: always that). Each draw is made between the ends, every value as likely, from a stream of its own for the job (a stone, a spine end, a layer face, a plane), so one setting never reshuffles another; Height and Depth are the exception, drawn mostly from a smooth field along the wall (below), so they wander rather than spread evenly.
  2. The envelope is the space the cliff may fill: here a wall on the tile, Squares long, as tall as the high end of Height and as deep as the high end of Depth, standing at the back of the square. Its bottom, its back and its two ends (the tile's edges) are crisp: no mass crosses them. Its top and its front are fuzzy: each stone draws how tall it reaches in Height and how far forward in Depth, two thirds from a smooth field along the wall and one third its own, so neighbours stop at similar places and the silhouette wanders instead of being noise.
  3. Spines run one Direction: Up (columns, or with many layers, courses), Through the wall from back to front (so the stones show their ends) or Along it (slabs). The envelope is cut across that direction into Layers, the outer faces of the first and last at the envelope's ends; each face where two layers meet is drawn on its own: it slopes along Width by an angle drawn in Lean and along Thickness by one drawn in Pitch, and curves along Width by a height drawn in Sag (its middle that far past its edges) and along Thickness by one drawn in Bow. Where neighbouring faces cross, a layer pinches out. Layers organise the spines and never cut a stone. In each layer in turn, spines are started by dart throwing: a dart is a point in the layer, its direction turned by an angle drawn in Wander, and its spine runs back to the layer's near face and on to its far face, and runs on into the next layer with the chance Span, drawn at each face it reaches, so breaks stagger. Its ends lie on layer faces inside the envelope's crisp sides (or the dart misses), except that past the fuzzy top or front a spine ends where its own stone stops. A dart is kept only if it is clear of every spine already in the layer by Width horizontally across the layer and Thickness the other way across it (vertically for Through and Along, front to back for Up), each the two ends' sizes together; until 80 darts in a row miss, the layer is not full. Order draws the darts' places from a grid instead, its pitch the middle of Width by the middle of Thickness, every other layer moved half a Width so the joints alternate as in a laid wall; below 100% each place is jittered. Each end's sizes are drawn in Width and Thickness.
  4. Two joinings, both chances: Span joins a spine to the next layer along the Direction, Fuse joins two neighbouring spines in one layer (their masses share a face) into one stone, one draw for each two from a stream of that pair's own, so changing one chance never reshuffles the other. A fused group is one body: its core is the hull of its members' spines, it runs through every layer they do, and a spine its core reaches round in a layer they share joins it, until nothing more joins. At 100% each layer is one stone. One spine, or one fused group, is one stone.
  5. Every stone grows until it meets its neighbours or the envelope. A stone's core is its spine, or for a fused group the convex hull of the middle halves of its members' spines (clear of the layer faces, which a whole layer's ends lie along). Between every two cores there is one split. Two that never share a layer (they meet end to end) split on the face between them (its tangent plane, where it curves; where a curved or pinched face would cut into one of the two cores, that plane is turned just enough to pass between them whole), so the layers order the stones. Two that share one split on the plane across the line between their nearest points, where their power distances are equal (the distance from each point less its radius there, squared; a fused group's radius is its members' mean), so a bigger end takes more of what it shares. That plane separates the two cores whole, and it leaves each at least 1.5 mm of its own. Joint, drawn for each split, sets where each side stops: half of it short of the split, so over zero a gap opens (separate stones), and under zero each reaches past it into its neighbour (they overlap). It changes each stone's size; nothing moves. A stone's mass is its envelope cut by its splits, nearest first: a convex cell. So with Joint at zero or more no two stones overlap, by construction.
  6. Rocks. Each mass becomes a stone, printable by construction. Its bottom is its mass's own faces that overhang past 65°: on a layer, the face of the layer below, level, tilted or curved as that is (one face for each stone below it); with Bedding, only the part of it that rests or is bridged (below); any other underside flatter than 65° from upright is cut away by a flare rising from the bottom at 65°, so the bottom is the only part that overhangs past the limit. A mass with no such face stands on its lowest point. Then every face of the mass keeps a small patch around a touch point (its plane turned by Tilt), so the stone fills its mass; Faces planes are spread over the faces by area, each its face's plane domed away from the touch point, turned by Tilt and sunk by up to Sink; then Chamfered of the edges' length and of the corners are cut, never past a touch point. The bottom is kept as it is, and no plane, crown, facet or chamfer is made that overhangs past 65°, so a little roughness under a stone stays in the printable range (between 55° and 65°: orange). Weathering rounds every outward edge. A rock stays inside its mass, so with Joint at zero or more rocks never overlap (the sink opens the joints between them), and under zero they overlap. Faces, Chamfered and Weathering are drawn for each stone, Tilt and Sink for each plane. Draw shows the rocks or the masses themselves (each in its own shade); the view options also show the layers and the spines.
  7. Bedding (on by default, under Rocks) builds each stone knowing what is below it, so it rests by construction and nothing is stretched or repaired afterwards. The order: a stone is finished only after every stone that holds it (whose mass lies under its mass's bottom); a circle, each partly over the next, is broken at the stone with the lowest bottom. The bottom: the points of its mass's bottom that rest (on a finished stone or the ground within mm, or inside one) and the points bridged between them, on a line between two resting points at most Bridge apart: the largest convex group of them seen from above with nothing inside that neither rests nor is bridged (cut back by a line through each such point inside it). The flare rises from it, from its corners and along each edge of its outline, so a stone over a gap is smaller rather than overhanging. The top: where a stone holds a mass above, its top stays on its plane under that mass's footprint but for a rim along the footprint's edges, of its width across each edge, where facets, crowns and chamfers may cut; so the stone above finds it within mm, and the two cut back into a V between them. A stone that rests nowhere keeps its whole bottom and is painted where nothing holds it. Off: each stone is its mass made printable alone, in no order: a view to compare with.
  8. Cracks (each style sets its own: Granite 35%, Columns 25% on a Columns network, Layered 30%, Massive 45% with wider openings, Wall 15%; under Rocks, whose sheet shows the other crack settings only while Cracks is above 0%) follow one hidden Network over the whole cliff: the cells of seeds on a grid in the cliff's own frame, in one, two or three of its axes: Planes across the Direction (bedding), Columns along it, or Blocks, spaced by the middle of Spacing, each seed moved within its spread. Where two cells meet is a flat boundary; where it crosses a stone's exposed faces is its trace, a line a crack can follow (a crack never runs from one stone into another). Cracks picks a share of the traces' length, and each stone the same share of its own: lines are taken in one fixed order, each time by the stone with the least share taken, which starts a crack at a line drawn by length while it has fewer than its share of the cracks it would have with every line taken (one per lines), and otherwise grows its shortest crack by the line at an end that turns least (taking a second as well with the chance Branching), or, its ends blocked, by a branch where an untaken line touches it; every draw has its own stream, so raising Cracks only adds: there are more cracks, and each grows longer. Only faces upright or facing up take cracks (not a bottom, nor any face facing down), and a face the boundary meets at less than ° (it would take off a skin) takes none. A picked line is cut into the stone as one groove of one V: two walls that meet in a sharp line, each ° off the face's inward normal, Opening apart at the face, so on an upright face its roof faces down at the overhang target whichever way the crack runs: printable by construction. Where it turns on a face (Wiggle), the two Vs meet on the turn's bisector, sharing one cross-section. Where it crosses an edge onto the next face, the next piece starts with exactly the last one's end cross-section (its two top points on the edge, its bottom point on the last bottom line) and twists back to the regular V over the shortest of a few steps (0.2 to 2 mm) whose walls all stay within the target; a face too narrow for a step is twisted whole, edge to edge. Where no twist fits (a sliver of a face, or a junction just past the edge), the two Vs meet flush on the plane halving the faces' angle. Its ends meet another crack (one within half its opening: a crack stays full through its junctions and branches), or the stone's visible outline (where it touches another stone or the ground, its bottom's edge included: it runs off it, flush), at full size (flush with the face beyond where that end wall is within the target, else rising to the face as the V does); at most three lines leave a junction (two corners of the network nearer than 0.75 mm are one, the hair of a line between them none; junctions joined by a line shorter than 3 mm count as one, since two T's that near read as an X), so cracks meet in a Y or a T, never a star or an X; a free end tapers to nothing, its depth falling 1 in (a branch with a free end too short for its taper, which would never open to its full width, is not cut) (the walls a hair past 35° so even a taper's roof stays within the target), and each stone's cracks are first settled as a graph, once, before any groove is cut, so the grooves keep these rules by construction: a crack has one width along its whole length, and cracks that join share it (the width drawn for the first of them picked); every end is one of three kinds: a junction, where it opens at full width into two others that are cut (where only two lines meet, they are one crack turning there, cut as one groove, its turn mitred on a face or joined across an edge as every crack's is) (a Y or a T: at most three arms, junctions joined by a line shorter than 3 mm counting as one, the arm picked last giving way); a joint, where it runs off at full width, flush, into the stone's outline where it touches another stone or the ground, or the edge of a face that holds another stone (so it never cuts under the stone above); or free, tapered to a point, the crack long enough to open to its full width first (a shorter one is not cut). A crack ends free either side of an edge it would cross nearly along (within 15°) and of a stretch whose groove would reach past the stone's bottom; nowhere else does it stop. A crack that comes out other than its graph says is listed under Cracks. The faces inside a crack are drawn dark grey (orange or red where they overhang, as everywhere). Each face the grooves touch is cut in its own plane into the convex pieces outside them (a face no groove touches stays whole). Cracks are planned on the masses, and each stone is cracked once every stone is built, its grooves kept clear of every point a stone above rests on, so cracking changes no stone's bottom; a weathered stone is not cracked. Each step of the build (a stone's bottom, the stone, its cracks, its paint) is kept by exactly what it reads, so a change remakes only what it reaches. Wiggle walks each line in steps mm long whose heading carries on from step to step, each turning it by up to its share of ° and keeping it within that share of the line (100%, the most), so a crack meanders; the walk is then pulled back so it ends where its line ends. Not here yet, from the proposal: erosion, pulling junctions to facet edges, seams between stones, and two networks at once.
  9. What rests or is bridged is not painted. Orange and red mark overhang past 55° and 65°, except where a downward face rests (on the ground, on a stone within mm below, or buried inside one) or is bridged (on a line between two of its stone's resting points at most Bridge apart), the rule a bottom is chosen by. A face not wholly resting is judged in triangles, split down to mm only where the answer changes (a weathered stone's faces, small already, whole).

Layered, the first style: nine level layers, a handful of slabs in each. Granite: one layer through the wall, so every spine starts at a point on the back and runs straight through to the front (turned about 10°), and the stones show their ends; Joint under zero, so they overlap into one irregular mass. Columns: one layer, the full height, so every spine is one upright column from a point on the ground, a hair apart. Massive: two layers parallel to the front, big stones. Wall: eight level courses, Order 100%, so each course is a row of stones a Width apart, every other course moved half a stone.

Not here yet, from the design: envelopes with concavities and holes (each mass is clipped by a box envelope), curved or irregular ends, twisting sections, and joint faces kept flush (a rock's sink opens every joint). Every job draws from its own stream (the planes, each plane's starts, each spine, each end, the fuzz, each joint, each rock), so one control never reshuffles another. Overhang paints orange past 55° and red past 65°.
