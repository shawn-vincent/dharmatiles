Cliffs from Spines

A cliff as a population of stones. The envelope is stacked in layers, each as thick as Reach; spines are thrown in each, Width and Thickness apart, and each spine is one convex stone, cut flat where it starts and stops and halfway between it and each neighbouring spine. Every cell is carved on its own into a faceted rock that rests on what holds it, and cracks follow one hidden network. Granite, Columns, Layered, Fieldstone and Ashlar are settings of one engine; Random picks one of them.

How a cliff is built

  1. Ranges. Every setting drawn at random is a range: on its slider a handle under each end, the low end and the high end, and a grip above the band between them that moves the whole range (a number’s slider is the same with one end); typed low..high (a number alone, 7, is the range 7..7: always that). Each draw is made between the ends, every value as likely, from a stream of its own for the job (a stone, a spine, a layer face, a joint, a plane), so one setting never reshuffles another; Height and Depth are the exception, drawn mostly from a smooth field along the wall (below), so they wander rather than spread evenly.
  2. The envelope is the space the cliff may fill: here a wall on the tile, as long as the high end of Length, as tall as the high end of Height and as deep as the high end of Depth, standing at the back of the square. Its bottom and its back are crisp: no stone crosses them, and when Length is one value its two ends (the tile’s edges) are crisp too. Its top, its front and, when Length is a range, its two ends are fuzzy: each spine draws how tall it reaches in Height, how far forward in Depth and how far along the wall each end reaches in Length (each end from a stream of its own), two thirds from a smooth field along the wall (along each end) and one third its own, so neighbours stop at similar places and the silhouette wanders instead of being noise. The Structure view always draws it: a crisp core (edges and a faint fill), the box every stone fills for sure, up to the low end of Height, forward to the low end of Depth and as long as the low end of Length, and round its top, front and ends a paler band that fades outward to a dashed outline at the high ends, where stones may or may not reach (a face whose range is one value has no band).
  3. Layers and spines. Every Direction is built the same way, only turned: the spines run up (Up), from the back to the front (Through) or along the wall (Along), and the envelope is stacked with layers across them. The first face is flat: the ground (Up), the back (Through) or the left end of the envelope (Along). Each face after it is the face below it moved on along the Direction by a smooth random amount in Reach, everywhere: the amount is a smooth field drawn from a stream of the face’s own, its features spine spacings across (the middle of Width and Thickness together), so a layer is as thick as Reach at every place, thickening and thinning smoothly, and faces never cross. Faces are thrown until one lies at the envelope’s end, which cuts the last layer; a Reach as long as the envelope is one layer. In each layer in turn spines are started by dart throwing: a dart is a point in the layer, its direction turned by an angle drawn in Wander; it is kept only if it is clear of every spine already in the layer by Width across the wall and Thickness front to back (each the two ends’ sizes together), and the layer is full when 80 darts in a row miss. A spine runs on into the next layer with the chance Span, drawn at each face it reaches, so breaks stagger. A spine is the plan of its stone: it ends on the layer face or, where that comes first, on the fuzzy reach of the envelope (the top drawn in Height, the front in Depth, the ends in Length), and the stone is built up to the spine’s end, its cap the plane the spine stops on. Order draws the darts’ places from a grid instead, its pitch the middle of Width by the middle of Thickness, every other layer moved half a Width so the joints alternate as in a laid wall; below 100% each place is moved at random by up to half of (100% − Order) of a spacing along each axis, and at 0% there is no grid: the darts are thrown at random. A layer whose spines are at least Widths long (a column) gives all its spines one lean along the wall, drawn in Wander, so neighbours stay parallel and none narrows against another.
  4. Cells. Each spine is one convex stone, from the layer face it starts on to the one it stops at (each taken as its tangent plane at the spine there). Between it and each spine that shares a layer with it, the stone is cut by the plane halfway between the two spines’ closest points (measured with Thickness against Width as the proportion, a stretch of the whole wall, so planes stay planes), then by the envelope. The planes are drawn pair by pair and do not have to agree, so some space can be left to no stone. Width and Thickness space the spines; they do not move the planes. Joint, drawn for each pair, moves each side half of it from the halfway plane: over zero a gap opens (separate stones), under zero each reaches past it into its neighbour (they overlap). The cells are not a chain: every contact is known before any stone is carved, so every stone is carved on its own.
  5. Bodies. A spine that runs through several layers is one stone, carved whole. Fuse joins two neighbouring spines whose cells share a face into one body, one draw for each two from a stream of the pair’s own; a cell narrower than 3 mm across joins the neighbour it shares the most side face with. A body is carved cell by cell with the faces where its cells meet kept flat, so the slicer fuses them into one stone; a body that rests on nothing the ground holds is joined to a touching body that is held.
  6. The bottom. The stones are placed holders first (a stone can hold another only when its spine ends below the middle of the other’s and starts lower, so a neighbour beside it never comes first and one it overlaps deeply does), and each in turn finds what holds it among the stones placed before it, as they stand. Its bottom reaches down to what is under it: it is lowered, kept parallel, until it lies in every top under it within mm (or on the plate, when the plate is that near), measured at the corners of its outline and every mm inside it, so where its own plane and the tops under it part, it sinks into them. The stones print as one solid, so where they overlap nothing hangs. Then the stone is held wherever another stone (or the plate) lies just under its surface. Each face that looks down, a side face too, is clipped by each stone placed before it as that stone stands, and a holder keeps those places from carving. A flare keeps the cone over the held places: all that lies within ° of upright above one (for each direction in plan, the plane at that angle through the held place farthest out). What is left of a face past ° that nothing holds would hang, and is cut off by the plane at ° that touches it from inside (of the directions within 45° of the way out from the held places, the one that keeps the most held surface), the largest first. Every plane that cuts a stone faces down less than °, and a convex stone has no point of a face past ° within ° above another of its points, so once those pieces are cut off every face of the stone past ° is held. A stone held nowhere is cut away whole. Where a stone holds another, its top keeps flat the place the stone above rests on most. Every stone rests on the ground or on a stone that does.
  7. Rocks. Each cell is carved alone, by the same rules whatever the style: the stone is its cell, and every edge that will be seen (two faces turning at least °, the edge not buried in another stone, and a point just outside it in no other stone: overlapping stones whose faces run flush meet flat, with no groove) gets one flat bevel, cut the same distance in on both faces, so at the middle angle between them: Bevel wide (drawn between its two ends at each end of the edge), at most of the narrower face’s inradius, longest edges first. The bottom, the flat tops under stones above, and the faces where a body’s cells meet are kept as they are; no plane is made that faces down past °, so every overhang left is the bottom’s own, and a bevel that would cut what is kept is moved out to it, or not made if less than a third of a nozzle is left. A stone stays inside its cell; Joint is at most −0.2 mm, so neighbouring stones always reach into each other. The stones are left as separate shells, not joined: the slicer fuses them. Draw shows the wall three ways. Finished is the stones as they print: a natural grey that runs a little warm, each stone its own, lit like the rest, the cracks dark and the overhang painted orange (55 to 65°) or red (past 65°), with no outlines. Rocks is the same stones for debugging: each in its own vivid colour, every edge outlined, the bevels paler, the crack network drawn (the lines that became cracks solid, the lines a crack could have followed and did not dashed and fainter) and the overhang painted. Structure is the cells as cut, before carving, each body its own shade, as frosted glass, with the spines, the layer faces and the envelope. A tap selects a stone (a mass, in Structure), outlined in yellow, with its spine in Structure, and opens its page in the sheet (Parts, then the stone: its body, layer and size, what it rests on and what it holds up, as links to those stones); in Finished and Rocks a tap near an edge selects that edge too, drawn in cyan with its report on the stone’s page, and the selection stays when the view changes. A tap on empty space clears it, and so does closing the sheet; the mosaic button opens the sheet at its top level. Round cuts each stone by an egg of its own shape as the stone is placed, before it is held, just holding the stone at 0% and just inside it at 100%, so corners go first, then edges, then the faces dome. Where it meets another stone (its bottom, its sides against its neighbours, the places on its top the stones above meet) it keeps the meeting but the rim, drawn in by the bevel’s width, and a ground stone keeps its whole bottom; the stones on either side give up the same rim, and where a stone above then reaches past a rounded rim, holding cuts that part off at the overhang target. So the wall’s back and ends round as its front does, and the joints open into grooves, never through. Weathering rounds each seen convex edge to an arc of the drawn radius, after the bevels (so a bevel is worn, its two edges rounded, not lost): the stone a ball of that radius rolls around inside, made of planes tangent to it, turning in steps under ° so the arc reads as a curve and is never bevelled again. Flat faces stay flat. Each plane obeys the same rule as a bevel’s: none faces down past ° (an arc on an underside stops there), and none cuts what a stone rests on or carries (a facet such a place holds back pivots on it and tapers, as a bevel does); stones are carved from the top down, so what a stone above rests on or is held by is kept only where that stone, as carved, still has an underside past 55°; so the stone stays convex, held and printable by construction, and the cracks are cut into it after.
  8. Cracks (each style sets its own: Granite 12.5% on a Blocks network, Columns 19.5% on a Columns network, Layered 90% on a Planes network, Fieldstone none, Ashlar 15%; under Rocks, whose page in the settings sheet shows the other crack settings only while Cracks is above 0%) follow one hidden Network over the whole cliff: a table of flat boundaries between cells of seeds on a grid, in one, two or three axes: Planes across the vertical (bedding), Columns along it, or Blocks, spaced by the middle of Spacing, each seed moved within its spread, and each boundary carrying the rank at which a crack would reach it (cracks start at even shares and grow from line to line, turning least, branching with the chance Branching, lines to a crack on average). The table is made once for each Network, Spacing and Branching (the first wall that needs one waits for it) and the wall takes it at a seeded offset. Where a boundary crosses a stone’s upright or upward faces it is a trace, and it is cut where those faces are the wall’s outside (front, top, back and ends alike) or bevels, not where one stone meets another; Cracks picks each stone’s own share of its traces’ length, in rank order, so raising it only adds cracks, and a crack never runs from one stone into another. A boundary a face meets at less than ° (it would take off a skin) takes none. A trace is walked in short steps (Wiggle: each step mm, its heading turned by up to this share of ° and kept within the share, so a crack meanders), and cut as a groove of one V: two walls that meet in a sharp line, each ° off the face’s inward normal, Opening apart at the face (at most mm), so on an upright face its roof faces down at the overhang target whichever way the crack runs, and a wall that would face down past it is turned up to it. A free end tapers to nothing, its depth falling 1 in ; an end that meets another crack, or the stone’s outline where it touches another stone or the ground, runs off at full size; and a stretch that holds a stone (where it rests, or wherever a face of it that looks down lies on this one) or lies on a bottom is left whole. Each face the grooves touch is cut in its own plane into the convex pieces outside them, and merged back to as few as keep it convex. The faces inside a crack are drawn dark grey.
  9. Overhang is painted after the wall is shown. The wall appears as soon as it is built; a pass over the finished stones then marks every downward face past ° that is not held, and the page paints it orange (55 to 65°) or red (past 65°), and reports each share of the downward area and the area unsupported. A downward face is held where another stone lies under it (within 0.2 mm), or on a line between two held places of its own stone at most Bridge apart; the ground holds what touches it. A face is painted whole when any of it is unheld. Each of orange and red may be 0.1% of the downward area. Red and orange on a model mean overhang only; the inside of a crack is dark grey.

Layered: thin layers (Reach 1.4 to 2.4 mm), a handful of slabs in each, the joints staggered, with cracks along Planes. Granite: Direction Through, one layer (Reach longer than the wall is deep): every spine runs from the back to the front, and the stones show their ends; Joint under zero, so they overlap into one irregular mass; cracks along Blocks. Columns: one layer (Reach longer than the wall is tall), the full height, so every spine is one upright column from a point on the ground, a hair apart; cracks along Columns. Fieldstone: rough courses (Reach 2.8 to 4.8 mm), the stones thrown at random (Order 0%), many-faced and tilted so they read round; Span 30% lets a stone run up through the next course; no cracks. Ashlar: courses of one Reach (8.6 mm), level, Order 100%, so each course is a row of stones a Width apart, every other course moved half a stone; cracks along Blocks.

The settings code is the colour mosaic that fills the button at the bottom right of the view, a sliders icon on a plate in the middle (the code with the camera, in eight muted colours of the day or night theme and error correction, for a screenshot to carry; the page's decoder reads it back whatever the colours), and opens the sheet, Wall: a row for the style, the seed and each group, each opening its page (a line for each setting: its name, which opens the setting’s own page with its range and its help, and its control beside it), then Parts, the stones the generator made (a search over the rows as they read, and a row for each stone: its colour, number, layer and what it rests on; a tap opens the stone’s page), and under them the code as text (with the camera) to copy, paste into or edit, and as a large QR. One code holds every setting that differs from its style, the seed and optionally the view; its last character checks it, and a code made by another version of this page is refused.

Not here yet: envelopes with concavities and holes (each cell is clipped by a box envelope), curved or irregular ends, twisting sections, erosion, seams between stones, and two networks at once. A cell is a convex region, so a stone is convex before its cracks.
