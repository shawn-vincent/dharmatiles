Cliffs from Spines

A cliff as a population of rock masses. Control planes sweep the space; a spine is drawn from one plane to a later one; each spine claims the space around it, bounded by its own size and ends, split with its neighbours by weight. Every mass is then a hull for Rocks from Hulls to turn into faceted rock. Granite wall, Columns, Layered and Massive are settings of one engine.

How a cliff is built

  1. The envelope is the space the cliff may fill: here a wall on the tile, Squares long, Height tall and Depth deep, standing at the back of the square. Its bottom, its back and its two ends (the tile's edges) are crisp: no mass crosses them. Its top and its front are fuzzy: each mass draws how far short of them it stops, up to Fuzz, from a smooth field along the wall (two thirds) and its own draw (one third), so neighbours stop at similar places and the silhouette wanders instead of being noise.
  2. Control planes, Count of them, sweep the envelope from one side to the other: Plane tilt 0° makes them level (sweeping up), 90° upright; Plane turn faces an upright plane across the wall (0°, sweeping along it) or along it (90°, sweeping from back to front). Each inner plane is moved and turned at random by up to Wobble. The planes organise the spines; they never cut a rock.
  3. Spines. In each interval between two planes in turn, spines are started by dart throwing: a dart is a point of the envelope between the two planes, its direction the sweep's turned at random by about Spread, and its spine runs back to the first plane and on to the next, and on through each further plane with chance Span; its ends lie on its planes, inside the envelope's crisp sides (or the dart misses), except that past the fuzzy top or front a spine ends where its own mass stops. A dart is kept only if it is clear of every spine already in the interval (at least Packing times their two radii apart, measured across their sections); a spine that only ends at the interval's plane does not count, so masses can stack end to end. Until 80 darts in a row miss, the interval is not full. Strata holds spines to shared strata: the wall's height is cut into bands about a mass thick, a start is pulled toward its band's middle, the spine runs flatter, and its thickness becomes the band's. One spine is one mass.
  4. Each spine's support is the space it may claim: the convex hull of its two end sections (a polygon of about Faces sides, Size times Reach across, flattened by Aspect, each end drawn on its own within Vary), so a mass can taper, flatten and change its sides from end to end. At the top of Reach there is no limit: the support is the whole envelope, and a mass grows until it meets its neighbours (its ends too: the ends below shape only limited masses). Each end lies on its control plane (or at its mass's fuzzy edge), turned by up to End tilt, rounded by a ring of chamfers Round deep, and roughened (every plane of the end turned and moved) by Rough. Weight, drawn at each end within Weight, is how hard a spine competes.
  5. Space is split by weight. Each spine scores a point by its power distance: the square of the distance across its section (and along it, past an end) less the square of its local size times weight; the lower score claims it. Two such scores are equal on a plane (exactly, for points), so where two supports meet, their overlap is split by the plane fitted (least squares) to where the scores are equal, and the planes of three masses meet in one line, leaving no hole. Spacing then sets where each side stops: half of it short of the split, so over zero a gap opens (separate stones, heaped), and under zero each reaches past the split into its neighbour (they read as one mass). It changes each mass's size; nothing moves. Each split adds its own random part, up to Spacing's Vary either way. A mass is its support and envelope (its hull) less its neighbours' claims, each claim the neighbour's support on its own side of the split (a convex cut, so the mass is a hull with hollows, as Rocks from Hulls reads it). A point both supports reach goes to exactly one side, so with Spacing at zero or more no two masses overlap, by construction, and only the shared overlap is split, so nothing either could not reach is lost. A mass must be one piece: it is read on a 1 mm grid, and while it is not, the claim cutting the most becomes a half-space instead (the side holding the spine is kept). Space no support reaches stays empty: gaps are allowed. Every spine keeps a mass: a split leaves each spine at least 1.5 mm of its own (and half of a gap); where the fitted plane cannot, the split is across the line between the spines' nearest points. So every spine is a mass, and every mass a stone.
  6. Bedding (on by default, under Rocks): a stone whose underside overhangs past 55° is made to stand on something, at construction. Stones are bedded from the lowest up, each on what is already there. A small gap (a face's drop to what is below, up to 2 mm) is closed by stretching the stone down, its top held; stretching also turns every face toward upright, so some faces need no drop at all. A stone with faces further off gets one corbel (as the cliff element's support cone): the hull of the points of those faces and their sweep along a direction within 55° of straight down, the one that reaches a bedded stone or the ground soonest for all of them, until all have run into it. Each wall of the corbel is parallel to the sweep, so it leans no more than 55°, or a copy of the underside, the lower one inside what it reaches. So a cantilever is held up by the stone beside or below it instead of a column to the ground, and the corbel holds up the stones above in turn.
  7. Rocks. Each mass is a hull for Rocks from Hulls, with its neighbours' claims as hollows: Rocks sets its faces, chamfers, tilt, sink and weathering. A rock stays inside its mass, so with Spacing at zero or more rocks never overlap (the sink opens the joints between them), and under zero they overlap and merge. Draw shows the rocks or the masses themselves (each in its own shade); the view options also show the control planes and the spines.

Granite wall, the first style: two planes, the back and the front of the wall, so every spine starts at a point on the back and runs straight through to the front (turned about 10°), and the stones show their ends; no limit on reach and Spacing under zero, so they fill the wall and merge into one irregular mass. Columns: two planes, the ground and the top, so every spine is one upright column from a point on the ground, no limit on reach, a hair apart.

Not here yet, from the design: envelopes with concavities and holes (each mass is clipped by a box envelope), ends curved or irregular beyond what planes make (Round is a ring of chamfers), twisting sections, and joint faces kept flush (a rock's sink opens every joint). Every job draws from its own stream (the planes, each plane's starts, each spine, each end, the fuzz, the strata, each rock), so one control never reshuffles another. Overhang paints orange past 55° and red past 65°.
