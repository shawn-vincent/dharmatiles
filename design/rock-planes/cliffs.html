Cliffs from Spines

A cliff as a population of rock masses. The envelope is cut into layers; spines are thrown in each, Width and Thickness apart; each grows until it meets its neighbours or the envelope. Every mass is then a hull for Rocks from Hulls to turn into faceted rock. Granite wall, Columns, Layered and Massive are settings of one engine.

How a cliff is built

  1. The envelope is the space the cliff may fill: here a wall on the tile, Squares long, Height tall and Depth deep, standing at the back of the square. Its bottom, its back and its two ends (the tile's edges) are crisp: no mass crosses them. Its top and its front are fuzzy: each mass draws how far short of them it stops, up to Fuzz, from a smooth field along the wall (two thirds) and its own draw (one third), so neighbours stop at similar places and the silhouette wanders instead of being noise.
  2. Spines run one Direction: Through the wall from back to front (so the stones show their ends), Along it (slabs) or Up (columns, or with many layers, courses). The envelope is cut across that direction into Layers, the outer faces of the first and last at the envelope's ends; where two layers meet is turned and moved at random by up to half of Wander. Layers organise the spines and never cut a stone. In each layer in turn, spines are started by dart throwing: a dart is a point in the layer, its direction turned at random by up to Wander, and its spine runs back to the layer's near face and on to its far face, and on through each further layer with chance Span, so breaks stagger. Its ends lie on layer faces inside the envelope's crisp sides (or the dart misses), except that past the fuzzy top or front a spine ends where its own stone stops. A dart is kept only if it is clear of every spine already in the layer by Width horizontally across the layer and Thickness the other way across it (vertically for Through and Along, front to back for Up), each the two ends' sizes together; until 80 darts in a row miss, the layer is not full. Order draws the darts' places from a grid instead, Width by Thickness across the layer, every other layer moved half a Width so the joints alternate as in a laid wall; below 100% each place is jittered. Each end's size varies within Vary. One spine is one stone.
  3. Every stone grows until it meets its neighbours or the envelope. Between every two spines there is one split. Two that never share a layer (they meet end to end) split where two layers between them meet, so the layers order the stones. Two that share one split on the plane across the line between their nearest points, where their power distances are equal (the distance from each point less its radius there, squared), so a bigger end takes more of what it shares. That plane separates the two spines whole, and it leaves each at least 1.5 mm of its own. Joint sets where each side stops: half of it short of the split, so over zero a gap opens (separate stones), and under zero each reaches past it into its neighbour (they merge); each split adds a random part that grows with Vary. It changes each stone's size; nothing moves. A stone's mass is its envelope cut by its splits, nearest first: a convex cell. So with Joint at zero or more no two stones overlap, by construction, and every spine is a stone.
  4. Bedding (on by default, under Rocks): a stone whose underside overhangs past 65° (red; orange is fine) is made to stand on something, at construction, and never leaves its envelope. Stones are bedded from the lowest up, each on what is already there. A stone near the ground (within 2 mm) gets a foot: its lowest 1.5 mm (or up to the top of any overhanging face that starts there, such as a chamfer along its ground edge, at most 6 mm) extended straight down to the ground, so its ground edges are sharp (no chamfer under it) and its bottom is flat on the ground. A small gap (up to 2 mm) is closed by stretching the stone down by the median of its red points' gaps to what is that near below them, so it sits on what is near without sinking into it, its top held; stretching also turns every face toward upright, so some faces need no drop at all (it holds the stone's footprint, so it stays in the envelope). Points further off get corbels (as the cliff element's support cone): a corbel is the hull of some loose points and their ends, each point swept along one direction within 55° of straight down until it meets a bedded stone or the ground, so each wall is parallel to the sweep (leaning no more than 55°) or rests on what the points reach. A corbel lands on one thing, the ground or one stone, so its bottom rests on it and never hangs between two. A stone gets up to six, each taking the direction and the landing that hold up the most of the points still loose: straight down under a slab (bridging a joint below), slanted back under a lip. A corbel is short: a point runs no further than its stone is tall (at least 4 mm, at most 10 mm), and never out of its stone's envelope, which also cuts every corbel; a point nothing holds within that is left as it is, since a long strut across the face reads as a sliver, not a stone. So a cantilever is held up by the stone beside or below it instead of a column to the ground, and the corbel holds up the stones above in turn.
  5. Held faces are not painted. Orange and red mark overhang past 55° and 65°, except where a face is held up: a face on the ground, or a face buried inside a foot, a corbel or another stone, or a bridge: a downward face is split into triangles, and a triangle with at least two corners resting on something (a stone, a corbel or the ground, within 0.15 mm below or inside it) spans between them.
  6. Rocks. Each mass becomes a stone: every face of the mass keeps a small patch around a touch point (its plane turned by Tilt), so the stone fills its mass; Faces planes are spread over the faces by area, each its face's plane domed away from the touch point, turned by Tilt and sunk by up to Sink; then Chamfered of the edges' length and of the corners are cut, never past a touch point. Weathering rounds every outward edge. A rock stays inside its mass, so with Joint at zero or more rocks never overlap (the sink opens the joints between them), and under zero they overlap and merge. Draw shows the rocks or the masses themselves (each in its own shade); the view options also show the layers and the spines.

Granite wall, the first style: one layer through the wall, so every spine starts at a point on the back and runs straight through to the front (turned about 10°), and the stones show their ends; Joint under zero, so they merge into one irregular mass. Columns: one layer, the full height, so every spine is one upright column from a point on the ground, a hair apart. Layered: nine level layers, a handful of slabs in each. Massive: two layers parallel to the front, big stones. Stone wall: eight level courses, Order 100%, so each course is a row of stones a Width apart, every other course moved half a stone.

Not here yet, from the design: envelopes with concavities and holes (each mass is clipped by a box envelope), curved or irregular ends, twisting sections, and joint faces kept flush (a rock's sink opens every joint). Every job draws from its own stream (the planes, each plane's starts, each spine, each end, the fuzz, each joint, each rock), so one control never reshuffles another. Overhang paints orange past 55° and red past 65°.
