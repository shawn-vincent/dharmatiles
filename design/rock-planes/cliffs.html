Cliffs from Spines

A cliff as a population of stones. The envelope is cut into layers; spines are thrown in each, Width and Thickness apart, and each spine is one convex stone, cut flat where it starts and stops and halfway between it and each neighbouring spine. Every cell is carved on its own into a faceted rock that rests on what holds it, and cracks follow one hidden network. Granite, Columns, Layered, Fieldstone and Ashlar are settings of one engine; Random picks one of them.

How a cliff is built

  1. Ranges. Every setting drawn at random is a range: on its slider a handle under each end, the low end and the high end, and a grip above the band between them that moves the whole range (a number’s slider is the same with one end); typed low..high (a number alone, 7, is the range 7..7: always that). Each draw is made between the ends, every value as likely, from a stream of its own for the job (a stone, a spine, a layer face, a joint, a plane), so one setting never reshuffles another; Height and Depth are the exception, drawn mostly from a smooth field along the wall (below), so they wander rather than spread evenly.
  2. The envelope is the space the cliff may fill: here a wall on the tile, Squares long, as tall as the high end of Height and as deep as the high end of Depth, standing at the back of the square. Its bottom, its back and its two ends (the tile’s edges) are crisp: no stone crosses them. Its top and its front are fuzzy: each spine draws how tall it reaches in Height and how far forward in Depth, two thirds from a smooth field along the wall and one third its own, so neighbours stop at similar places and the silhouette wanders instead of being noise.
  3. Layers and spines. Every Direction is built the same way, only turned: the spines run up (Up), from the back to the front (Through) or along the wall (Along), and the envelope is cut across them into Layers. Each face where two layers meet is drawn on its own: it slopes along Width by an angle drawn in Lean and along Thickness by one drawn in Pitch, and curves along Width by a height drawn in Sag (its middle that far past its edges) and along Thickness by one drawn in Bow. A face never lies under the face below it, so where two cross, a layer pinches out. In each layer in turn spines are started by dart throwing: a dart is a point in the layer, its direction turned by an angle drawn in Wander; it is kept only if it is clear of every spine already in the layer by Width across the wall and Thickness front to back (each the two ends’ sizes together), and the layer is full when 80 darts in a row miss. A spine runs on into the next layer with the chance Span, drawn at each face it reaches, so breaks stagger. Order draws the darts’ places from a grid instead, its pitch the middle of Width by the middle of Thickness, every other layer moved half a Width so the joints alternate as in a laid wall; below 100% each place is moved at random by up to half of (100% − Order) of a spacing along each axis, and at 0% there is no grid: the darts are thrown at random. A layer whose spines are at least Widths long (a column) gives all its spines one lean along the wall, drawn in Wander, so neighbours stay parallel and none narrows against another.
  4. Cells. Each spine is one convex stone, from the layer face it starts on to the one it stops at (each taken as its tangent plane at the spine there, so Sag and Bow become planes). Between it and each spine that shares a layer with it, the stone is cut by the plane halfway between the two spines’ closest points (measured with Thickness against Width as the proportion, a stretch of the whole wall, so planes stay planes), then by the envelope. The planes are drawn pair by pair and do not have to agree, so some space can be left to no stone. Width and Thickness space the spines; they do not move the planes. Joint, drawn for each pair, moves each side half of it from the halfway plane: over zero a gap opens (separate stones), under zero each reaches past it into its neighbour (they overlap). The cells are not a chain: every contact is known before any stone is carved, so every stone is carved on its own.
  5. Bodies. A spine that runs through several layers is one stone, carved whole. Fuse joins two neighbouring spines whose cells share a face into one body, one draw for each two from a stream of the pair’s own; a cell narrower than 3 mm across joins the neighbour it shares the most side face with. A body is carved cell by cell with the faces where its cells meet kept flat, so the slicer fuses them into one stone; a body that rests on nothing the ground holds is joined to a touching body that is held.
  6. The bottom. A cell finds what holds it before it is carved: the places where its bottom meets the tops of the cells below (any stone placed before it: the stones are placed holders first, and a stone can hold another only when its spine ends below the middle of the other’s and starts lower, so a neighbour beside it never holds it and one it overlaps deeply does), each made a patch. Each patch is inset by the bevel’s width (the high end of Bevel), so a stone rests on what its bevels leave; an overlap too small to keep mm² that way is shrunk about its centre to mm² instead, and a stone keeps the whole overlap with a stone of its own body. In a thin layer (under mm: Layered) every patch is kept while the patches and the hulls of pairs within Bridge cover their whole hull. Its bottom is those patches, and every point of it either rests on one or lies on a line between two patches at most Bridge apart (less mm, so the plan sits inside the measure). A bottom the patches cannot hold whole is made of the largest group of patches whose hull is covered, or in a thin layer cut where it would hang, along the line that keeps the most; else it keeps the best pair, or the largest patch. A stone with no holder is lowered to the nearest top below, or stands on the ground when it is near the plate. A flare rises from the bottom’s outline at ° from upright, so what lies past a patch is cut away rather than overhanging. Where a stone holds another, its top keeps flat the hull of the patches the stones above rest on, and its fuzzy top is never below its holders’ tops. Every stone rests on the ground or on a stone that does.
  7. Rocks. Each cell is carved alone, by the same rules whatever the style: the stone is its cell, and every edge that will be seen (two faces turning at least °, a point just outside it in no other stone) gets one flat bevel, cut the same distance in on both faces, so at the middle angle between them: Bevel wide (drawn between its two ends at each end of the edge), at most of the narrower face’s inradius, longest edges first. The bottom, the flat tops under stones above, and the faces where a body’s cells meet are kept as they are; no plane is made that faces down past °, so every overhang left is the bottom’s own, and a bevel that would cut what is kept is moved out to it, or not made if less than a third of a nozzle is left. A stone stays inside its cell, so with Joint at zero or more stones never overlap and under zero they overlap. The stones are left as separate shells, not joined: the slicer fuses them. Draw shows the rocks or the masses (the cells as cut, before carving, each body its own shade); the view options also show the layers and the spines. Weathering then cuts each stone by an egg of its own shape, just holding the stone at 0% and just inside it at 100%, so corners go first, then edges, then the faces dome; what a stone rests on or carries, and the downward limit, hold it back.
  8. Cracks (each style sets its own: Granite 12.5% on a Blocks network, Columns 19.5% on a Columns network, Layered 90% on a Planes network, Fieldstone none, Ashlar 15%; under Rocks, whose sheet shows the other crack settings only while Cracks is above 0%) follow one hidden Network over the whole cliff: a table of flat boundaries between cells of seeds on a grid, in one, two or three axes: Planes across the vertical (bedding), Columns along it, or Blocks, spaced by the middle of Spacing, each seed moved within its spread, and each boundary carrying the rank at which a crack would reach it (cracks start at even shares and grow from line to line, turning least, branching with the chance Branching, lines to a crack on average). The table is made once for each Network, Spacing and Branching (the first wall that needs one waits for it) and the wall takes it at a seeded offset. Where a boundary crosses a stone’s upright or upward faces it is a trace; Cracks picks each stone’s own share of its traces’ length, in rank order, so raising it only adds cracks, and a crack never runs from one stone into another. A boundary a face meets at less than ° (it would take off a skin) takes none. A trace is walked in short steps (Wiggle: each step mm, its heading turned by up to this share of ° and kept within the share, so a crack meanders), and cut as a groove of one V: two walls that meet in a sharp line, each ° off the face’s inward normal, Opening apart at the face (at most mm), so on an upright face its roof faces down at the overhang target whichever way the crack runs, and a wall that would face down past it is turned up to it. A free end tapers to nothing, its depth falling 1 in ; an end that meets another crack, or the stone’s outline where it touches another stone or the ground, runs off at full size; and a stretch that holds a stone above or lies on a bottom is left whole. Each face the grooves touch is cut in its own plane into the convex pieces outside them, and merged back to as few as keep it convex. The faces inside a crack are drawn dark grey.
  9. Overhang is painted after the wall is shown. The wall appears as soon as it is built; a pass over the finished stones then marks every downward face past ° that is not held, and the page paints it orange (55 to 65°) or red (past 65°), and reports each share of the downward area and the area unsupported. A downward face is held where another stone lies under it (within 0.2 mm), or on a line between two held places of its own stone at most Bridge apart; the ground holds what touches it. A face is painted whole when any of it is unheld. Each of orange and red may be 0.1% of the downward area. Red and orange on a model mean overhang only; the inside of a crack is dark grey.

Layered: thirty-eight thin level layers, a handful of slabs in each, the joints staggered, with cracks along Planes. Granite: Direction Through, one layer: every spine runs from the back to the front, and the stones show their ends; Joint under zero, so they overlap into one irregular mass; cracks along Blocks. Columns: one layer, the full height, so every spine is one upright column from a point on the ground, a hair apart; cracks along Columns. Fieldstone: eighteen rough courses, the stones thrown at random (Order 0%), many-faced and tilted so they read round; Span 30% lets a stone run up through the next course; no cracks. Ashlar: eight level courses, Order 100%, so each course is a row of stones a Width apart, every other course moved half a stone; cracks along Blocks.

Not here yet: envelopes with concavities and holes (each cell is clipped by a box envelope), curved or irregular ends, twisting sections, erosion, seams between stones, and two networks at once. A cell is a convex region, so a stone is convex before its cracks.
