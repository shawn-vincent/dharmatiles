Cliffs from Spines

A cliff as a population of rock masses. The envelope is cut into layers; spines are thrown in each, Width and Thickness apart; each grows until it meets its neighbours or the envelope. Every mass then becomes a faceted stone. Granite, Columns, Layered, Massive and Wall are settings of one engine; Random picks one of them.

How a cliff is built

  1. Ranges. Every setting drawn at random is a range: two handles on one track, the low end and the high end, typed low..high (a number alone, 7, is the range 7..7: always that). Each draw is made between the ends, every value as likely, from a stream of its own for the job (a stone, a spine end, a layer face, a plane), so one setting never reshuffles another; Height and Depth are the exception, drawn mostly from a smooth field along the wall (below), so they wander rather than spread evenly.
  2. The envelope is the space the cliff may fill: here a wall on the tile, Squares long, as tall as the high end of Height and as deep as the high end of Depth, standing at the back of the square. Its bottom, its back and its two ends (the tile's edges) are crisp: no mass crosses them. Its top and its front are fuzzy: each stone draws how tall it reaches in Height and how far forward in Depth, two thirds from a smooth field along the wall and one third its own, so neighbours stop at similar places and the silhouette wanders instead of being noise.
  3. Spines run one Direction: Up (columns, or with many layers, courses), Through the wall from back to front (so the stones show their ends) or Along it (slabs). The envelope is cut across that direction into Layers, the outer faces of the first and last at the envelope's ends; each face where two layers meet is drawn on its own: it slopes along Width by an angle drawn in Lean and along Thickness by one drawn in Pitch, and curves along Width by a height drawn in Sag (its middle that far past its edges) and along Thickness by one drawn in Bow. Where neighbouring faces cross, a layer pinches out. Layers organise the spines and never cut a stone. In each layer in turn, spines are started by dart throwing: a dart is a point in the layer, its direction turned by an angle drawn in Wander, and its spine runs back to the layer's near face and on to its far face, through as many layers as it draws in Span, so breaks stagger. Its ends lie on layer faces inside the envelope's crisp sides (or the dart misses), except that past the fuzzy top or front a spine ends where its own stone stops. A dart is kept only if it is clear of every spine already in the layer by Width horizontally across the layer and Thickness the other way across it (vertically for Through and Along, front to back for Up), each the two ends' sizes together; until 80 darts in a row miss, the layer is not full. Order draws the darts' places from a grid instead, its pitch the middle of Width by the middle of Thickness, every other layer moved half a Width so the joints alternate as in a laid wall; below 100% each place is jittered. Each end's sizes are drawn in Width and Thickness. One spine is one stone.
  4. Every stone grows until it meets its neighbours or the envelope. Between every two spines there is one split. Two that never share a layer (they meet end to end) split on the face between them (its tangent plane, where it curves), so the layers order the stones. Two that share one split on the plane across the line between their nearest points, where their power distances are equal (the distance from each point less its radius there, squared), so a bigger end takes more of what it shares. That plane separates the two spines whole, and it leaves each at least 1.5 mm of its own. Joint, drawn for each split, sets where each side stops: half of it short of the split, so over zero a gap opens (separate stones), and under zero each reaches past it into its neighbour (they merge). It changes each stone's size; nothing moves. A stone's mass is its envelope cut by its splits, nearest first: a convex cell. So with Joint at zero or more no two stones overlap, by construction, and every spine is a stone.
  5. Rocks. Each mass becomes a stone, printable by construction. First its mass gets a flat bottom just above its lowest point (at most 1 mm up, or a fifth of its height), and every underside flatter than 65° from upright is cut away by a flare rising from that bottom at 65°, so the bottom is the only face that overhangs past the limit. Then every face of the mass keeps a small patch around a touch point (its plane turned by Tilt), so the stone fills its mass; Faces planes are spread over the faces by area, each its face's plane domed away from the touch point, turned by Tilt and sunk by up to Sink; then Chamfered of the edges' length and of the corners are cut, never past a touch point. The bottom is kept as it is, and no plane, crown, facet or chamfer is made that overhangs past 65°, so a little roughness under a stone stays in the printable range (between 55° and 65°: orange). Weathering rounds every outward edge. A rock stays inside its mass, so with Joint at zero or more rocks never overlap (the sink opens the joints between them), and under zero they overlap and merge. Faces, Chamfered and Weathering are drawn for each stone, Tilt and Sink for each plane. Draw shows the rocks or the masses themselves (each in its own shade); the view options also show the layers and the spines.
  6. Bedding (on by default, under Rocks): the bottom is then the only part of a stone that needs holding, so bedding only sets the bottom on something. Stones are finished in the order of their tops (what holds a stone lies below its bottom, so it is finished first), and each is stretched down, its top held, by the widest gap under its bottom within 6 mm, to the finished stones or the ground (a lip out over nothing that near is left as it is); so its whole bottom rests on what is below it, a little into it where that is uneven. Stretching only turns its other faces toward upright, and it holds the footprint, so the stone stays printable and in its envelope; it changes only the stone's size and shape.
  7. Held faces are not painted. Orange and red mark overhang past 55° and 65°, except where a face is held up: a face on the ground, or a face buried inside another stone, or a bridge: a downward face is split into triangles, and a triangle with at least two corners resting on something (a stone or the ground, within 0.15 mm below or inside it) spans between them.

Layered, the first style: nine level layers, a handful of slabs in each. Granite: one layer through the wall, so every spine starts at a point on the back and runs straight through to the front (turned about 10°), and the stones show their ends; Joint under zero, so they merge into one irregular mass. Columns: one layer, the full height, so every spine is one upright column from a point on the ground, a hair apart. Massive: two layers parallel to the front, big stones. Wall: eight level courses, Order 100%, so each course is a row of stones a Width apart, every other course moved half a stone.

Not here yet, from the design: envelopes with concavities and holes (each mass is clipped by a box envelope), curved or irregular ends, twisting sections, and joint faces kept flush (a rock's sink opens every joint). Every job draws from its own stream (the planes, each plane's starts, each spine, each end, the fuzz, each joint, each rock), so one control never reshuffles another. Overhang paints orange past 55° and red past 65°.
