Bedding by construction

Where the bedding conversation has landed. Instead of building a stone and then stretching it down onto whatever is below, each stone is built knowing what is below it: everything under it is already finished, its bottom is chosen to sit on what is there, and a point of the bottom is allowed only where it rests or bridges between two points that rest. Nothing is repaired afterwards.

Where we landed

Five rules. The first four are agreed; the fifth has its proposal and is open.

1

Order by what holds what agreed

A stone is built only after every stone under its bottom. Not by the height of tops: a sloping neighbour can have a high top and still be under it. A circle (three stones each partly over the next) is broken at the stone with the lowest bottom.

2

Everything below is known agreed

When a stone is built, what is under it is finished. A ray down from any point of its bottom says what holds it and how far away it is.

3

The bottom is a choice agreed

The mass is the space a stone may use; the stone's bottom can be any flat face inside it, any size. A 65° flare rises from the bottom, so the stone is printable by construction. A smaller bottom means a smaller stone.

4

Supported, or bridged agreed

A point of the bottom must rest on something (within 0.15 mm, or inside a stone), or lie on the line between two resting points no more than Bridge apart. Bridge is a setting (15 mm). The same rule paints red.

5

Look only as deep as a held top can sink proposed

Under a stone that will rest on it, a top face keeps its facets, but their turn is limited so they drop no more than one Sink bite across the footprint. The stone above looks down exactly that far.

1 · The order

Stones are finished one at a time, and each is set on the stones already finished. Today the order is by tops, lowest first. That is right whenever a stone's top is roughly level, because what holds a stone lies under its bottom, so its top is lower.

It goes wrong when a neighbour's top slopes. Stone C here is under S's right end, but C's top rises past S's top further along. By tops, S comes first and is bedded without knowing C is there; C is then built into the space under S. They overlap there, or S hangs over a stone it never saw.

Raise C's top to see the order flip. Ordering by what holds what (C is under S's bottom, so C goes first) never flips.

Where it happens: rarely with level layers; more with Lean, Pitch, Sag or Bow, with Through and Along, and with Massive's big stones.

3 · The bottom, and its flare

A mass is a convex cell: the space its stone may use. Its stone needs one face that rests, the bottom; everything else must be printable, so no other underside may face down past 65° (flatter than 25° from level).

So the stone is its mass cut by a flare: two walls rising from the ends of the bottom at the steepest overhang allowed. Inside the flare nothing overhangs past the limit; what lies outside it is cut away.

Drag the bottom's ends or raise it. Keeping the mass's own bottom keeps the whole mass. A smaller or higher bottom loses the wedges beside it: the price of sitting only where there is support.

5 · How far down to look

The stone below was built by the same stone builder, so how rough its top is follows from its settings. Facets bite by Sink (1.5..3% of the stone's size: a few tenths of a millimetre). What drops a top far below its plane is Tilt: every plane is turned, and a turned plane falls away across the face.

A support found below the bottom's plane holds nothing unless the bottom comes down to it, and a flat bottom brought down to the deepest point buries the high ones. So the useful depth is small, and the proposal makes it small by construction: under the footprint of a stone above, a top's planes may turn only as far as keeps their drop across the footprint within one Sink bite. Outside footprints, the full Tilt.

Then the stone above looks down one Sink bite plus the 0.15 mm tolerance, a few tenths of a millimetre, and finds most of the top there. Switch the limit off to see what Tilt does to it.

True proportions: an 8 mm stretch of the top under a stone, magnified with both axes alike.

4 · Supported, or bridged

Seen from above: the bottom's footprint, and where it touches down (the green patches, from the rays of rule 5). A point of the bottom is allowed if it rests, or if it is on the line between two resting points no more than Bridge apart: a printer spans that gap in the air.

Between two patches, everything between them is allowed (the band round the two). With three small patches, the middle of their triangle is not on a line between two resting points, so it is not allowed: it is on a line between a patch and the empty space opposite.

Drag the patches; change Bridge. What is left (red) cannot be the bottom: the bottom shrinks to the allowed part, and the flare does the rest.

One stone, step by step

  1. Wait until every stone under the mass's bottom is finished (rule 1).
  2. The plane: the mass's own bottom face, the face it shares with the layer below.
  3. Look down from points over that face, one Sink bite of the stone below plus 0.15 mm (rule 5). Where a ray finds a stone or the ground within that, the point rests.
  4. The bottom is the resting points plus every line between two of them no more than Bridge apart (rule 4), cut to the mass.
  5. The stone is the mass inside the 65° flare from that bottom (rule 3), then faceted and chamfered as now; the bottom is kept, and under the footprints of masses above, planes turn only within one Sink bite (rule 5).
  6. It is finished: it rests by construction. No stretch, no repair, nothing below its mass.

Still open

  • Joint below zero. Overlapping masses make "under" fuzzy where they overlap; the proposal judges it from bottoms over tops and treats a point inside the stone below as resting.
  • A stone with no resting point at all (a lip with nothing within reach): the allowed bottom is empty. Options: it keeps a point bottom at its lowest corner and is painted red, or its mass is given to its neighbours.
  • Chamfers along a held top's edge are narrow and usually bridged; if Bridge is set below twice their width, the same turn limit would apply to them.
  • Bridge belongs to the printer: on this page a setting beside Bedding; in the program, in the printer profile with the 55° and 65° angles. A new word, so Orin reviews it before it is built.
