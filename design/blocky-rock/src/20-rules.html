2 · The rules

The smallest set that explains the sheet

A rock is lobes grown together, every edge rounded off by a chain of planes. Every number is proposed: taken from the study and from drawing four reference rocks by hand. Lengths are in figure heights (fh); m is the shorter plan side. A rock is about 0.3 to 1.3 fh at its longest.

B1 Base: a box, or a ramp

Class shares of the sheet: slab .22, block .30, pillar .22, ramp .26. Plan W, D and height H:

  • slab: W 0.75–1.1 fh, D 0.6–0.76 W, H 0.28–0.36 W
  • block: W 0.5–0.75 fh, D 0.75–0.95 W, H 0.68–0.95 W
  • pillar: W 0.42–0.56 fh, D 0.7–0.9 W, H 1.7–2.1 W (never above 1.3 fh)
  • ramp: W 0.5–0.7 fh, D 0.65–0.85, H 0.62–0.82; the top rises toward the back (y), from 0.22–0.32 of H at the front

Sides are near upright: a pillar leans with chance .4 by at most 0.03.

L1 A rock is lobes

The footprint is cut across its full depth into 1 to 4 lobes, each a box (or ramp) from the ground to its own top. Lobes touch and share the outline: no waist, no gap, the same face planes across a joint. Their tops are level, or within 0.02 of each other, except for a step. A block's second lobe may be split again in depth (the sheet's R1C8: three lobes round a corner).

L2 A split is the crease between two lobes

Cut positions are at 0.3–0.7 of the length. Slab: 1 (.5) or 2 (.5) cuts; block: 0 (.2), 1 (.45), 2 (.35); pillar: one in four; ramp: one in five. The crease is the V made by the two lobes' chamfers, a quarter of the radius of an outer edge (0.018 fh at least): narrow, sharp at the bottom, running down the faces and across the top, ending at the ground in a small notch like the sheet's feet.

L3 A step is a lower lobe

Chance .12 on a slab (its end lobe at 0.6–0.75 of the height), .22 on a block (first lobe at 0.45–0.65), .5 on a pillar (one end lobe 0.38–0.52 of the width, at 0.35–0.5 of the height), .6 on a ramp: a seat, 0.35–0.5 of the width by 0.3–0.4 of the depth at the front-left, at about half the ramp's height there. A lower lobe is chamfered like any other, tread and riser included.

C1 Chamfers: a rounded-off edge as a chain of planes

Each convex edge gets 2 (.4) or 3 (.6) planes, each tangent to a circle of radius r tucked into the edge (normals at 0.33 and 0.67, or 0.2, 0.5 and 0.8 of the way round), so the outermost planes meet the faces at small angles and the edge reads round at a distance. r is 0.14–0.22 m on a top edge, 0.12–0.2 m on an upright, 0.1–0.16 m at the bottom, never under 0.05 fh and never over 0.42 of the face it sits on; each plane's radius varies ±15 per cent and each is tilted so its width changes by up to 0.4 r from the middle of the edge to its ends. Slabs take a smaller r. A bend under 35 degrees (a ramp's top against its front) is left a crease. Concave corners are never cut.

C2 Wobble

One to three planes a rock (one on a slab), 1.2–3.2 degrees off a side face or 0.5–1.3 off a top, 0.012–0.035 fh deep at the crease, never on a joint face: gentle breaks in a large face, never a facet.

P1 Undersides print

No face is flatter than 35 degrees above horizontal, except the flat bottom. A bottom chamfer plane whose normal points more than sin 55° (0.819) down is dropped; the finished mesh is checked and a rock with a face over 55 degrees is drawn again with other chamfer widths (the table in chapter 4 gives how often).

P2 Where a face must stay plain

For the cliff: bottomBevel: false leaves the bottom edges sharp (an edge on the ground, or on another stone). A top that carries a stone should take no wobble or chamfer, and a face against a neighbour none; the generator has no switch for these yet.

P3 One closed mesh

The union is welded and its T-junctions repaired; the page checks that every edge has two triangles.
