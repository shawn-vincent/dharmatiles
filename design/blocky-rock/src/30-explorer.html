4 · How a rock is built

Lobes, rounded off, grown together

Three stages of one rock. Then the cost, measured in this browser.

  1. Lobes. A rock is one to four lobes: boxes, or ramps, that stand on the ground and touch along full-depth joints. They share one outline and one top level, except that a lobe may stand lower (a step) or a ramp's lobes share one sloping plane. Each is a convex solid held as planes.
  2. Chamfers. Every convex edge of every lobe is rounded off by two or three planes, each tangent to a circle whose radius is set from the edge's own line in space, tilted a little so the width changes along the edge. Joint edges get a quarter of the radius, so the crease between two lobes is a V, wide at the surface and sharp at the bottom. Inside corners are never rounded.
  3. Wobble. A few planes at 0.5 to 3 degrees, never on a joint face, break the large faces and tops.
  4. Join. Each polygon of each lobe is split at the other lobes' planes; pieces inside another lobe are dropped, and so are two faces that touch back to back. What is left is the surface of the union.
  5. Mesh. Weld the corners, repair T-junctions, fan the polygons into triangles, count the edges (each must have two) and find the steepest face facing down; a rock that fails is drawn again with other chamfer widths. Normals are smoothed across bends under 40 degrees for display only.

Nothing is a voxel or a field: every face is an exact plane.
