Blocky Rock Study

Rocks come in levels of detail. The cliff generator places blocks and gives each a box with chamfers; this is the next level, which turns that box into a rough rock with a few big structural features, in the style of a reference sheet of forty single rocks. Fine surface and weathering come after, and are not here. The page has the study of the sheet, the rules it gives, and an explorer that draws one rock from a seed.

1 · The study

Forty rocks, taken apart

Cells are named R1C1 to R5C8 (row, then column). Each rock is read as a base shape plus its big features, each with a rough place and size in the rock's own proportions. The reading is in data/rocks.json, written by study.py, which also measures each silhouette.

What the sheet shows

Every rock is one mass: a column stood on a flat bottom, seen from the same corner at about 20 degrees up, lit softly from the upper left. The left face is the one that is broad; the right face is seen at a slant. All of them are made of flat planes. The edges are not sharp: every convex edge, top, upright and bottom, is cut by one to three narrow planes whose width changes along the edge. The concave corners (where a step meets its riser, where a groove goes in) are sharp. Nothing is rounded in.

The surface is a smooth clay sculpt, so planes are slightly wavy and the creases are soft. I take that as shading and not as geometry; the rules below are for planes.

Base shapes measured estimated

The class of each rock comes from its silhouette's height over width in the 3/4 view (a cube reads about 1.0 there) and from whether the top slopes. Slab, block and pillar are one family that differs in proportion only: the aspects run 0.54 to 2.05 with no gap, so they are one box with a height drawn from a wide range, named for the generator's control. A ramp is the only different base: a sloped top rising to a high back, seen on 10 rocks.

Correction to the first read: the stepped L-profile is not a base shape. A step is found on every class (slab, block, pillar and ramp), so it is a feature, and a ramp with a seat is a ramp with a step.

Size ranges estimated

The size of each feature in the rock's own units, so it scales with the rock. A chip is small: 0.15 to 0.3 of the face it is on. A step takes 0.2 to 0.55 of the face's width and is cut 0.35 to 0.5 down from the top. A facet covers half the face or more. A split that does not run the full height comes 0.3 to 0.8 down from the top, median 0.6. Widths of the grooves and chamfers are in the chamfer table below.

Features, and how often estimated

Six kinds explain the sheet. A rock has two features on average (2.25; one to four, never none): split (a V-groove down a face), step (a corner or end cut down to a shelf), chip (a flake off a corner or a V notch), facet (a plane a few degrees off a face), bed (a line across the top, or one horizontal line on a face) and offset (one side of a split a hair lower).

Splits are the most common, and every slab has one. They read as a V-line in one mass, level on both sides, never a gap. Bed lines are weaker than the first read: 5 of the 6 are a line across the top, not a horizontal groove on a side; only R5C3 has a horizontal one.

Where features sit estimated

Faces: the left face carries most features because it is the one seen square; the right face is foreshortened, so its counts are low and I do not read a preference from them. Height: chips are low (12 of 17 in the lower third: feet and lower corners), steps are cut at 0.35 to 0.5 of the height (a shelf at the lower left, or a seat in the top), facets sit mid-face, beds are at the top. Splits reach from the top: 11 of 32 run the full height and the rest come down 0.3 to 0.8 of it (median 0.6); none starts from the ground and stops short, which is what lets every groove print.

Not seen on any rock: a chip in a top face, or a groove that is closed at the top and open below. The first is the cliff rule (a top that carries something stays flat); the second is the print rule.

Which go together estimated

The cell is the number of rocks with both. The diagonal is the number of rocks with that feature. A split goes with the rest because it is the commonest. What is rare is the stack of everything: step with chip is on only 2 rocks and bed with anything on 1 to 4. A step and a split are together on 7 of the 16 stepped rocks, so the generator has them independent: a scored line, and a bite from a corner.

How irregular a chamfer is estimated

On the main top edge of each rock I counted the planes (1, 2 or 3) and guessed the ratio of the widest to the narrowest width along the edge. Almost all have two or three planes; the width runs between about 1.3 and 2.3 times. Widths against the rock: a top chamfer is about 8 to 12 per cent of the shorter plan side, wider on the top than it is deep; an upright one about 8 to 12 (my first read was 5 to 8; the renders looked too sharp, so I took it up); a groove's two halves are about 4 to 6 each. The bottom edges are beveled too (not sharp) at 60 to 100 per cent of the top width, which is what I see; where a rock sits in a cliff the bottom is left sharp, as the cliff rules say.

What one 3/4 view cannot tell

  • Hidden faces. The back and right-back are never seen. A split drawn on the left face may run through the rock or stop; I count what shows. Every position in the table is on the visible faces only.
  • Proportions are projected. The silhouette aspect includes the top face and the depth, so it is not H over W. Slab below 0.75, pillar from 1.25 are cuts in a continuous range, not natural classes.
  • Sizes are by eye, to about a tenth of the rock. The 190-pixel cells give a groove a few pixels wide; I can say narrow, not 4 per cent against 5.
  • Depth of grooves and steps is foreshortened; I take a groove to be as deep as it is wide.
  • Scale. There is no figure in the pictures. Rocks are generated at 0.3 to 1.3 fh, the cliff work's range, with the longest side the size.
  • Bottoms. The ground hides the bottom edge in the lower third of every cell; the bottom bevel is read from its visible edge only.
2 · The rules

One idea: chamfer every edge

Round 10 halves every chamfer width; round 7 kept the one idea of round 6, with single-arc chamfers. It strips the rock to one idea. Every number is proposed. Lengths are in figure heights (fh); m is the shorter plan side.

B1 Base: a box, or a ramp

Class shares of the sheet: slab .22, block .30, pillar .22, ramp .26. Plan W, D and height H: slab W 0.75–1.1, D 0.6–0.76 W, H 0.28–0.36 W; block W 0.5–0.75, D 0.75–0.95 W, H 0.68–0.95 W; pillar W 0.42–0.56, D 0.7–0.9 W, H 1.7–2.1 W (not above 1.3 fh); ramp W 0.5–0.7, D 0.65–0.85, H 0.62–0.82, rising toward the back.

B2 Nothing is square

Each outer side leans by its own −0.03 to 0.07 and is twisted in plan by up to 0.07; the top slopes up to 0.045 each way. The bottom is flat.

C1 Every edge is one arc

24 segments an edge. The width is wider or narrower in the middle, by up to 10 per cent either way; the tilt is constant or changes once, by up to ±4 degrees on a top edge, ±3 on a bottom edge, ±8 on a side edge; the strip bows by up to 0.15 of its width, in or out. Each edge has its own random numbers.

C2 Angles and widths

  • top edge: width 0.065–0.12 of the shorter side, 8–27 degrees below horizontal, a short drop down the side;
  • bottom edge: width 0.07–0.11 of the shorter side, 40–43 degrees above horizontal (twist ±1), held clear of the 55 degree limit, so it tucks under: the rise up the face is its width times tan 37–41;
  • side edge: width 0.06–0.11 of the shorter side, 32–58 degrees, so no dark gash

On one face the chamfers together never take more than a third of its width (each leg at most 0.16 of the face and 0.24 of the rock's thinnest side, so a slab never pinches). Each chamfer is shown as its own band: normals are not smoothed across bends over 6 degrees.

F1 Faces curve a little

Each face (not the bottom) carries a dome or saddle with an amplitude of 0.02–0.08 per cent of its size and a small tilt term, each from slightly concave to slightly convex. The chamfer lines sit on the curved faces.

M1 Corners and closure

The corner facet is one triangle whose vertices are, on each of the three faces, the point where its two chamfer lines meet. Every strip, face grid and facet shares those vertices, so the mesh is closed.

P1 Printing: bottom chamfers

A bottom chamfer is held at 35 degrees or more above horizontal (at most 55 from vertical), so it prints; the steepest underside is reported from the real mesh. The rest is not enforced. bottomBevel: false still leaves the foot sharp, for the cliff.

X1 Off, kept in the code

Lobes and steps, cracks (a wandering faceted cut with the far piece moved aside), battered corners and edges, and kink planes: makeRock(seed, { full: true }).

4 · How a rock is built

One block, every edge chamfered

Two stages of one rock. Then the cost, measured in this browser.

  1. The block. The base shape (slab, block, pillar or ramp) as six planes, each side tilted a little off square by its own amount, so no two sides are parallel, and the top with its own slight slope. The bottom is flat.
  2. A strip along every edge. Each edge becomes a ruled strip of 24 short segments between a line on one face and a line on the other, and everything about it is one simple arc: the width is a little wider or narrower in the middle (up to 10 per cent), the tilt is constant or changes once along the edge, and the strip bows a little along its length (up to 0.15 of its width), in or out. Each edge has its own arcs. Top edges lie 8 to 27 degrees below horizontal with a short drop down the side, bottom edges tuck under the rock, rising 40 to 43 degrees above horizontal (47 to 50 from vertical, inside the 55 degree print limit), side edges take 32 to 58 degrees, leaning into either face. Widths are 6 to 12 per cent of the rock's shorter side (half of round 9's), and the chamfers on one face together never take more than a third of its width.
  3. Faces. Each face is nearly flat: tilted by up to about 1.5 degrees, and curved by one faint arc each way (0.02 to 0.08 per cent of its size), from slightly concave to slightly convex. Each face is an even 24 by 24 grid (a Coons patch between its four sides, whose corners are shared), so there is no seam or fan.
  4. Corners. On each face, the point where its two chamfer lines meet is found directly; the three such points, one per face, are the vertices of the corner facet, one triangle. Each strip runs between its two end points, so it never folds.
  5. Mesh. Faces, strips and corner patches share their vertices, so the mesh is closed by construction (every edge in two triangles). Normals are smoothed across bends under 6 degrees, so each chamfer shows as a band for display only. About 7,500 triangles, 3 ms. Each bottom corner is one clean facet, the same as a top corner, so the rock sits on an undercut foot.

Off for this round, kept in the code (makeRock(seed, { full: true })): lobes and steps, cracks, battered corners, kink planes, and the print rule (reported as "steepest underside", not enforced).

5 · Against the references

What looks right, and what does not

Round 10: half-width chamfers. Compared beside R1C8, R2C4 and R1C5. Judged by rendering the same view beside the cell.

Looks right

  • Chamfers are half the width of round 9's, so the rock reads as a crisp block with bands that show but do not round it; faces are near flat and the large faces no longer cushion.
  • Printability: 0 of 600 seeds per base shape pass 55 degrees from vertical (steepest 51 to 54), fixed at the source by holding the bottom chamfer at 40 to 43 degrees above horizontal.
  • A closed mesh by construction, 3.4 ms a rock.

Does not look right yet

  • Too tame. Against R1C8, R2C4 and R1C5 it is a clean chamfered block; the steps, lobe creases and chips that make the references read as rocks are off.
  • Each bottom corner is still a facet whose lowest point is the corner of the foot; it sits on the ground line, not below it (the lowest vertex of the mesh is z = 0), but from the side it can still read as a faint point.
  • Halving the chamfers makes the tops and bottoms read as slightly plain: the bands are thin against the large faces.

What I did not look at

  • The back of any reference rock; there are none.
  • A real phone: headless Chrome at 1280 and 390 px only.
  • Statistics of generated rocks against the study tables.
  • The rock in a cliff, with neighbours.
