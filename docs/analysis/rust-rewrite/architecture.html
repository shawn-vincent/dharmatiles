Target architecture

What the Rust version should look like. A tile is written as a short declarative document. The document names built-in looks (hills, rocks, meadow, water, cliff, walls, trees) and tunes them with natural controls. Underneath, a Rust engine kit of about twenty shared operations implements those looks once each, with no duplicates.

What changed in revision 2. The first draft proposed that documents be written directly in seven verbs (scatter, trace, build, relieve, seat, fill, pack). Walking each lab's approved result through those verbs showed that every lab needed 7 to 11 extra constructs to reproduce, and two verbs merged mechanisms that really differ. The verbs now live one layer down, inside the engine. Documents compose looks. The change log lists every edit with its evidence.

Two layers

Why two layers and not one: the approved looks are solvers with dozens of tightly tuned internal constants. The meadow, for example, has fifteen rim-steering constants, per-obstacle wrap bands and a stroke-blade profile. Exposing those as verbs in documents would make every tile file a program. Keeping them inside a built-in, behind controls such as bare="20%", keeps documents declarative, which is what Shawn asked for ("a static definition file… declarative").

The document language: six ideas

Everything a tile author writes is one of these. The column on the right says where each idea comes from.

IdeaWhat it meansStatus
DefinitionOne form for everything: parameters with defaults, plus a stack. A tile is a definition called at the root with a square size, a connector and a printer. cols and rows are ordinary parameters, so a definition can declare its squares or receive them from its caller. Definitions nest without a depth limit.owner conversation L2582, L2807, L3003
Lines and regionsNamed Bézier lines with optional organic wobble. A region is the lines it names, the tile perimeter, and one or more fill points. Lines a region does not name have no effect. Two derived lines are added: offset(line, d) and contour(field, level). A band is two offset lines plus a fill point on the original line. No union, intersection or difference.owner L1843, L1987 derived lines
StackAn ordered list. Each entry applies a definition to a region (self, defaulting to the caller's). Order is bottom to top. Evaluation runs in two passes, plan then build (next section).owner L2025 two passes
ParametersNumbers, enums, booleans, colours, names. Any number may be a distribution ("6..8", "0.2..0.4^1.4"). Horizontal lengths are in squares and heights in fractions of the tall wall (h). The parameter's declared axis supplies the unit, so documents rarely write one. Heights can name a tile-level level (water, dirt, floor). Variability is given as up and down in h. A parameter can also take a field, a value that varies across the tile (noise, distance to a tag).owner L505, L628, L685, L883, L930, L3191 fields
TagsEvery solid a built-in makes carries a material (user-named, such as heroic_rock) and a kind (rock, reed, wall…). Other entries refer to what is already there by tag: grass avoids obstacle, scree gathers near=cliff, water reads which kinds emit waves. Tags replace today's shared obstacle masks.proposed
Seed by pathEach call's seed is a hash of its parent's seed and its own name in the tree. Inserting an entry never rerolls its siblings. Two siblings with the same name get #1, #2 by order of appearance, or an explicit id. Any entry may pin seed=.owner L3212

Contracts that apply everywhere without being written: the printer profile (0.45 mm lines, 0.2 mm layers, 55° overhang) that built-ins read to guarantee printability; the edge contract that makes neighbouring tiles butt; the connector; and material → colour for painted output.

Plan, then build

The labs exposed an ordering puzzle that a single bottom-to-top pass can't solve. Rocks in a meadow must claim their footprints before the grass grows, so blades steer around them. They must also be seated after the grass, so each rock rises above the grass top (meadowlab.py:224 then :236; shorelab.py:286 then :296). The rule that solves this is: first everything decides where it goes, then everything is built, bottom to top.

  • Plan pass, in stack order: each entry publishes its plan, which is positions, footprints, tags, height fields and curves. A plan sees the plans below it.
  • Build pass, in stack order: each entry makes its solids. A build sees the solids below it and every plan.

Step through the shore tile below. Blue dashed cells are what the current step reads.

The plan pass is cheap: 2D work and fields, typically well under a second. That makes it the natural live preview while editing (see feasibility). It also gives Shawn's lazy-model question a concrete answer. Plans are computed for everything up front, solids are built eagerly once, and both are cached by path, seed and inputs.

Regions: the paint bucket

This demo uses the conversation's checkerboard example: four lines and five fill points. Click the tile to add or remove fill points, and toggle which lines the region names. The last button shows that a band needs no new concept: it is two offset lines and one fill point.


    
The page rasterises each named line onto a 180 × 180 grid and flood-fills from each fill point with 4-connectivity. The library already does exactly this (core/region.py, using scipy.ndimage.label). Soft areas such as "bare earth covering 20%" or "where the ground is below the water level" are not regions. They are fields passed as parameters to the built-in that needs them.

  





Definitions, recursion and seeds


Every call has a path, such as tile/courtyard/floor/slab#3, and a seed derived from its parent's seed and its own name. Select a node to see what it receives. Then press Insert an entry at the bottom of the stack to compare two seeding schemes. With seeds derived from stack position, as ChatGPT's draft proposed (conversation L3258), every later sibling's seed changes and its whole subtree rerolls. With path-derived seeds, nothing that already existed changes.



  

  

    

      
      
      

    
    

  





Example documents


These use KDL, a small node-based document format with a Rust parser that preserves comments and layout when a program edits the file. That matters for the GUI and for agents (why KDL). Numbers across are in squares and numbers up in h, with h = 40 mm here. Each document was checked against the lab it reproduces; the comments give the lab's value where it helps.




A shore tile (shorelab)


tile shore cols=2 rows=2 {
    heights water=0.065 bed=0.01     // 2.6 mm, 0.4 mm
    line shore {
        through 0.94 0  0.9 1  1.0 2     // squares
        organic amount=0.3 scale=0.4
    }
    region sea { lines shore; fill 1.8 1.0 }

    stack {
        hills down=0                      // rises only
        ramp self=sea to=bed width=0.23   // 8 mm
        rocks count="6..8"
        meadow bare="20%" steer=waterline
        reeds clumps="3..5"
        water level=water
    }
}


Water covers the whole tile, column by column. Land above the level shows through, so the shoreline is wherever the ground crosses the water level, exactly as Shawn described (L2293). rocks plans footprints before the meadow plans its blades, then seats each rock on the grass top, and wet rocks clear the water by 0.6 mm. The water reads every tagged rock and reed as a wave source. No region bounds the water and nothing says "connected".



A meadow, and what a built-in exposes


// built-in; these are its controls and defaults
meadow bare="20%" {             // incl. ground under rocks
    flow swing=60 meander="10..30" downhill="30%"  // degrees
    clearing length="0.29..0.51" aspect="0.45..0.65"
    blade length="0.10..0.19" height="0.04..0.05"
    spill "0.02..0.09^2"            // tips over bare earth
    sprigs "10%"
    against tuck=0.014 foot="50%"   // pressed on obstacles
}


Blade width is always 4 printed lines and is not a control. Strand spacing (2.65 mm), the frond shape, rake, the 15 rim-steering constants, bank behaviour and smoothing are fixed internals of the built-in, as they are in grasslab v3. In squares, blade length reads 0.10..0.19 (3.5 to 6.5 mm at 35 mm squares). Whether print-scale details like this should be in squares or in mm is an open question.



A cliff run (clifflab rockpile)


tile cliff-run cols=2 rows=1 {
    line foot { through 0 0.55  2 0.45 }
    region shelf { lines foot; fill 1.0 0.2 }
    stack {
        dirt
        cliff self=shelf form=run height=0.9 \
              shatter=1.5 dirt-on-tops=#true
        scree near=cliff
    }
}


form picks a preset (run, corner, canyon, escarpment, plateau, massif, mound, spire, needles) that fixes height cap, mass and the mix of stone kinds (column 38%, block 38%, slab 12%, odd 6%, monolith 6%). Everything else is internal to cliff: the gravity partition, columns, dressing, the seat cones at the overhang limit, the face crack network, relief carving, grain, the hidden core, the 4 mm end section and fit-to-tile. scree is separate because it is reusable: it gathers near anything tagged cliff.



A user definition, used twice


define rocky-meadow {
    param rocks "2..4"         // per square
    stack {
        dirt
        rocks density=rocks family="dome 35%, slab 20%, block 20%, wedge 25%"
        meadow bare="25%"
    }
}

tile courtyard cols=4 rows=4 {
    line wall-line { through 1 1  3 1  3 3  1 3  1 1 }
    region yard   { lines wall-line; fill 2 2 }
    region around { lines wall-line; fill 0.5 0.5 }
    stack {
        hills
        rocky-meadow self=around
        floor self=yard texture=cut-stone      // = wall laid flat
        wall along=wall-line family=fieldstone ruin=0.3
    }
}


A definition is the unit of reuse, which replaces the helpers and shared constants that today's .tile.py files write in Python. There are no loops or assignments. Iteration lives inside built-ins.

recommend
A list of tagged solids
The scene is an ordered list of solids, each with a material, a kind and an owner path. The mesh is the common format. Queries such as top(x,y) are cached per snapshot, and a lifted heightfield answers them directly without rasterising its mesh. Combining solids into one mesh happens once, at export. Inside a built-in, booleans are used freely; the cliff does hundreds.

  
recommend
Plan lazily, build eagerly
Plans and fields are cheap and computed on demand. Solids are built once, in stack order, and cached by (path, seed, hash of inputs). Most real operations are global (Poisson saturation, gravity stacking, geodesic distance), so building on demand point by point would recompute the world.

  
recommend
Four kernels
Grid: f32 fields, EDT, morphology, blur. Plan: 2D polygons, offsets, Voronoi. Mesh: Manifold for booleans, hull, extrude, slice, refine, simplify, level sets. Rays: a BVH. Crate choices are on the feasibility page.





Language decisions


Each decision is marked: owner settled by Shawn in the conversation, proposed by this analysis, ask needs Shawn's call.


    
  
  1. owner Units are fractions. Across: squares. Up: h, where 1 is the tall wall. Named levels live in a tile-level heights table. Printer rules are not units; the printer profile is a contract that built-ins meet internally, by working in true millimetres and rounding to whole lines and layers. Angles in documents mean real angles, so they don't shear when the two scales differ. ask whether print-scale details (blade length, rock size, grain) should scale with the square size.
    2. 
  
  2. owner Regions are paint buckets only. Derived lines (offset, contour) feed the same bucket. The first draft's self - bare, where(field > t) and coverage-grown regions are removed; soft areas are field parameters.
    3. 
  
  3. owner Water covers the whole tile. Per column, the water surface is level + tide + swell wherever the ground is below the level, with a 0.6 mm margin. Elsewhere it is buried under the ground. No connectivity rule. A pit dug later below the level floods, and stack order decides that.
    4. 
  
  4. proposed Plan, then build. Plans see plans below; builds see solids below and all plans.
    5. 
  
  5. proposed Tags carry kinds, consumers carry tables. A rock is tagged rock. The water built-in owns the table that says what wavelength band a rock emits. The first draft put emitter(band: 2mm..4.5mm) on the rock, which coupled rocks to water.
    6. 
  
  6. proposed Below zero belongs to the connector. Carves may go below 0 (pits, stairs down). They are recorded as voids and applied to the connector, which is built last because DungeonBlocks' peg height depends on the content's height. Where there is no connector material, the carve becomes a hole straight through. ask where z = 0 sits for OpenLOCK, which assumes a thick tile.
    7. 
  
  7. proposed Read anywhere, add anywhere, remove only inside self. Trees may overhang their region, and a definition can't damage its neighbours. This loosens the "restricted view" Shawn described (L3171). ask to confirm.
    8. 
  
  8. proposed One edge contract at the root. Water: tide wavelength half a square with crests at corners and square midpoints, 0.6 mm amplitude, calm within 4 mm of the edge. Ground: one edge level (the labs disagree today: 3.4 mm in grasslab and shorelab, 4.0 mm in dirtlab). Lines: anchors at fixed edge positions. Cliffs: the standard end section.
    9. 
  
  9. proposed OpenLOCK is a second evaluation, not a parameter. A 25.4 mm build changes footprints and densities. With resolution-independent noise and path seeds, both builds share one layout.
    10. 
  
  10. proposed The audit is a gate. Built-ins guarantee by construction, but round 11 cliffs still have 1–13 mm² of overhang and some see-through gaps, so the audit must fail or warn loudly, and every render paints failing faces red.
    11. 
  
  11. proposed Documents are KDL files in directories. A directory is a namespace, definitions are imported by path, and the CLI selects tiles with globs (L180). Types come from the Rust model, which also produces the JSON Schema the GUI and the MCP server use.
    12.

Questions only Shawn can answer


1. Do print-scale details scale with the square?
You ruled "everything with virtual fractions" (L685). Taken literally, an OpenLOCK build at 25.4 mm makes rocks, grass blades and grain 27% smaller than the DungeonBlocks build. Blades are always 4 printed lines wide, so they would get thinner relative to their length. Today the library keeps those details in mm, so they don't scale. Options: (a) fractions everywhere and accept the shrink; (b) fractions everywhere, with detail-size controls given in printed lines and layers; (c) fractions for layout, mm for detail.


2. How identical must two builds be?
Same machine, same version, byte-identical is cheap. Byte-identical across Mac and Linux costs speed (single-threaded Manifold, a portable maths library, no SIMD FFT). Recommendation: byte-identical on one machine, visually identical across machines.


3. What is the acceptance test for the port?
Python and Rust can't match byte for byte. Proposal: your side-by-side review of painted renders over four seeds, plus numbers that must hold (watertight, overhang area, see-through holes, bare-earth fraction, blade height range).


4. Where do new looks get invented after the port?
The labs are fast to iterate in Python. Options: keep Python labs as the design bench and port each look once you approve it; or move lab work to Rust once the kit and hot reload exist.


5. Is "plan, then build" the answer to your lazy-model question?
It is lazy where that is cheap (plans, fields, preview) and eager where most work is global (solids).


6. Removing only inside self, and the OpenLOCK datum
Is it right that a definition may add outside its region but not remove? Where should z = 0 sit for OpenLOCK, given that its tiles have thickness?


7. Labs first, and clifflab last?
v1 would be built from the labs plus the library's regions, walls, trees and bases, without the library's soil, grass carpet, thatch or water. The cliff is still changing (round 12 and later), so it would be ported last.


8. KDL instead of the YAML-like sketches?
The conversation sketched YAML-like syntax. Rust YAML libraries are deprecated or drop comments when a program edits a file. KDL keeps comments through GUI edits and was on ChatGPT's list (L303).
